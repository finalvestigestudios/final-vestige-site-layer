## What the commissioners actually recorded (English translation pp. 65–69)
1. Charles Deslon selected a boy about twelve whom he already considered highly susceptible; this was **not a random-sample efficacy trial**.
2. A **single apricot tree was deliberately treated** in Franklin's garden at Passy, while the boy remained indoors. The commissioners reported trying to distance Deslon and prevent him communicating signals, but he retained a presence and pointed his cane toward the treated tree. This was **not a modern double-blind trial**.
3. Blindfolded, the boy was brought successively to **four other trees, all untreated**, instructed to embrace each for two minutes. The report records responses at each.
4. Recorded straight-line distances of each untreated test tree **from the treated tree**, not total path length: Tree 1 ~27 feet (sweating, cough, mild head pain); Tree 2 ~36 feet (stupefaction/head pain); Tree 3 ~38 feet (more intense symptoms, belief he was nearing treated tree); Tree 4 ~24 feet (collapse, faint, stiffened limbs, revived by Deslon). **Distance does not establish the relative tree layout or a route across the orchard**.
5. The boy **never reached the treated apricot tree** in the recorded sequence. The commissioners judged this result contrary to the proposed physical fluid. Deslon countered that trees naturally shared magnetism, augmented by his presence. Their conclusions are the commission's contemporary interpretation, not physiological measurements or proof that the child was faking.
6. The printed report does not establish the precise calendar day of the orchard trial; do not invent one, weather, dialogue, private thoughts, or eyewitness testimony outside the report.

## Website-only companion: exact proposed section
### Four trees. None of them treated.
The boy never reached the apricot tree that Charles Deslon had treated. That detail is easy to lose when the 1784 experiment is retold as a child collapsing beside a magnetic tree. According to the commissioners’ report, the subject, whom Deslon himself had selected as susceptible, embraced four **different** trees while blindfolded. The treated apricot tree remained apart.

At the first untreated tree, the report says the boy described sweating, coughing and head pain. At the second, he reported stupor and a headache. At the third, the symptoms appeared stronger and he thought he was nearing the treated tree. At the fourth, he fainted. The report’s four distances—27, 36, 38 and 24 feet—refer to each tested tree’s distance **from the treated apricot**, not distances walked between the four. There is no surviving orchard plan in this source that would justify drawing an accurate route.

Deslon had insisted on staying in the garden and directing his cane and face toward the treated tree. The commissioners placed him as far away as they could and positioned members between him and the boy to obstruct signaling. Their precautions are significant, but describing this as a modern, fully double-blind clinical trial would go beyond the record. The experiment was designed to separate awareness of a purported force from the force itself. The commissioners read the crisis at an untreated tree as evidence for imagination rather than the fluid they were asked to evaluate; Deslon offered an alternative explanation that the report rejected.

What followed was a sharp argument about evidence. The boy’s reported symptoms were not erased because the theory failed. They were part of the observation to be explained. Nor does the printed report give us independent clinical follow-up, an exact test date, or grounds to diagnose the child from two centuries away.
