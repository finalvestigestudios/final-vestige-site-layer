The Two Timelines

Two records disagree about the hour. Perhaps one clock was wrong. Perhaps one person was remembering. Perhaps the records are describing two different moments and neither is mistaken.

A chronology becomes reliable when we stop flattening those possibilities into a single line. This free, local-only research desk compares the time windows you have actually verified. It will not guess a historical time zone, invent a missing date, or decide which witness is truthful.

01 / Separate the events

What did the clock actually date?

A record may date the event, the writing of an entry, the arrival of a message, the printing of a newspaper, or the release of an archive scan. These are different clocks attached to different actions. Before arranging them in order, name the action each date describes.

The National Archives distinguishes several date fields in its catalog, including inclusive dates, production, coverage, broadcast and release dates. That is an archival principle worth carrying into ordinary research: the date printed by a database is not automatically the day the event occurred.

Three descriptions of an hour

Event: the sound was heard at 08:12, according to a direct log.

Transmission: the report reached another office at 08:18.

Publication: a newspaper reported the occurrence the following day.

Invented example. Three compatible records may carry three different dates. None is a duplicate event timestamp merely because the subject matches.

02 / Keep the original words

Do not repair a clock without showing the repair.

Copy the timestamp as the record gives it, including abbreviations, punctuation and oddities. “8:12,” “2014-03-09 02:12,” “8:12 local,” and “08:12Z” are not interchangeable claims. Record place and observed convention before conversion. An archival annotation may be later than the source; a recollection can be rounded; a chronometer may have a documented correction.

Historical daylight-saving rules have changed. NIST identifies a substantial U.S. rule change effective in 2007, and the authority for civil time rules lies with the Department of Transportation. An offset that is correct now must not be silently assigned to an event from a different era. The Moving Noon addresses local solar time, railway/company time, daylight time and other historical systems in more depth.

Rule: If the date, place, clock standard or historical offset cannot be established from the record and credible independent documentation, leave the normalized UTC window blank. The comparison will say “unresolved,” which is a result worth preserving.

03 / Use windows, not false decimals

A minute can be precise without being accurate.

A witness saying “around eight” gives you a looser interval than an instrument marked to the second. Yet even that instrument may have an unknown offset, drift or reporting delay. Precision of display is not independent evidence of accuracy.

For each source, state its own uncertainty first. Only then choose an earliest and latest plausible UTC minute if the conversion has been independently established. This tool accepts minute-wide boundaries, not seconds or milliseconds; the Apollo 1 figures below are deliberately not run through it. A wide window can be defensible. A narrow one invented to make a timeline attractive cannot.

The comparison below treats source claims as intervals only after each has an original-time transcription, a named source, a defined kind of timestamp and a dated explanation of its clock basis. Overlap means a shared time is possible, not that two sources independently confirm it. Separation means the supplied windows cannot both date the same instant under the stated assumptions; it does not identify an unreliable witness. If one source dates an event and another dates a later transmission, the desk marks them different actions instead of manufacturing a contradiction.

04 / The working desk

Put two source witnesses side by side.

Enter only details you can trace back to the original item. A comparable interval requires a named record, its time as written, the event or recording action it dates, UTC basis, a provenance note, and both UTC limits. An empty note, unknown action or an unverified historical offset produces an unresolved result; choosing “verified” does not make it so. The form remains in this browser tab. It does not send your records to Final Vestige or anyone else; if you close the tab without exporting, the notes are lost. For privacy, avoid entering names or identifiers you would not want exposed if the device is shared.

Enter two sources and select “Compare intervals.” Unverified clock standards remain unresolved even if someone has typed apparent UTC dates into the fields.

05 / A controlled example

The clock disagreement that was not a verdict.

Imagine, strictly for demonstration, that three surviving records purport to date the moment a warehouse alarm began. The first log constrains it to 08:11–08:13 UTC. A second operator’s record allows 08:12–08:15 UTC. Their intervals overlap. The third claims 08:19–08:21 UTC. If all three genuinely concern the same instant and their clock conversions and uncertainty limits have been established, the third interval is incompatible with the other two.

But perhaps that third record dates the receipt of the alarm, not the sounding. Its separation then belongs to the history of a message passing through a system, not to a contest over whose memory failed. Ask who created each record, which system generated the time, what action caused the entry, and whether one account merely copied another.

In the real record, compare documents before narrating. The Apollo 1 Evidence Atlas connects individual sources to a chronology; the underlying Apollo 204 Review Board report is a model of why a sequence must be tied to known observations, procedures and their documentary provenance. The warehouse time windows above are wholly invented; the separately sourced Apollo 1 case below examines actual changes in reported times.

06 / A documented revision, not a fictional disagreement

Twenty seconds, two official accounts.

January 27, 1967. Inside Apollo Command Module 012, a fire overtook the crew during a ground test. Later descriptions sometimes give slightly different seconds for the same accident. These are not rival legends. They are records made at different stages of a technical investigation—and, in one important pair, they do not even date the same observation.

The source-witness comparison

QuestionFeb. 3 preliminary memorandumApollo 204 Review Board chronology
Earliest named fire observationFirst detected: 6:31:03 p.m. ESTFirst verbal report: 23:31:04.7 GMT, explicitly 6:31:04.7 p.m. EST
Cabin ruptureAbout 6:31:17 p.m. EST23:31:19.4 GMT (6:31:19.4 p.m. EST)
Evidence cautionPreliminary figures; recordings from different stations may be out of synchronization by a second or twoLater reconstruction using recorded data and witness evidence; telemetry ended by 23:31:22.4 GMT

The necessary distinction: “first detected” is not necessarily “first spoken report.” Treating 6:31:03 and 6:31:04.7 as competing stopwatch readings for the identical action would manufacture a conflict. The rupture estimates are more directly comparable; the later number is about 2.4 seconds later than the earlier one. The memorandum expressly warns about possible cross-station synchronization errors of one or two seconds. That warning matters, but it does not independently establish why every part of the 2.4-second revision occurred.

The Review Board chronology gives a reported verbal indication, not a proven second of ignition. The original ignition instant cannot be recovered by subtracting a guessed delay from the transcript. Likewise, a later official investigation should not erase the earlier estimate from the historical record. Label both, date both, and show which evidence each source was using.

Try the method: Write down the original phrase identifying the event, then its reported time and source status. Keep the two rupture reports as two time claims; do not pretend their difference is a verified uncertainty interval. The comparison widget above operates at whole-minute granularity, so it is not suitable for adjudicating the seconds in this case.
