Three Pages. One Day. Three Different Witnesses.

A field method for reading a mid-century U.S. Navy deck log without flattening its title sheet, narrative remarks and weather columns into one imaginary diary.

Original explanatory diagram · Free printable entry witness card · Institutional sources checked 17 November 2026

A scanned ship's log looks wonderfully conclusive. Dates are printed. Times run down the page. Someone signed the remarks. Yet a logbook is an arrangement of records, not a single uninterrupted testimony. The title page can identify a vessel without proving it was present at a particular place. The remarks can establish that an officer recorded an event without reporting every event aboard. A weather column can contain measured conditions while remaining silent about the purpose of the voyage.

For U.S. Navy logs from roughly 1941 through 1959, the National Archives describes a recurring three-part arrangement: a monthly title sheet, daily remarks sheets and daily columnar sheets. Many volumes also have officer lists. The layout changed over time, and individual sheets may be incomplete. Read the page's function before reading its particulars.

First witness: the month's identity

NARA says the monthly title sheet usually has spaces for the ship name or hull number, commander, attachment to a unit or fleet, dates covered and sometimes location. It also warns that title sheets are frequently incomplete. That matters when a vessel has been renamed, when two hulls share a name, or when the month spans a commissioning or transfer. The title is a strong locator. It is not an alibi for every hour of the month.

Begin with the literal transcription, including uncertain handwriting and blank fields. Then compare the hull number with a separate official ship history and the catalog description. A ship's name without its identifier is not sufficient to resolve an ambiguity. If the title page says one month and a remarks sheet another, preserve both readings and inspect the adjacent sheets before explaining the discrepancy.

Second witness: remarks, not omniscience

NARA describes the daily remarks of this era as chronological entries for selected ship activities: changes of course or speed, landmarks, operations, supplies, transfers and certain incidents. The sheet may be typed or handwritten. The chronologically arranged 24-hour entries are dates and times recorded in the log; they are not automatically proof that clocks on another ship, at a shore station or in later testimony were synchronized.

Read around a passage. Note the first line of the watch, any continuation into the next sheet, the signature, corrections and the units of any numbers. A later handwritten correction is not necessarily deception: NARA's guide to rough and smooth logs says corrections to the official smooth record could be made while leaving the original legible and signing the change. Record a correction as a change you can see, not as an inferred motive.

Most importantly, a remarks sheet was not written to name everybody aboard or describe every assignment. An absence of a person's name should not be translated into evidence of absence. NARA explicitly lists day-to-day individual work assignments and many activities occurring away from the ship among the categories deck logs do not reliably record.

Third witness: the numbers in the column

For the same mid-century era, the columnar sheet could record wind direction and speed, pressure, air and water temperature, clouds, visibility, latitude and longitude, fuel and water use, draft and other operating measurements. Some sheets were incomplete or not filled in. A blank measurement is not zero; a single number with no column heading is not safely interpreted.

Write the heading, unit and exact date alongside every value you transcribe. Keep reported or estimated positions distinct from an independent survey fix. When studying weather, compare the columnar observation to remarks about conditions, then to an external weather record only after checking location, observation time and measurement basis. The NARA weather guide points researchers toward the Beaufort scale when interpreting wind and wave descriptions; do not silently impose today's instrument precision on an older qualitative entry.

Era trap. NARA reports that formats changed in 1960–1964, often combining abbreviated columns with remarks. The separate columnar weather sheets were discontinued in 1965. A 1970 photograph of a remarks sheet is not evidence that a missing 1951-style weather sheet was lost. The form itself changed. Read NARA's 1960–1978 format guide.

A seven-pass page examination

  1. Locate the archival object. Copy repository, record group, catalog or series identifier, ship number, month and image locator. A search-result snippet does not establish the image contents.
  2. Capture the title sheet. Preserve all identifying fields as written, including blanks and doubtful letters.
  3. Establish page function. Label remarks, columnar observation, officer list, cover or a later annotation. Different sheets answer different questions.
  4. Preserve the timestamp. Transcribe the original notation before making conversions. Keep clock zone or basis unresolved unless the source explains it.
  5. Read the neighboring material. Inspect preceding and following pages; a sentence or watch can continue beyond the cropped image.
  6. Inspect amendments and blanks. Record crossings-out, signatures and missing entries exactly; do not supply missing values.
  7. Write two conclusions. One line for what the page states, another for what a separate source corroborates. Leave unverified possibilities plainly marked.
