## Reader question

When two archival records report different times for an apparent single event, which clock was wrong? That question may be premature. A source may date first detection, a transmitted warning, a later log entry or the event itself. Separate them before attempting a conversion or declaring the accounts inconsistent.

**Method:** Record the exact item and original time as written → identify the action being dated → establish the historical clock and calendar basis from evidence → state an independently justified common-reference interval and its precision → compare only like actions → document unresolved assumptions. A modern DST calculation cannot establish a historical source's clock practice.

## Primary-source case: Apollo 1, January 27, 1967

The NASA preliminary memorandum by **Robert C. Seamans Jr. dated February 3, 1967** reports the fire **first detected at 6:31:03 p.m. EST** and cabin rupture **about 6:31:17 p.m. EST**. It warns that clocks at separate recording stations could have differences of one or two seconds and describes the account as preliminary.

The later **Apollo 204 Review Board chronology** records the **first verbal indication at 23:31:04.7 GMT / 6:31:04.7 p.m. EST** and the pressure vessel rupture at **23:31:19.4 GMT**. The two rupture reports differ by approximately **2.4 seconds**. Fire first *detected* and fire first *spoken about* are not identical event definitions. The early station-synchronization caveat is not sufficient by itself to explain all of the later difference; neither account proves a precise moment of ignition. Do **not** average those times or treat a mark on an editorial chart as an original instrument record. The existing minute-granularity interactive desk is **not** suited to adjudicating seconds-scale evidence.

5. NIST, [Daylight Saving Time Rules](https://www.nist.gov/pml/time-and-frequency-division/popular-links/daylight-saving-time-dst). The current rule is not a full historical-zone converter.
