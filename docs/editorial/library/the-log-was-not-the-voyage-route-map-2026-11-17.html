Find the right witness before reading the wrong one.

A ship's log is not a complete memory of its voyage. It is one record, made for particular duties, kept under particular rules, and sometimes found generations later as a scan of a scan. If you came here with a ship's name, a disputed hour or a missing sailor, don't read nine chapters in order. Begin with the specific question that brought you to the archive.

This is an editorial route map, not a claim that any one record will settle a case. Each route opens a complete free chapter farther down this page. The original wording of archival records, their dates and their rights must still be checked against the item itself.

Before the first archive search

  1. Make the claim testable. “A vessel entered port on Tuesday” is a claim. “The voyage ended strangely” is not yet a search instruction. Write the exact event or disputed assertion.
  2. Name the record creator. Navy, Coast Guard, merchant master, customs collector and wireless office are not interchangeable. They produced different paper trails, and retention rules differ.
  3. Keep the full identity. Record the vessel name and hull or registry identifier where applicable, flag, service dates, and known renamings. A shared name does not identify a shared ship.
  4. Define the date before converting it. Note the original clock and date convention verbatim; distinguish local ship time, a port office's administrative date and an archivist's modern catalog date.
  5. Inspect the object. A finding-aid listing proves that a series or item has been described. It does not prove a particular page says what you hope it says. Record the image, folio and entry before drawing conclusions.
  6. Write down what did not survive. A blank field, no digitized image, no search hit and an expressly destroyed or unretained log are four different evidentiary states. Record which one you actually encountered.

Three useful boundaries. A catalog record is evidence that a repository describes something, not a substitute for reading its images. A ship's daily record is evidence of the events selected for its purpose, not a complete passenger or crew database. And two marks on a page—whether coordinates or edits—need their creation context before anyone turns them into a story. These distinctions follow the National Archives' descriptions of deck logs, their limits and rough/smooth copying practices.

The first ten lines of a research receipt

Print this sheet, or copy the fields into a notebook. It deliberately asks for things a search engine often hides.

1. Exact claim or question
2. Vessel name as written
3. Hull / registry / renaming evidence
4. Target date in source notation
5. Record creator and repository
6. Series / catalog identifier / URL
7. Page, folio, frame or image number
8. Observation, transcription or inference?
9. Rights statement and access limitations
10. What remains unverified / next request
