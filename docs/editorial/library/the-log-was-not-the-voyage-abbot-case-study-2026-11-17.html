The Name Is Not the Ship's Record

A catalog search for Abbot can point you toward a destroyer. It cannot, on its own, tell you which destroyer, which navy, which period of service, or which pages actually survive.

Here is the trap in miniature: the U.S. Navy had two destroyers named Abbot. The first carried the number 184 and eventually served under a different name and flag. The second carried the number DD-629. The National Archives lists a February 1951 deck-log item for the second ship; the Navy's historical account puts that ship's recommissioning on February 26 of the same month. These are pieces of the same research route, not permission to invent entries for February 1 through 25.

The difference is not semantic. A search hit for a familiar vessel name is only a proposed identity. The record must be tied to a particular hull, a particular period, and the archive that describes the material.

Where the first ship's story goes

The Naval History and Heritage Command's Abbot I entry identifies Destroyer No. 184 as commissioned July 19, 1919; recommissioned June 17, 1940; and transferred to British service in September 1940, where it became Charlestown. A document dated in British service must be sought using the appropriate Royal Navy identity and records. It cannot be read as an early chapter of DD-629 merely because the American name matches.

But do not simply replace one keyword with another and declare success. A ship's service history identifies likely custodians; it is not itself a logbook inventory, and custody of a particular diary, deck log or report must still be shown by a series description or a repository confirmation. The first ship's precise surviving logs have not been inventoried for this case study.

Why February 1951 requires two separate checks

NARA's digitized Navy deck-log index explicitly lists Abbot (DD-629) and a link for February 1951, which points to National Archives Catalog ID 285104169. The official Abbot II history says this destroyer had been decommissioned in 1946 and was recommissioned on February 26, 1951.

That apparent tension is a useful stopping point, not a new historical mystery. A monthly catalog heading need not establish the existence of a complete daily run of entries. A historical summary and a repository index answer different questions. This review verified the catalog listing and the official service-history description; it did not inspect each image of the underlying February volume. It therefore does not assert what, if anything, was entered before recommissioning, nor claim that any particular officer performed a particular duty that month.

Documented: the National Archives indexes a February 1951 item to DD-629; the Navy historical entry dates recommissioning to February 26. Unresolved: how the volume itself labels or handles earlier days. Next evidence: its title page, first dated remarks sheet, page sequence, and relevant explanatory notes.

Eight checks before citing a shipboard entry

  1. Copy the vessel name exactly as the record or secondary account prints it, including an apparent misspelling or suffix.
  2. Identify the hull number or other period-specific official identifier. A name without a number may refer to several separate vessels.
  3. Use the target date to test the vessel's actual service periods; distinguish commission, decommission, transfer, renaming and later reuse of a name.
  4. Determine whether you need a U.S. Navy deck log, merchant official log, operating log, private journal or a report from a parent command. The same word “log” does not mean the same series.
  5. Search the NARA catalog using ship name plus “Deck Log”, then add the hull number and year as NARA recommends; do not rely on an exact search that includes the prefix “USS.”
  6. Open the descriptive record and preserve the catalog identifier, series, dates covered, and access state; a month in an index does not prove full image coverage.
  7. Read the actual title page, remarks pages and contiguous dates before making claims about an incident, crew member, route or silence in the log.
  8. Keep the unknowns in the citation. If images are inaccessible or dates are incomplete, state “index-confirmed, contents unexamined” rather than “nothing happened.”

Printable identity and coverage card

Use one card for each candidate vessel. If two identities remain possible, keep two cards; do not merge their citations. This worksheet is intentionally paper-first and works without scripts, sign-in or transmitted data.

Vessel identity / source-coverage receipt

2 · Hull or official number, source
3 · Flag, service and target date
4 · Commission/transfer/rename evidence
5 · Record type, creating office and series
