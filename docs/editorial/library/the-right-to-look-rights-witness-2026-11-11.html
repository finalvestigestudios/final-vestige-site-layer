The Right to Look Is Not the Right to Reprint

There are ninety doors in our Research Sources directory. A door to a document is not a license to copy what you find behind it.

A working rights and provenance desk for historical investigations, filmmakers, researchers and careful readers.

A federal report can be free to download and still contain a photographer's copyrighted image. A museum can make a million objects searchable and allow commercial reuse of only the ones marked CC0. A newspaper can be old and its digitized edition can carry a separate rights notice. The questions are not interchangeable.

This is a decision aid, not a legal opinion or an item-level permission certificate. The ninety source destinations come from an existing Final Vestige WordPress research directory; policy guidance was reviewed on November 11, 2026. The ninety destination URLs were not independently retested in this run. Check each object, caption, file and creator before reproducing it.

When linking

Retain the canonical source URL, work and collection title, object or report identifier, publication date (if known), agency or holding institution, date accessed and what the document actually supports. Link directly to the page a second reader can inspect.

When reproducing

Before placing a scan, screenshot, audio clip, figure or photograph on Final Vestige, identify who made that specific material, what the institution says about it, whether third parties retain rights, what kind of reuse is planned, and how the caption will credit the work.

The five-minute preflight

  1. Keep the record intact. Save its exact title, stable URL and reference number. A search page is not the item itself.
  2. Identify the creator. Hosting at a government domain does not make a contractor photo federal government work. Inspect bylines and captions.
  3. Read the item-level rights line. “Link-only,” “public data,” “download available” and “CC0” do not mean the same thing.
  4. Name the intended use. A scholarly citation, documentary insert, merchandise reproduction and advertisement create different questions.
  5. Check noncopyright restrictions. Privacy, publicity, donor agreements, access terms, trademarks, seals, sensitive cultural material and protected personal data matter.
  6. Keep an evidence trail. Record the policy URL, date checked, permission or license text, precise asset identifier, and credit line. If the status remains unknown, link rather than copy.

Institutions and rights policies

Policy entries were reviewed against the linked official pages on November 11, 2026. When a source uses the general federal rule, the linked statute is a starting point, never a substitute for creator/rights verification. For aggregators, use the originating item's record.

  1. NARA · NARA holdings: creator, donor and special-media restrictions must be checked

    Federal records in an archive can contain copyrighted and donated materials. Investigate the particular record and any privacy or donor restrictions.

  2. LOC · LOC: item Rights and Access statement controls

    Check the actual item Rights and Access or Rights Advisory. A collection-level label is not an item license.

  3. NASA · NASA: media generally usable with restrictions

    Check who created the media, licensed soundtrack/footage, identity and endorsement limits. NASA logos/seal are protected.

  4. USGS · USGS: government-created works generally public domain

    Check photo/figure credits and contributor exceptions. Credit USGS and do not copy its protected identifier.

  5. NOAA · NOAA: confirm author and credit before reuse

    Verify NOAA created the particular work; third-party materials are excluded. Clearly label edits and avoid implied endorsement.

  6. NTSB · NTSB: NTSB-created media versus third-party evidence

    An NTSB report can contain photos made by other parties. Board logo and seal require separate permission.

  7. NPS · NPS: government work generally public domain with exceptions

    Check creator and third-party credits. NPS arrowhead mark is protected; donor/privacy rights may apply.

  8. GPO · GovInfo: official publication can include copyrighted inserts

    Official publication does not make incorporated third-party images, figures or text automatically reusable.

  9. SI · Smithsonian: CC0 item badge is decisive

    Only CC0-designated assets receive that dedication. Other assets have usage conditions; trademarks and personal rights are separate.

  10. RUMSEY · David Rumsey: image permission with underlying map checks

    The site's general image permission does not override source copyright, especially post-1929 works; standalone facsimile sales need contact.

  11. BHL · BHL: record-level copyright status and license

    Public-domain volumes may be reusable; in-copyright volumes can be CC BY-NC-SA 4.0. Check volume and country/use.

  12. DPLA · DPLA: aggregator; use originating institution's rights notice

    Metadata/links do not transfer copyright or permission in source images; open the contributing institution's object page.

  13. UNT · UNT CyberCemetery: check originating agency and capture provenance

    A preserved website is not automatically a government-authored public-domain compilation. Inspect each captured component.

  14. COURT · CourtListener/RECAP: docket access is not blanket reuse permission

    A public filing may contain third-party copyrighted exhibits or protected personal information. Check the actual filing.

  15. FED · U.S. federal work: verify the work's creator and exceptions

    Section 105 concerns works made as part of federal official duties; it does not clear contractor material, third-party attachments, logos, privacy or non-U.S. rights.

Worked example: one PDF, three different claims

Imagine an official accident report that includes an agency-drawn diagram, a privately taken photograph, and a contractor's inspection table. The report may be free to download. The agency's own diagram might be a U.S. government work. The private photograph may have a copyright notice; the contractor table may carry another license. Those are three separate rights determinations, even when they appear on the same page.

The proper sentence is not “the whole PDF is public domain.” It is: “The report is publicly accessible; reuse rights for individual embedded materials require separate review.” An explanatory redraw using facts, independently cited and clearly credited as original, may be a better editorial choice than copying a doubtful image. This is a fictional composite example, not a claim about an actual docket.
