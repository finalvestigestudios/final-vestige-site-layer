A Missing Log Has More Than One Meaning

A blank online search looks like an answer. It is only the beginning of a question: was this book created, was it preserved, was it transferred, is it open, and does it record the thing you want to know?

A logbook can be on a shelf without appearing online. Another may never have been retained as a permanent record. A third may have survived but been withdrawn for review. A fourth may be exactly where it belongs yet contain nothing about an event that happened off the ship. Those are not variations on the same absence. They lead to different next steps, and treating them as one produces bad historical claims.

The National Archives distinguishes these situations in separate guidance on deck-log custody, withdrawn and missing records, and information never routinely recorded. Use those distinctions before declaring a ship's paper trail lost.

Seven different doors behind the same failed search

1 — The vessel might not have been a commissioned Navy ship

NARA says deck logs of commissioned Navy ships are permanent records. Many small service craft reported through a parent command rather than preserving an independent deck log. The Second World War brought limited exceptions. Check the ship's commissioning and hull history before searching again by name alone. A missing independent log for a service vessel is not evidence of an unrecorded voyage.

Next query: Which parent command received the vessel's reporting? NARA, deck-log introduction.

2 — The book may be held but not online

NARA's monthly digitized index is a growing access route, not an inventory of every surviving original. Some scans remain queued for processing; some dates have no predefined index link. Search the National Archives Catalog using vessel name without the USS prefix, “Deck Log,” hull number and target year. Then ask textual reference staff for the holdings, rather than telling a reader the book has disappeared.

Next query: Does the relevant month physically exist in the series even if no digital images are visible? NARA, digitization and searches.

3 — You may be asking the wrong custody desk

For U.S. Navy records, NARA generally places deck logs through 1940 in Washington and 1941 onward in College Park, with exceptions at the boundary. Merchant-vessel official logs follow still other paths: earlier RG 41, and later overlapping RG 26 records arranged by submitting port. A series number and record dates are stronger finding aids than a vessel nickname.

Next query: Who holds the original for this time, record group and port? NARA merchant-vessel guide.

4 — The record exists but access may be restricted

NARA distinguishes classified logs from withdrawn logs and explains the different correspondence and FOIA routes. Later deck logs may also await privacy review before online publication. Those are distinct statuses. Do not describe “withheld” as “lost,” or assume a restricted record contains the sought event. Confirm custody and current restriction before choosing a disclosure route.

Next query: Is this exact ship/month currently classified, withdrawn or awaiting processing? NARA, restricted and missing deck logs.

5 — The record may truly be missing from holdings

NARA identifies a small number of known missing Navy deck logs. Possible causes include ship loss, misfiling, failure to file, combat damage or other recordkeeping failures. None should be assigned to a specific ship without an archive response establishing it. A search miss alone does not authorize choosing a cause.

Next query: Can staff confirm that a known-created log was not transferred or cannot be located, and identify any related series? NARA, missing-logs guidance.

6 — The book may not record that kind of event

Deck logs generally do not itemize individual daily work assignments, aircraft movements after leaving a carrier, detached-duty events away from the vessel, or the details of shipyard repairs. Some temporary manifest records were never retained permanently. A blank deck-log page cannot establish that an off-ship task or flight did not happen.

Next query: Which personnel, operational, medical, maintenance or unit record was designed for the event? NARA, material not in deck logs.

7 — The vessel may have produced a different kind of merchant log

NARA separates U.S. merchant official logbooks (which can record crew and statutory incidents) from operating logs and engine-room or deck-department records. These follow different record groups, arrangements, dates and survival patterns. Not finding a narrative position journal in an official merchant series does not mean no operating log survives elsewhere.

Next query: Is the question about crew, legal events, navigation or machinery—and which series created that kind of record? NARA merchant logbooks.

Three sentences with different evidentiary weight

What the researcher knowsWhat the researcher may sayWhat remains unproven
The named month is absent from an online index.“I did not find an online link for this month in the index checked on [date].”Whether a paper original survives, is catalogued elsewhere or awaits digitization.
Reference staff confirm an exact log is held but withdrawn.“The holding institution reports the specified volume currently withdrawn from general access.”Its contents and any future release date.
Reference staff confirm a specifically created log is missing from their holdings.“The institution cannot locate this specified volume in the holdings searched.”How, when or why the loss occurred, unless separately documented.
A complete readable deck log has no mention of an off-ship flight.“This inspected deck log contains no entry documenting that flight.”Whether the flight occurred, which aircraft flew, or whether another record documents it.

The archivist needs a better question than “Do you have the logs?”

A useful reference inquiry is brief, specific and transparent about what the writer has and has not inspected. NARA asks for a ship name and hull number, dates of interest and, for restricted logs, the Record Group 24 deck-log series. Do not send a speculative FOIA request before establishing whether an open original exists. Do not send personal medical or veteran information in a general public inquiry.

Adaptable holdings inquiry — copy manually into your own message

Subject: U.S. Navy deck-log holdings question — [ship name / hull number], [month and year]

I am trying to establish the archival status of the U.S. Navy Ship Deck Logs for [ship, hull], covering [specific dates]. I checked [catalog/index and date checked] and found [exact observed result]. Can you advise whether this volume is held, undigitized, currently restricted, or recorded as missing from your holdings? If the volume is not available, is there an associated series or reference desk worth checking? My research question concerns [type of event, not an assumed finding]. Thank you.

Adaptable U.S. merchant-log inquiry

Subject: Merchant vessel logbook series / port inquiry — [vessel name and official number], [dates]

I am looking for [official incident/crew record OR navigational/engine operating log] for [vessel identity and approximate voyage date]. I have checked [specific series/index and search terms]. Could you advise whether RG 41, RG 26, RG 178 or another record series is appropriate, and whether arrangements by port, date, vessel name or official number apply? I am not assuming the requested book was created or preserved.

Follow the actual record: an index example, not a claim about the voyage

NARA's digitized month index lists Abbot (DD-629), February 1951, and links to Catalog Identifier 285104169. That is a useful successful discovery: a named month routed to an institutional catalog identity. It does not certify that an individual image is readable, establish a particular course, or prove an unmentioned event happened or did not happen. In this run we verified the index listing, not the underlying scanned pages.
