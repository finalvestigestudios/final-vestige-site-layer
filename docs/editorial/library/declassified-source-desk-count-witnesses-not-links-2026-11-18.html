Count the witnesses, not the links.

Three official pages can look like three confirmations. Sometimes they are one document, traveling in three coats.

Before adding a citation to an investigation, ask where its information came from. Two sites repeating one memo are two access points, not two independent accounts. The mistake matters most when a report says several sources confirm and the several sources lead back to one original statement.

A real example: one telephone memorandum, two official publications

In July 1953, a record of a telephone conversation between Secretary of State John Foster Dulles and CIA Director Allen Dulles referred to an Iran-related operation. The 1989 Foreign Relations volume, Document 335, printed a memorandum of that conversation, dated July 24, 1953. Its source line points to the Eisenhower Library's Dulles papers, “Telephone Conversations.”

A later editorial note, Document 251, in the 2018 Iran volume reproduces the telephone text and explicitly directs the reader back to Document 335, pages 737–738. That second appearance is valuable: it supplies context and a route for checking the passage. It does not supply a second listener to the telephone call. These are two editorial placements of one underlying memorandum. The original archival manuscript or typescript was not examined for this guide; the published transcriptions are the evidence inspected.

Defensible count: two reviewed State Department web documents, one identifiable underlying telephone memorandum and one line of reported dialogue. Not two independent records of a presidential decision. The reported assertion in the conversation is not itself an inspected presidential authorization instrument.

The six tests before calling a source independent

01 / Identify the claim. Write the exact assertion needing support. “A memorandum reports that approval was said to have occurred” and “the approval document has been found” are different claims.

02 / Find each document's source line. Capture original writer or recorder, date, repository, collection, series, page and item identifier. An agency host is not the same as an original creator.

03 / Trace explicit dependencies. Look for “reprinted from,” “citing,” footnotes, editorial notes, shared transcripts, syndication, identical errors and identical page references.

04 / Separate copies from accounts. A scan, translation, excerpt, transcription, revised edition and rehosting may all be different representations of one account. Differences among representations deserve checking; they do not create new witnesses.

05 / Test truly separate origins. Independent evidence might be another contemporaneous office record, a separate participant's note or a distinct archive's related correspondence. Shared institutional custody alone neither proves nor disproves independence.

06 / State the narrow result. Record how many pages you checked, how many underlying documents you can identify, whether any records really corroborate the proposition, and what remains unexamined.

Which count belongs in the story?

Count by evidence family when speaking about corroboration; count by publication when discussing access, interpretation, editorial history or textual variants. Both counts can be useful, provided they are named.

Try it: the four-link trap

Fictional practice exercise, not historical evidence. An online article cites four URLs for a supposed official finding. Link A is an agency-hosted memorandum. Link B is a later edited collection quoting A. Link C is a newspaper story quoting B. Link D is a photograph of B's page on a forum.

How many independent witnesses support the supposed finding? Based on that description alone, one identifiable underlying document, not four. The underlying memorandum itself may contain hearsay or error. To strengthen the claim, find a separate contemporaneous record, check its relationship to the original and report any contradiction. The four links remain useful as a transmission history; they do not independently verify the contents.

The Witness-Family Receipt

A record for the evidence behind a claim, not a score assigned to the source. Print this page or copy the field labels into your own research notebook. Do not enter personal information into third-party tools unnecessarily.

1. Claim being testedWrite narrowly, without assuming the conclusion.
2. Original creator / recorderNot necessarily the current website.
3. Original document dateDistinguish event, record, edition and access dates.
4. Repository / collection / seriesKeep collection and item levels distinct.
5. Source A: document / page / URLSpecify the edition actually examined.
6. Source B: document / page / URLRecord a second publication separately.
7. Shared source / explicit cross-citationWhich item do both derive from?
8. Textual differences, if inspectedState exactly where they appear.
9. Independent second record, if anyCreator, date, repository and dependence test.
10. Evidence families identifiedCount families, not web tabs.
11. What is not yet establishedMissing original, missing authorization, conflict, etc.
12. Checked date / next record to retrieveMake the search reproducible.

Decision line: SINGLE FAMILY / INDEPENDENCE VERIFIED / POSSIBLE DEPENDENCE / INSUFFICIENT EVIDENCE. Never convert “not located” into “never existed.”
