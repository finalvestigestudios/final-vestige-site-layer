The Report Can Be Read. The Wireless Slip Is Still Missing.

The gap between a received message and a delivered warning is no longer supported here by a modern transcription alone. A digitized copy of the actual 1912 British wreck commissioner's printed report is publicly accessible, and its relevant pages can be examined. That is a stronger documentary footing. It is not the same thing as recovering the original wireless form.

On printed pages 27–28 of Shipping Casualties (Loss of the Steamship “Titanic”), British command-paper Cd. 6352, the inquiry reviews the ice messages received during the voyage. Its account records the Mesaba report as reaching Titanic's Marconi room at 9:40 p.m. The report then distinguishes that reception from delivery to the bridge, concluding that Captain Smith did not receive it. This is the inquiry's published finding—not a surviving bridge receipt or recovered operator's handwritten slip.

A time written on one clock is not another clock's evidence

Adams's later testimony identifies 7:50 p.m., New York time, in his account of transmitting the report. The British findings describe its reception as 9:40 p.m., Titanic time. These are differently labeled historical reports. Preserve both clock labels before considering an offset: the first is attached to an operator's testimony, the second to the court's reconstructed narrative. A conversion is not a recovered timestamp, and two time references should not be treated as independently verified identical instants without checking their conventions.

The strongest available conclusion remains narrow. The British inquiry found nondelivery to the captain and emphasized the missed warning. That finding should not be silently upgraded to a verified account of the exact movements of a particular paper message. If a researcher discovers the original wireless log or the contemporaneous full transcript scan, it should be added as a separately identified witness, not retroactively attributed to this report.
