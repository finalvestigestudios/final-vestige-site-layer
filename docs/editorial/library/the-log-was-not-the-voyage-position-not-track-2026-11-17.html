The Log Was Not the Voyage · Position evidence desk · Free field instrument

The position was not the track.

An old ship's log gives you a latitude and longitude. A modern map accepts the numbers and offers a satisfying pin. The pin looks precise. That is where the historical argument can go wrong.

A position in a surviving record is an observation, estimate, copied report, or later transcription made under a particular method and convention. It is not the ship's continuous path. Two positions may describe the endpoints of a morning without revealing where the vessel went between them; a chart may be built from surveys separated by generations; an exact-looking coordinate may not even use the reference frame a modern map expects.

For historical interpretation only.This field instrument is not a navigation product, a current maritime chart, a route planner or a substitute for trained navigation. Do not use its figures to operate a vessel or assess present-day depths, hazards or clearance.

First, identify which witness supplied the position.

The U.S. National Archives describes NOAA and earlier Coast and Geodetic Survey vessel logbooks as containing, with variations among series, daily positions alongside weather, mechanical and surveying remarks. Its separate Navy deck-log index describes a chronological record of notable shipboard events, not a complete minute-by-minute movement trace. Those are related kinds of documents, not identical templates. Begin by naming the service, vessel, series, log form, date and page—not by extracting the coordinate alone. NARA survey-vessel logbook guide; NARA Navy deck-log index.

Then read the caption or heading around the position. Does it identify a clock time? Is the position labeled observed, estimated, at noon, at anchor, underway, or entered in a remarks column? Can you tell who entered it or whether it was copied from a different instrument or report? If the page offers no answer, record that it offers no answer. The temptation to fill blank metadata with the habits of modern navigation is stronger than the evidence.

When?The inscribed date and time, if legible.That the clock convention matches the one on a modern map or in another witness; see The Moving Noon.

Reconstruct the evidence, not the smoothest line.

  1. Recover the uncut page. Record collection, series, vessel identification, original page number, catalogue identifier, image URL and any adjoining pages that explain headings or columns. A search-result snippet is not a coordinate witness.
  2. Keep a literal transcription. Write degrees, minutes, cardinal direction, punctuation and any ambiguous character exactly as they appear, separately from a cleaned reading. If a handwritten digit could be a 3 or an 8, preserve both candidates. Do not silently choose the one that looks more plausible on a map.
  3. Separate date from clock system. An entry for 08:00 is not necessarily 08:00 UTC or 08:00 at the present-day location. Determine the record's stated zone, ship's convention or correction from contemporaneous instructions. If unknown, leave the comparison unconverted.
  4. Identify the kind of position. Recorded, calculated, estimated, reported by another vessel and observed are not synonyms. Preserve the actual label. When an entry has none, do not assign one.
  5. Find the chart of the correct period. NOAA's Historical Map & Chart Collection is a discovery route for former chart editions. Verify each candidate chart's edition, survey dates, corrections and legend. A chart published in one year may incorporate much older observations.
  6. Check horizontal reference information. NOAA explains that historical chart compilation may involve different source surveys and NAD27/NAD83. NGS's datum FAQ is a useful primer; NCAT supports appropriate conversions and transformations, but only if the input reference information is genuinely established. Do not select a datum merely to make a pin align with shore.
  7. Plot observations as points with limits. Mark source time, source page, uncertainty and any known chart or transformation details. Distinguish an actual recorded fix from a line drawn by a modern researcher. With two points, label the connecting segment an illustrative straight-line displacement, never an observed route.
  8. Cross-check independent witnesses. Compare adjacent pages, corresponding reports, port entries, contemporary charts and other surviving records where available. A log may say a ship was moored without documenting the shipyard work; absence of a note does not prove the ship was stationary or an incident never occurred. NARA's omissions guide is a necessary companion.

The chart is not neutral paper.

NOAA's Office of Coast Survey explains that charted data can combine surveys from widely different dates and with varying positional accuracy. Old hand-plotted compilation lines can introduce local errors that are not repaired by a uniform shift. Some positions are explicitly qualified as approximate or doubtful. A beautiful modern overlay therefore risks creating a level of agreement that neither the log nor the historical chart supports. Read NOAA's assessment of chart accuracy.

For a named historical vessel, never invent intermediate fixes to turn a single page into a voyage animation. Even if an interpolation is useful for explaining an hypothesis, label it an interpolation, show its basis, and do not present it as observed maritime evidence. A third-party map tile, scanned chart or archival photo also needs its own use-rights check; a public URL is not a universal reproduction license.

Illustrative case: two entries, three unsupported stories

Imagine a fictional log page containing an 08:00 position and a 12:00 position. No course, speed, navigation method, clock zone or chart reference survives in the supplied excerpt. One researcher connects the points directly. Another traces a coastline. A third plots a wide detour. None can show which path occurred from those two entries alone. All three stories fit the endpoints; that makes each an untested possibility, not a finding. The responsible public caption is much smaller: “Two positions are recorded. The intervening track cannot be reconstructed from this excerpt.”

Nor can a reader conclude that there was no deviation. The log may have adjacent pages or other sources worth locating. A missing intermediary record and a negative historical finding are different things. That distinction is the point of the larger Log Was Not the Voyage desk.
