The port date was not the docking.

A date in a customs register and an hour in a ship's deck log may both be correct. They may also answer two different questions. Here is how to find the actual records before making the dates agree.

Research method · Original explanatory diagram · Printable port-call receipt · Sources last checked November 17, 2026

A vessel turns toward a harbor. Hours later somebody signs an entry. A newspaper prints the arrival the next morning. A customs register gives the vessel one date; a logbook appears to give it another. It is tempting to pick one. First find out what each date belongs to.

The distinction matters whenever someone tries to reconstruct a voyage, a cargo's arrival, a ship's encounter, or a person's movements. A physical movement and an administrative act are not automatically the same event. Neither is a newspaper report the same witness as the harbor record it may have repeated. The evidence is strongest when the dates remain attached to their original record types.

The rule of this desk: Write “entered in the customs record” when that is what the document establishes. Write “reported moored at…” only when the actual log entry says so. Do not silently promote either into the precise time the bow crossed an invisible harbor boundary.

Four witnesses, four jobs

01 · The ship's own recordA commissioned U.S. Navy ship's deck log records selected notable activities, including movement and mooring when entered. It is not a passenger diary or a complete record of everything happening on shore. A merchant vessel's official logbook may serve a different purpose from its operating or narrative log. NARA Navy guide.
02 · The customs recordCustomhouses kept entrances, clearances, manifests and vessel documentation in record groups often organized by district and port. A record labeled “entered,” “arrived under permit,” or “cleared” needs to be read in its own administrative context. NARA RG 36 guide.
03 · The identity fileA vessel's documentation may identify its official number, master, owners, home port and physical characteristics. Use it to distinguish ships with similar names and to follow a vessel through changes in paperwork. Documentation alone does not demonstrate a specific port visit. NARA vessel documents.
04 · A later reportA newspaper shipping column or compiled historical chronology can suggest dates and spelling variants to investigate. Record who supplied the notice, its publication date and whether it says “arrived,” “entered,” “cleared” or “sailed.” A publication date is not necessarily the voyage date. Seek the earlier witness before treating a repeated notice as independent corroboration.

A real archive route: Superior, Wisconsin

National Archives at Kansas City's RG 36 series list names multiple Customs Service series for the Port of Superior, Wisconsin. Its descriptions include foreign-trade entrance-and-clearance registers for 1937–1941 (NARA Identifier 4719508) and separate coastwise registers for the same years (4719522). Later dated series distinguish vessels entered or arrived under permit to proceed (4719511) from vessels cleared or granted a permit to proceed (4719516). Those are archive series descriptions, not inspected individual vessel entries.

The distinction is practical: a researcher pursuing a 1939 coastwise call should not automatically request a foreign-trade register, and a researcher pursuing a 1950 foreign-trade clearance should not assume the 1937–1941 register covers it. The catalog IDs are useful retrieval anchors, not proof that any particular ship appears. The National Archives Catalog can open at Identifier 4719508, but its interactive record details and underlying item images could not be independently examined in this pass.

How to reconstruct one call without inventing an hour

  1. Define the question before searching. Do you need the vessel's presence at a port, the date of official entry, its physical mooring, clearance paperwork, departure, cargo discharge or a passenger's arrival? These are different requests.
  2. Fix the vessel identity. Record the literal name spelling, flag, master, official number or naval hull number, relevant year and home port. Record later name changes separately. NARA's vessel-document guide explains how registers, enrollments and official numbers intersect.
  3. Find the correct custodian and series. For U.S. customs holdings start with RG 36, then the target port or district and applicable dates. Verify the present location of the original series with NARA staff; older descriptions may refer to a different facility or transfer state.
  4. Search separately for a shipboard witness. If the subject is a commissioned U.S. Navy ship, find its deck log by identity and date. A merchant official logbook is not automatically a voyage narrative. NARA's merchant logbook guide distinguishes official and operating logs and describes port-based arrangements.
  5. Transcribe before interpreting. Copy the exact date, time, heading, abbreviations, location and surrounding lines. If the source says “under permit to proceed,” keep those words. Record blank, crossed-out or corrected entries faithfully; do not replace them with the label you expected.
  6. Build a record-by-record chronology. Make separate lines for observed approach, anchoring, mooring, official entry, clearance, departure and publication. Enter only events supported by a viewed item. Never derive an elapsed interval from two differently defined date labels.
  7. Check the clock. A naval 24-hour notation, a port civil clock and a later normalized timestamp may not align automatically. For overnight and time-zone questions consult FV's Library timekeeping field guides; preserve each original clock notation until the convention is documented.
  8. Close with a bounded finding. Use “this register series covers X dates” if you have only the finding aid; “this entry states X” only after viewing the entry; and “physical departure at Y” only where the vessel's record or another contemporaneous witness actually records it. Note any missing images or untested series.

The small disagreement that changes the conclusion

Consider a fictional research problem. A deck-log transcription says “moored, 23:40” on day A. A customs line says “entered” on day B. A newspaper published on day C lists the ship as arrived. It would be an error to call day B a mistaken arrival date merely because the deck log says the ship was alongside on day A. Each witness may be dating a different act. Equally, a customs “clearance” on day D is not direct proof of a vessel's departure on day D. Find the operative original entries and ask what their headings mean. Until then the disagreement is a question, not a correction.

There is a second risk: two apparently supporting summaries may descend from the same earlier notice. A newspaper copied from a shipping exchange and a later chronology repeating that newspaper are not three independent observations. Keep a field for probable shared origin. The original statement may be valuable; its copies do not magically turn it into eyewitness testimony.
