The Blank in the Official Volume

A government history can be authoritative, carefully edited, and still leave a hole exactly where your question begins. The first job is not to fill the hole with a theory. It is to establish what kind of hole you are looking at.

In 1989, the U.S. Department of State published an official volume about Iran between 1951 and 1954. In its own 2018 retrospective preface, the Department explained that the earlier volume had not documented the CIA's part in policy formation or the covert operation preceding the overthrow of Prime Minister Mohammad Mosadeq on August 19, 1953. The later publication did not erase the earlier one. It supplied a second, complementary body of edited records. Read the 2018 editorial preface and open the 1989 volume.

The distinction matters far beyond this particular history. An empty search result, a withheld paragraph and a document omitted from a selected edition are three different situations. They call for three different next questions.

What the editors disclosed

The 2018 volume's declassification review ran from 2004 through 2014. The preface reports three categories of remaining withholding. These figures describe the review of that volume, not all U.S. files about Iran and not all historical events.

10documents withheld in full
38documents with a paragraph or more excised
82documents with smaller excisions

The numbers do not license a reconstruction of what was withheld. They do establish that an authoritative compilation has limits its own editors recorded. Source: the official 2018 preface, declassification review.

Three blanks that should not be confused

Not chosen for this edition

The item may exist elsewhere, have been outside the editors' selection, or have been unavailable at the time. Read the preface, source list, and other editions before making an absence claim.

Deliberately withheld

The editors may identify a wholly withheld document or mark text removed during declassification review. Record the exact withholding notation; do not supply the missing words.

Transcribed, not a facsimile

An official transcription may give the words without showing handwriting, page breaks, ink, folds, or the underlying file. An editorial heading is not necessarily the document's original title.

Read a specific item before generalizing

Document 48 of the 2018 volume is a memorandum dated October 9, 1951. The printed text marks several portions as not declassified. Its first source note identifies a CIA file, job, box and folder, along with an original classification and drafting context. A reader can cite the text that was published and the repository locator the editors supply. The withheld lines remain unknown from this published transcription. The original physical file, its complete annotations and the masked text were not inspected for this guide.

A useful distinction: an editor's description of a source is evidence about the source's editorial treatment. It is not independent proof that every detail in the source is historically accurate. Nor does a later, fuller edition make the first edition a fabrication.

A seven-move method for the next archival gap

  1. Name the exact edition. Record its year, volume identifier, editors and publisher. Do not cite “the government report” as though there were only one.
  2. Read the preface before the search box. Look for selection boundaries, how dates are assigned, transcription conventions and declassification procedures.
  3. Open the source note. Capture institution, collection, file series, box, folder and classification where the editor gives them. Mark an unvisited repository as unvisited.
  4. Classify the blank. Selection omission, security excision, an unavailable source, a damaged page or a failed website are not interchangeable.
  5. Check another edition and nearby documents. Compare what each adds, while keeping repeated printings of the same memorandum in one document family.
  6. State only the supported claim. “Not printed here” is narrow. “Never existed” demands a far larger evidentiary search.
  7. Leave a recovery trail. Save canonical URLs, document numbers, last-checked date, access restrictions and an honest description of what you could not see.
