The sailor was not in the log.

A ship has a date. A sailor has a name. The archive does not necessarily have one document that puts both in the same room. Finding the person means learning which record was supposed to answer which question.

Imagine opening a ship's daily deck log and finding no trace of the person you are looking for. Perhaps a family story places him aboard; perhaps a photograph does. A blank search of that log is not a contradiction. U.S. Navy deck logs generally record selected shipboard events. They are not a daily census, and the ordinary movements of individual crew members may never appear in them. The next record is often not another page of the same log. It is the muster roll or personnel diary. NARA explains the exclusions.

Three different questions: Where was the ship or what event was entered? Who was formally attached to the command for a reported period? When did an individual's reported assignment or status change? No single answer proves the other two.

Read the period before choosing a roster

Record arrangements changed. NARA identifies pre-1940 Navy muster rolls in Washington, DC, with later rolls generally at College Park. For the period 1939–1956, its muster-roll guidance distinguishes enlisted names on the rolls from officer rosters ordinarily found in commissioned ships' deck logs. NARA's separate deck-log guide says most monthly officer lists ran through spring 1956; its muster-roll article says lists of officers and enlisted personnel appear together from March 1957. Treat the intervening change as a search qualification, not an invitation to impose a precise universal transition date on each ship. Read NARA's period notes.

Six checks before writing the conclusion

  1. Anchor the vessel. Record the full ship name, hull number or other command designation, and the narrowest supported date range. Similar names can refer to different vessels. Link the catalog description separately from any pages actually examined.
  2. Establish the ship-side event. Record the exact day and page of a deck log, if accessible. A catalog listing for February is evidence of an archival item, not of what happened on February 7.
  3. Find the relevant roster period. Search NARA's muster-roll guide and series. For twentieth-century material, note that selected records may be accessible via a commercial genealogy partner, but that does not make the data free to reproduce or guarantee completeness.
  4. Check movements, not just names. A personnel diary may record a reporting date, transfer or leave change. Examine surrounding reporting periods where available. No entry found is a search result, not proof that no transfer occurred.
  5. Separate assignment from attendance. A roster entry supports a claim about attachment to a command for that record period. It does not automatically put the sailor at a particular gun, on a particular boat, or even physically aboard on a target day. For individual service questions, consult NARA's Official Military Personnel File guidance.
  6. Declare the boundary. Record the repository, series/item identifier, page image examined or not examined, date, any contradiction and what remains unknown. If you cannot inspect the underlying roll or diary, stop short of confirming a crew identity.

A consequential boundary: from 1971, Navy muster rolls and personnel diaries use Social Security numbers as identifiers and face privacy restrictions. Do not publish those numbers in a public research receipt, and do not assume an old index gives unrestricted access to a newer person's record. NARA states that individual OMPFs become archival 62 years after separation; some copies may involve a fee. See NARA's public access rules. For 1971–1982 roll requests, see the NARA FOIA instructions.

A worked research distinction — with no invented crewman

The National Archives' online deck-log index lists a February 1951 logbook item for USS Abbot (DD-629). That locator is useful. It does not identify any particular sailor. To ask whether an individual was assigned to Abbot that month, the next source is a relevant muster roll or personnel diary for the same exact hull/command, with the surrounding reporting dates checked. Until those actual records are read, a name-level claim is not established. This is an archival research scenario, not a reconstructed incident.

Source ledger and rights · Checked 17 November 2026

  • National Archives, U.S. Navy Muster Rolls, 1798–1939 — series, custody and reporting-period guidance; points separately to later holdings. Free institutional guide; some record images may be hosted by a subscription genealogy service. Item-level rights must be checked.
  • National Archives, Know Your Records: Muster Rolls and Personnel Diaries — quarterly rosters, monthly diaries, officer transition and post-1970 privacy. Institutional archive article, not a promise that every described record is online.
  • National Archives, 1941–1959 Deck Logs — limited role of officer lists, remarks and transfer entries; pages vary by ship.
  • National Archives, Information NOT Included in Deck Logs — exclusions for routine daily assignments and off-ship activities.
  • National Archives, digitized deck-log index — discovery metadata only; does not substantiate unexamined page-level facts.
  • National Archives, OMPF public-access guidance — privacy qualifications for non-archival individual files; service-record requests and the 62-year rule.
