01 / Two honest records, different times

The first contradiction is often in the question.

Two accounts may both be authentic and still fail to describe the same instant. A crew member speaks. A microphone carries the words. A ground station registers them. A later chronology fixes a time to the recording. Those are related acts, not interchangeable ones.

Begin with the action, not the conversion. Copy the time as written. Name the event or observation it dates. Establish its clock standard from the historical record, not from your computer's modern time-zone menu. Compare only justified time windows.

The stopping rule: when the clock basis or event identity cannot be established, mark the comparison unresolved. Uncertainty properly preserved is evidence of care, not a failure of research.

02 / A documented historical distinction

The Apollo 1 times that should not be averaged.

NASA's February 3, 1967 preliminary memorandum recorded the cabin rupture at approximately 6:31:17 p.m. EST. The later Apollo 204 Review Board chronology places rupture at 23:31:19.4 GMT, corresponding to 6:31:19.4 p.m. EST. The reported figures differ by approximately 2.4 seconds. The early memorandum warns that recording stations may not have been synchronized to within a second or two. That caution is relevant, but it does not establish precisely why the later figure differed.

The preliminary memo also reports the fire first detected at 6:31:03 EST. The later chronology dates the transmission reporting the fire to 23:31:04.7 GMT. A detection and a spoken report are not automatically the same action; the figures should not be averaged to invent an exact ignition time. Nor are two NASA publications automatically independent witnesses: both may draw upon a common test-data chain.
