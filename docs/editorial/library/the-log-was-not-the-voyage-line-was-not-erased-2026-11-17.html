The Line Was Not Erased

There is an instinctive suspicion about a line drawn through an official record. Someone changed the words. Therefore someone must have wanted the first version hidden. But a surviving correction can mean almost the opposite: the procedure required the original wording to remain visible.

One of the most useful sentences in the National Archives' guidance on U.S. Navy logs is a distinction between a rough record and a smooth one. Rough and smooth identify a stage of record-making, not two universally separate classes of naval document. A working log could be copied into a final official log. That copying is part of the evidence's history. So is a later authorized correction. NARA: Other Logs

The researcher’s task is not to decide whether the change looks suspicious. It is to reconstruct the documentary sequence: which version survives, what was changed, by whom, when, under what rule, and what other record might explain it. A single altered numeral does not establish intent. Neither does the absence of an earlier notebook prove that one never existed.

Two records, and the third one we usually read

The working record

The rough log or quartermaster's notebook may have been the contemporary working surface. Corrections can occur there as part of drafting. Do not call every change an official amendment.

The official version

The smooth log was the finished record. NARA says erasures were not permitted; corrections were possible with a signature from the authorized keeper, typically retaining the previous entry under a single struck line.

The surviving holding

NARA generally retains deck logs of commissioned U.S. Navy ships as permanent records. Many other working logs were temporary: NARA describes approximate retention of one to three years for quartermaster and similar logs.

The image we inspect

A scan records a view of a page at the time it was photographed. It may leave small signatures, verso marks, binding context or subtle writing difficult to read. A catalog record proves a description exists; it does not show every mark on the sheet.

These are the U.S. Navy record-practice distinctions documented in NARA's guides, not a universal rule for all countries, periods, vessel classes or privately written journals. NARA's account of 1941–1959 logs also describes watch-by-watch signatures and navigation-officer approval. Those are points to inspect on a particular record, not proof that every surviving page is complete. NARA: 1941–1959 Deck Logs

What a changed entry proves — and what it does not

Suppose a scanned page appears to show a time crossed out and another time entered beside it. The first defensible observation is narrow: this image depicts a changed inscription. If the scan is clear enough, a researcher may transcribe both readings and describe the physical mark. The next question is whose hand or authority is visible, and whether the supposed signature is part of the correction or a routine watch sign-off.

Examine the correction, not the conspiracy

  1. Identify the page before the mark. Capture ship name and hull number, record group, series, date, watch, page or image number, catalog identifier and repository URL. The name of a ship by itself is rarely sufficient.
  2. Read outward. Check the monthly title page, previous and following remarks sheets, adjoining watch blocks and, where available, columnar data. Keep the original clock system visible before normalizing time.
  3. Transcribe twice. Record the older legible reading and the replacement separately. Preserve punctuation, abbreviations, uncertainty and visible strike marks; mark undecipherable characters as undecipherable rather than silently guessing.
  4. Separate text from authority. Describe any initials or signatures and their locations. A routine end-of-watch signature is not automatically a special certification of a later correction.
  5. Look for a second witness. Where they survive, compare related deck-log pages, orders, communications, operational summaries and contemporary shore records. A matching later summary might be dependent on the same source rather than independent corroboration.
  6. Ask the archivist a version question. Request any accessible rough notebook, original rather than microfilm or scan, correction sheet, page replacement or related series. NARA warns that many ancillary working logs were scheduled as temporary, so a negative search must not be reported as a confirmed historical loss.
  7. Keep the scan's limits explicit. Cropping, low resolution, skew, bleed-through or contrast can mimic obliteration. A questionable mark may require inspection of another image or the original record by repository staff.
  8. Write a conclusion at its actual strength. “The surviving smooth-log image shows an altered time whose earlier reading appears to be 1400” is a statement of evidence. “The crew changed the time to hide a collision” needs independent proof of event, chronology, authorization and intent.
A non-event matters too. The National Archives emphasizes that deck logs were not comprehensive diaries. They generally omit day-to-day individual assignments and many events away from the ship. Even a perfectly preserved and unaltered entry does not establish everything that happened around it. What deck logs do not include.

A research request that can actually be answered

When contacting an archive, do not start with “Why was the record changed?” That asks the repository to supply a motive it may never have documented. Ask for the custodian, record type and version: “I am comparing the rough and smooth records for [ship / hull], [date and watch]. The online image is [catalog and image ID]. Can you confirm whether an earlier working record, the original smooth page, replacement page or correction documentation survives, and whether it can be consulted?”

For U.S. Navy holdings, start at NARA's deck-log introduction and digitized-record guidance. For a port, merchant vessel, coast guard or British ship, establish the responsible record system first. The procedural description here is not a substitute for that record system's own practice.
