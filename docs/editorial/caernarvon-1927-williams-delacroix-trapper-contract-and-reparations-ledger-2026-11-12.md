## New case located — findings and source strength
**Williams v. Delacroix Corporation**, 160 So. 444, Louisiana Court of Appeal, **April 15, 1935**, reproduced as a complete reporter-text transcription at [Counsel Stack](https://law.counselstack.com/opinion/williams-v-delacroix-corporation-lactapp-1935). This is a **judicial account written eight years after the flooding, accessed through a third-party transcription**, not a 1927 interview, original party contract, reparations register, courthouse file or inspected official printed reporter page. Facts listed are the court's treatment of the record, not independent findings by Final Vestige.

- The appellate opinion identifies **Aleide Williams, Andrew Rhodes and Sam Sanchez** as three parties to a **March 17, 1927** trapping-land agreement with **Acme Land & Fur Company**, covering **lots O and Q** of the Swift tract in **St. Bernard Parish**. The stated total was **$3,500** over a term originally ending **February 5, 1931**, with **$422.45** up front (cash and/or fur deliveries) and later interest-bearing notes. The disputed contract granted trapping rights, with a contingent transfer of land title only after full performance. Do not simplistically call these parties outright owners.
- The artificial **Caernarvon breach** submerged the parcels; the opinion reports **no trapping during the winter 1927–28 season**, and an additional 1928–29 suspension while animals replenished. The agreements were subsequently adjusted; **Delacroix Corporation acquired Acme's rights on October 6, 1930**.
- The opinion recounts a negotiated arrangement around a **Reparations Commission** payment for the 1927–28 trapping losses, part allocated to a note and part returning the initial **$422.45** to the three plaintiffs. **The opinion's freely accessible transcription contains contradictory amounts for the total Commission payment**, and therefore none of these totals should be printed as a verified payout without checking an original reporter page or separate claims record. See below.
- The trappers later sued over money retained after contract default; **the Louisiana Court of Appeal affirmed dismissal in 1935**. The court's holding was about whether their payments exceeded fair rental value under the contract, not a universal judicial ruling that *all* displaced Caernarvon trappers were denied reparations. The court did **not have to determine** whether the original contract was legally a sale or lease because it decided the claim failed under either framing.

**Arithmetic/transcription red flag:** The accessible copy says **$1,239.45** in its early description of the reparations settlement, **$1,230.45** when discussing the payment among plaintiffs' claimed expenditures, and **$1,247.45** when calculating $2,140.66 less $893.21. It also describes **$617** toward a note and **$422.45** delivered to the plaintiffs; those add to **$1,039.45**, not the first amount. At least one number may be an OCR or republication/transcription error. The $2,140.66 − $893.21 = $1,247.45 subtraction is internally consistent, but this alone **does not authenticate** the payment amount. Do not silently reconcile the numbers or invent a clean settlement figure; inspect a reporter scan or an original reparations entry before quantitative use.

## Independent but different judicial corroboration
[**Acme Land & Fur Co. v. Commissioner**, 31 B.T.A. 582 (U.S. Board of Tax Appeals, Nov. 14, 1934)](https://openjurist.org/31/bta/582/acme-land-fur-co-v-commissioner) independently confirms that Acme's trapping-land business and corporate claims were entwined with the 1927 artificial breach. The opinion reports **108 days of inundation**, a **two-season trapping pause** and substantial corporate wildlife-loss awards. **It does not independently establish Williams's exact contract, reparations ledger allocation, payment arithmetic, or the later court's interpretation.** Do not merge corporate award receipts, individual trapping-contract payments, total community reparations or unearned profits into one figure.

The previously cited **Fabre v. Board of Commissioners**, 127 So. 603 (La. Mar. 5, 1930), [court opinion](https://hallapproved.com/la/cases/supreme/1930/3476857/), rejects a distinct Delacroix Island fish business's **$3,441.62** lost-profit demand while noting property-loss compensation. That was a different claim and outcome; no identity between these litigants is inferred.

## Documentary significance and cautious editorial use
The strongest verified new insight is **mechanism, not another scandalous headline**: the flood interrupted a conditional path toward ownership, a compensatory payment interacted with a private installment agreement, and a later contract lawsuit arose after trapping seasons were lost. The court's treatment demonstrates how a public emergency and private financial obligations overlapped. It **cannot** establish the total scope of relief, common treatment of all trappers, or actual aggregate dollars reaching families.

**Optional future paragraph for controlled editorial review (NOT inserted into WP draft):**

> One later lawsuit shows how the water moved through private contracts long after the levee was closed. In March 1927, Aleide Williams, Andrew Rhodes and Sam Sanchez had contracted with Acme Land & Fur to trap two parcels of marsh, with ownership possible only if they completed the payments. Caernarvon submerged the land weeks later. Trapping stopped for two seasons, the payment schedule was revised, and the agreement eventually collapsed into litigation. In 1935, a Louisiana appellate court ruled that the men could not recover the payments retained after default. The opinion documents one dispute, not a representative sample of claimants; even its reproduced reparations arithmetic needs confirmation against the printed reporter.
