## Primary-source verification ledger

### 1937 — refunding bonds did not cancel the obligation

**Source:** Louisiana Supreme Court, *Tonry v. Board of Levee Commissioners for Orleans Levee District*, 186 La. 159, 171 So. 836; decision January 15, 1937. Full opinion: https://law.justia.com/cases/louisiana/supreme-court/1937/186-la-159.html

The court affirmed a plan to substitute **$3,387,000 in lower-interest refunding bonds** for the same amount of original 1928 Caernarvon reparation bonds. The original special-tax lien followed the bonds: the state-provided property tax was not extinguished simply by exchanging the obligations for new securities. The opinion describes the interest on the original issue at **4¾ percent**, with replacement rates **3½ and 4 percent**. These are *bond-financing facts*, not direct evidence of awards to flooded residents. See the text around printed pp. 160–164.

### 1941 — attempted repeal of reparation-bond tax authority

**Source:** Louisiana Supreme Court, *Graham v. Jones*, 198 La. 507, 3 So. 2d 761 (1941), including rehearing discussion. Full opinion: https://law.justia.com/cases/louisiana/supreme-court/1941/198-la-507.html

The challenged Act 384 of **1940** proposed sweeping changes and repeal of the constitution's Article XVI-A (“Caernarvon Levee Break — Reparation Claims”). The court identified **several million dollars of reparation bonds still outstanding** and the Orleans one-mill tax provision as the specified authority for servicing principal and interest. It invalidated the multi-subject amendment under constitutional submission/separability requirements. The opinion also recounts the **December 1936** bond refunding and the 1937 *Tonry* decision. See printed pp. 560–564, 606–612 for Caernarvon-specific reasoning.

**Judicial-date caution:** The reported litigation includes an opinion of June 30, 1941, and rehearing material dated July 18; Justia's summary uses **July 18, 1941**. The article deliberately says simply “in 1941,” without treating one metadata date as the complete litigation chronology.

## Editorial inference, bounded

The cases expose an asymmetry: financing for the reparations bonds received enforceable continuing protection, while other Louisiana Supreme Court opinions in the same article narrowly excluded certain lost-wage and anticipated-profit claims. **Neither new opinion supplies a full claims ledger, identifies what every household received, or proves that bond proceeds reached all people affected by the breach.** Avoid describing the bonds as compensation fully paid.

This is a post-disaster institutional and financial afterlife, not a new hydraulic proof that the levee breach saved New Orleans. The opinion's historical language reflects the court's explanation, not an experimental no-breach river-stage observation.
