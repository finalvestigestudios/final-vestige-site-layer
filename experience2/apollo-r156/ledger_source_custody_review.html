EVIDENCE 01 / 06DOCUMENTED CONTEXT

Three men. One ground test.

NASA archival image: catalog ID not published on linked page (context only)

This was a countdown rehearsal, not a launch. The rocket was unfueled; the spacecraft was not free of danger.

SOURCE CUSTODY — IMAGE ≠ TECHNICAL PROOF

Image: Portrait of Grissom, White and Chaffee near Launch Complex 34 during training (January 1967). Claim evidence: NASA historical account. Ground-test status and context; the portrait does not document ground-test hazards.

EVIDENCE LIMITATION

An unfueled launch vehicle did not mean a safe cabin.

Review the underlying finding

Gus Grissom, Ed White and Roger Chaffee prepared to rehearse an Apollo countdown at Launch Complex 34. The plugs-out test was treated as comparatively nonhazardous because the Saturn IB was not fueled. The spacecraft still contained a dense oxygen environment, combustible materials and an escape hatch that could not be opened quickly under pressure.

EVIDENCE 02 / 06BOARD-SUPPORTED FINDING

An atmosphere built for a different risk.

NASA archival image: S67-15717 (context only)

One hundred percent oxygen at elevated ground-test pressure greatly increased the fire hazard inside Command Module 012.

SOURCE CUSTODY — IMAGE ≠ TECHNICAL PROOF

Image: Spacecraft 012 being moved for mating to the Saturn Lunar Module Adapter (1967-01-03). Claim evidence: Apollo 204 Review Board report. Cabin oxygen concentration and ground-test pressure; the photograph does not measure atmosphere.

EVIDENCE LIMITATION

Oxygen accelerated combustion; it was not itself the ignition spark.

Review the underlying finding

The 1967 Review Board documented a 100 percent oxygen cabin atmosphere at 16.7 pounds per square inch absolute during the ground test. Combustible materials and potential ignition sources were present. This was not the same pressure regime the spacecraft would use in orbit.

EVIDENCE 03 / 06REVIEW BOARD CHRONOLOGY

Fourteen seconds changed the system.

NASA archival image: S66-58038 (context only)

The surviving instrument record describes an extraordinarily rapid pressure-and-fire sequence.

SOURCE CUSTODY — IMAGE ≠ TECHNICAL PROOF

Image: Apollo 1 crew preparing to enter the command module in an altitude chamber (1966-10-18). Claim evidence: Apollo 204 Review Board chronology. Time markers and pressure observations; 1966 training photo is not a January 1967 sensor record.

EVIDENCE LIMITATION

The timeline reports observed markers. The intervals between them are not interpolated sensor measurements.

Review the underlying finding

The first fire transmission began at approximately 23:31:04.7 GMT. About six seconds later the cabin-pressure sensor showed a clear rise; by approximately 23:31:16 its range was exceeded. The pressure vessel ruptured at least 29 psia around 23:31:19. These timings are approximate marks from the Board account, not a reconstructed second-by-second animation.

EVIDENCE 04 / 06HARDWARE AND DESIGN FINDING

The hatch opened inward.

NASA archival image: KSC-20170124-PH_KLS01_0164 (context only)

A seal intended to help contain pressure became an obstacle when pressure rose during the fire.

SOURCE CUSTODY — IMAGE ≠ TECHNICAL PROOF

Image: Apollo 1 tribute display with spacecraft and hatch exhibits photographed by Kim Shiflett (2017-01-24). Claim evidence: Apollo 204 Review Board report. Original hatch and emergency-egress hazard; museum display photographed fifty years later is context.

EVIDENCE LIMITATION

The hatch was one of several interacting hazards, not a standalone explanation of the fire.

Review the underlying finding

The Block I command module used an inward-opening pressure hatch. The Review Board found that pressure relief could not keep up with the fire-driven pressure rise, so the hatch could not be opened before the pressure vessel ruptured. Ed White was responsible for opening the hatch; investigators observed movement consistent with an attempt to begin the procedure.

EVIDENCE 05 / 06EVIDENCE BOUNDARY

The spark was never conclusively identified.

NASA archival image: S67-21294 (context only)

Investigators found probable evidence of electrical arcing, but the final ignition source remained unproven.

SOURCE CUSTODY — IMAGE ≠ TECHNICAL PROOF

Image: Fire-damaged Command Module 012 interior photographed after the accident (1967-01-28). Claim evidence: Apollo 204 Review Board report. Probable electrical arcing and unresolved ignition point; damage image does not establish the spark.

EVIDENCE LIMITATION

Probable is not proven. The prototype deliberately keeps this uncertainty visible.

Review the underlying finding

The Apollo 204 Review Board identified an electrical arc in or near the probable origin area as the most likely initiator. Fire damage destroyed crucial physical evidence; it did not conclusively establish one wire or arc as the ignition source. The Board nevertheless documented vulnerable wiring, combustible materials, the hazardous oxygen environment and inadequate egress provisions.

EVIDENCE 06 / 06ENGINEERING RESPONSE

Inward became outward.

NASA archival image: KSC-20170124-PH_KLS01_0090 (context only)

The redesigned Block II hatch could be opened outward quickly; the safety overhaul extended far beyond the hatch.

SOURCE CUSTODY — IMAGE ≠ TECHNICAL PROOF

Image: Redesigned outward-opening hatch exhibited in the 2017 Apollo 1 tribute, photo by Kim Shiflett (2017-01-24). Claim evidence: NASA 2017 tribute description and original Board report. Tribute describes the redesigned hatch; original Board report records hazards and predates redesign.

EVIDENCE LIMITATION

The improvements were part of a broad redesign; Apollo 1 did not create every safety innovation from nothing.

Review the underlying finding

NASA replaced the complex inward-opening hatch with an outward-opening, quick-release assembly. It also changed materials, wiring protection, rescue preparation and safety review. The Apollo investigation article notes 102 deliberate ignition tests of Command Module boilerplate 1224 from December 1967 through February 1968—an example of testing how fire would actually spread rather than assuming it could always be prevented.
