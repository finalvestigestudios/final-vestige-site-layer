In 1843, James Braid published a book with a distinctly nineteenth-century title: *Neurypnology; or, the Rationale of Nervous Sleep*. It asked readers to reconsider experiences then associated with animal magnetism. It also presented case reports and therapeutic assertions of its era. Those pages remain valuable records of what Braid argued—not proof that everything he proposed worked.

Nearly two centuries later, we face a version of the same evidentiary problem. A recording can exist. A listener can find it pleasant. A seller can promise better concentration. Those are three different claims, and one cannot establish the next.

Consider **Total Focus**, a downloadable session sold by Hypnosis Live. Its own listing describes a **33-minute, 47-MB recording** by J.A. Amos. Those are product details a buyer can check. The same listing promises improved concentration and productivity. We have **not independently tested that recording**, and research on hypnosis in general does not prove those branded promises.

Even the apparently simple question *“Can I return it?”* deserves a second look. The product page advertises a 14-day refund window. The merchant's FAQ adds a limit of **five refunded sessions per year**. That's a material qualification worth finding before paying; exact checkout and refund handling have not been independently verified.

Here is a better five-minute buying exercise. On a single sheet, write down (1) the exact format and length, (2) the outcome the vendor actually promises, (3) what evidence tests **this particular recording**, and (4) the complete refund terms. If any answer depends only on broad claims about hypnosis, label it unknown. It's a useful habit for evaluating almost any digital product.
