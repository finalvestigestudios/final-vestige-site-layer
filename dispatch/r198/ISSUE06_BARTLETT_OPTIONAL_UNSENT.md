**Subject:** The canoe became a boat
**Preheader:** A 1932 memory experiment shows what our retellings can change.
**Type:** Editorial only · no merchant CTA · historical research
**Reader promise:** A documented experiment and a practical method for checking your own retellings.

In a remembered story, a canoe became a boat.

It was not a correction made by an editor. It was part of what a listener later said had happened.

In his 1932 book *Remembering*, Cambridge psychologist Frederic Bartlett described asking readers to reproduce a story often called "The War of the Ghosts." Its unfamiliar cultural details did not necessarily survive the journey from reading to retelling. Participants sometimes transformed elements into more familiar terms. A canoe might become a boat.

That is a compelling observation. It is also easy to abuse.

Bartlett's work did **not** prove that every memory is false, that eyewitnesses are always mistaken, or that every retelling changes in the same way. His study used particular materials and social settings. Later research has found some narratives are remembered more faithfully than others.

The useful question is narrower: **when you retell a record, which parts did you check, and which parts did your expectations fill in?**

Try this with a harmless account of your own. Read a short documented story and write down three precise details. A day later, retell it without looking. Compare your version with the original. Notice what disappeared, what became more familiar, and what stayed. This exercise is personal observation, not a diagnostic test or scientific proof about your brain.
