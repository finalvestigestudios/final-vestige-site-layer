Subject: The picture changed. Did you see it?
Proposed preheader (not provider-certified): A 1997 experiment shows why a visible change can escape notice.

A scene flickers. A detail changes. You watch the two versions again and again—and still cannot find what is different.

The change is there. The surprise is that looking at the picture does not guarantee noticing it.

THE EXPERIMENT

In 1997, Ronald Rensink, J. Kevin O’Regan and James Clark alternated original and altered scenes with brief blank interruptions. Viewers could struggle to detect even substantial repeated changes. A verbal cue often made detection faster. Their paper was about change detection under experimental viewing conditions, not about proving that any particular historical witness had been mistaken.

THE DIFFERENCE THAT MATTERS

An archival photograph can preserve a small feature its first caption writer overlooked. Finding the feature later may change an interpretation, but it does not establish why the earlier observer missed it. We still need the original image, its provenance, the circumstances of the observation and independent corroboration.

That is the Final Vestige rule: keep the observation separate from the explanation. Do not treat a confident account as a complete record; do not treat an omission as proof that nothing was there.

A SMALL EXPERIMENT FOR YOUR NEXT DISCOVERY

Choose a familiar picture and write down three details you notice without searching. Look again slowly, and record any new observation separately from what you think it means. If nothing new appears, that is an honest result, too.

This exercise is not a clinical intervention. The experiment does not establish the effectiveness of a meditation or hypnosis recording. It offers something more useful to an investigator: a reason to look again before declaring a case closed.
