**Subject:** Apollo 1: the cause was bigger than a spark

**Preheader:** NASA documented the hazards but could not isolate the exact ignition point.

**Content type:** editorial

---

One sentence can make the Apollo 1 fire seem comprehensible: an electrical spark ignited the cabin. The problem is that the official investigation did not conclusively identify the precise point of ignition.

What it did identify was a deadly arrangement of conditions: a pressurized, oxygen-rich environment during a ground test, combustible materials, vulnerable electrical equipment, and a hatch that could not be opened quickly enough in those circumstances.

The timeline deserves the same care. A preliminary NASA account put the cabin rupture approximately fourteen seconds after the first fire indication. The Apollo 204 Review Board's final report described rupture roughly fifteen seconds after the crew's verbal report of fire. Those are different reference points and approximate measurements, not a stopwatch for the crew's deaths.

The investigators didn't need an invented certainty about the first spark to describe what made the situation catastrophic. The response was not merely to hunt for one failed component. It forced a reconsideration of the materials, test conditions and escape system.

A useful habit for the reader is to distinguish three columns: what instruments recorded, what investigators inferred from those records, and what remained unknown. It's a better test of an explanation than whether it sounds tidy.
