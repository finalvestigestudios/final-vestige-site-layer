**Subject:** The stranger they didn't see change

**Preheader:** A 1998 street experiment asks how much attention can really hold.

**Content type:** editorial

---

In 1998, researchers stopped pedestrians to ask for directions. Mid-conversation, two workers carried a door between the speakers. While it blocked the view, a different researcher took the first one's place.

Only about half the pedestrians detected the switch. The paper did not prove that nobody notices anything. It showed that even a person we're speaking to can change without our registering it, under particular experimental conditions.

That ought to make anyone who works with testimony curious.

When Final Vestige examines a photograph or a witness statement, we ask what it establishes. The same question belongs in our examination of attention. An experience may be sincere and still leave out a consequential detail. Being confident about what you saw is not the same as having a complete record.

Here's a modest exercise: after your next conversation, write down three things you remember with confidence. Then mark which details you actually attended to and which you reconstructed afterward. Don't treat the list as a clinical test. It's a reminder of how quickly observation becomes explanation.

Our investigation traces the door-switch experiment back to the published work, rather than turning a surprising result into a promise that an app, an audio track, or a course can fix your attention.
