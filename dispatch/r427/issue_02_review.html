What the 1784 blindfolded tests really tested

Paris, 1784. People described unusual sensations during treatments that supposedly worked through an invisible substance called animal magnetism. Some accounts were dramatic. The royal commissioners faced a more demanding question: how could they tell whether the proposed fluid—not the expectations surrounding it—caused those experiences?

Benjamin Franklin joined other investigators to examine the claims of Charles d'Eslon, a practitioner of animal magnetism. The resulting report describes tests that separated what a participant believed was happening from what the practitioners said they had actually done.

In some tests, people expected to receive the influence even when the alleged physical treatment had not taken place. In others, the supposed influence was applied without the subject knowing it. The commissioners reasoned that reactions produced without the alleged fluid could not establish that a distinct fluid existed. They discussed imagination and other ordinary human processes as explanations.

That is strikingly relevant to how we evaluate historical testimony. A sincere witness can accurately report a sensation while being mistaken about its cause. A persuasive story may document an event without resolving the mechanism. The relevant question becomes: what comparison would distinguish one explanation from another?

The boundaries matter, too. The Franklin commission examined specific eighteenth-century claims; it was not a modern clinical trial, did not test every treatment that later came to be called hypnosis, and cannot establish whether an individual contemporary audio product delivers what its seller promises.

To carry the method forward, try writing two competing explanations for an extraordinary claim you encounter this week. Then ask what evidence would differ between them. If the answer is nothing, the claim may not yet have a meaningful test.
