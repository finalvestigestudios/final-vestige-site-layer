The map that didn't stop the outbreak

John Snow’s 1855 map, an earlier pump intervention, and the evidence the picture leaves out.

OFFLINE REVIEW ONLY — NOT A JETPACK PREVIEW — UNSENT, UNAPPROVED

In September 1854, cholera spread through the streets around Broad Street in London. A familiar version of the story says one doctor drew a map, spotted a pump, had its handle removed, and stopped an epidemic. That account is memorable. The dates make it less tidy.

John Snow met the St James's parish guardians on September 7. The pump handle was removed the next day. In Snow's own account, new attacks had already declined before that intervention. We cannot responsibly attribute the entire decline to the missing handle.

The famous map that many of us picture was published in 1855. Its black bars mark fatal attacks at houses, as far as Snow could identify them. They are not a count of every infected person, and the map does not tell us who actually drank pump water.

That does not make the map useless. It makes the map a starting point for better questions. Snow combined addresses with interviews, water-supply comparisons, and exceptions worth investigating. A visible cluster suggested where to look; other records helped assess why it appeared.

Try the evidence test on any historical image: first name what is depicted. Then name what you must obtain from somewhere else—date, denominator, exposure, or testimony. Finally, ask which appealing conclusion the picture cannot prove by itself.

That is also a lesson about perception: clarity is not completeness. A picture can guide attention while hiding exactly the uncertainty that matters.
