Imagine asking someone for directions. You listen, think about the route and answer. Halfway through the conversation the person you are speaking to is replaced by someone else. Would you notice? The answer feels obvious—until you look at an actual experiment.

In a 1998 study by psychologists Daniel Simons and Daniel Levin, an experimenter stopped pedestrians for directions and was covertly replaced during the interaction. In the first experiment, only about half detected the switch. That is a finding about those participants and that experimental arrangement, not a claim that half of everyone will miss every change.

## A missing detail is not a missing mind

This is called change blindness. We usually experience the world as a continuous, detailed scene, yet some substantial changes pass unnoticed when the visual sequence is interrupted. Attention is selective. Being confident that we looked at something does not establish that we registered every detail.

The researchers also reported differences linked to social-group membership in their setting. That observation needs to be described with the same care as the headline result: it is not a warrant to stereotype people, infer anyone's motives, or turn a particular study into a universal rule.

## Don't confuse two different experiments

In 1999, Simons and Christopher Chabris published work on inattentional blindness: missing an unexpected event while engaged in a demanding visual task. Change blindness and inattentional blindness are related, but they are not interchangeable. One concerns detecting a change across views; the other concerns noticing an unexpected event while attention is occupied.

Both findings offer a useful editorial discipline for historical research. A witness may report accurately what drew their attention and still lack information about what happened outside it. We should neither dismiss the witness nor treat a confident recollection as a complete recording. Compare the account with photographs, documents, independent testimony and the timing of events.

## A two-minute field exercise

Before describing the next surprising thing you see, write two columns. In the first, list only what you observed—positions, words, movements, the sequence you actually saw. In the second, write what you concluded. Then mark anything that would require another source to verify. The exercise doesn't diagnose perception or prove that any memory is wrong; it makes the difference between evidence and interpretation visible.
