Subject: Behind the door, the stranger changed
Proposed preheader (Jetpack delivery unverified): What a 1998 street experiment really showed about noticing a person swap.

A pedestrian was answering a stranger's request for directions when two workers carrying a door passed between them. Behind the interruption, the original questioner was replaced by another person. The conversation resumed. In one of the experiments, only about half of the pedestrians noticed the substitution.

It sounds like a practical joke. It was a field experiment published in 1998 by psychologists Daniel Simons and Daniel Levin, and its point was more serious than the surprise. How much of the person in front of us do we actually retain while we're busy understanding what they want?

THE OBSERVATION IS NOT THE EXPLANATION

The paper tested change detection during a real interaction, not merely while volunteers watched photographs. The findings also varied with social-group relationships, so 'half' is not a universal estimate of what any passerby will notice in any situation. The study does not prove that memory is useless or that eyewitnesses are always wrong. It establishes something narrower and more useful: even a change to the person we are speaking to can go unnoticed under particular conditions.

A later experiment by Simons and Christopher Chabris explored a related but distinct phenomenon: inattentional blindness while people were occupied with a monitoring task. Change blindness and inattentional blindness belong to the same conversation about selective attention, but they are not interchangeable. We should not flatten two different studies into one viral anecdote.

AN EVIDENCE HABIT WORTH BORROWING

Try a two-column note the next time you investigate an image, a surprising claim or a remembered conversation. In the left column, write only what you could directly observe or independently check. In the right, write your interpretation. If you cannot put a source beside a detail, mark it as uncertain rather than erasing it.

That habit is at the heart of Final Vestige's historical work. Archives are full of sincere witnesses, missing context and interpretations that outgrew their evidence. The same standards we use on an old photograph belong in the study of perception.

Our published Change Blindness investigation explains the experiments, their limitations and a simple demonstration you can explore without supplying an email address.
