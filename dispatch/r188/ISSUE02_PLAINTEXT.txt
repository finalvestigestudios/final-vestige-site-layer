Subject: The broken glass nobody saw
Preheader: A 1974 experiment shows how one word can enter the record of an event.

A crash happens once. The account of it can shift with the questions asked afterward. That makes the wording of an interview part of the record, not merely a neutral container for a witness’s answers.

In 1974, Elizabeth Loftus and John Palmer reported two controlled experiments involving films of automobile accidents. In the first, people given questions using the word “smashed” offered higher average speed estimates than people asked with less forceful verbs. In the second experiment, people asked about cars that “smashed” were more likely to report broken glass a week later, although the film contained none.

The finding is about reports under those experimental conditions. It does not prove that every eyewitness is mistaken, that all memory is easily altered, or that a short film recreates the pressure of a real investigation.

Here is a useful habit for the next archival record you read: preserve the exact question beside the answer. Begin with open descriptions before introducing a theory. Note when a remembered detail first appears, and whether another person's phrasing came before it.

This is one reason Final Vestige distinguishes testimony from inference. Our investigation of change blindness takes up a related but different question: why can an obvious visual change go unnoticed in the moment? Failure to notice and a later change in recollection are not the same effect; each can be studied with carefully designed comparisons.

No new product to buy, no claim of perfect recall. Just one more question for the source file: who chose the words?
