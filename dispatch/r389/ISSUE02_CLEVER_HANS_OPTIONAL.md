**Subject:** The horse answered. The questioner gave it away.

**Proposed preheader (not verified as native Jetpack preheader):** Berlin, 1904: a remarkable animal, an invisible cue, and an experiment that changed the question.

**Editorial type:** historical evidence and perception; zero product promotion; one editorial CTA.

---

Imagine standing in a Berlin courtyard in 1904. A horse is answering arithmetic questions by tapping his hoof. The spectators aren't merely watching a trick: the answer seems to arrive even when his owner isn't the one asking.

That horse was Hans. The 1904 inquiry found no evidence that his handler was deliberately cheating. That result was important — and incomplete. It ruled out one explanation without proving that Hans could calculate.

Psychologist Oskar Pfungst changed the experiment, rather than arguing with the witnesses. In one condition the questioner knew the answer; in another, the questioner did not. He also tested whether Hans could see the person asking. Performance deteriorated when the questioner lacked the answer or Hans could not see that person.

The clues turned out to be small, largely unintentional changes in the questioner's posture and expression as the taps approached the expected number. Hans was attending very closely — just not to arithmetic in the way audiences believed.

That's the more interesting finding. Honest observers can help produce a result without realizing they're doing it. The test isn't whether somebody seems sincere. It's whether the evidence survives when a source of expectation is removed.

**Try the test yourself (as an observation exercise, not evidence of a psychological effect):** ask someone to describe an unfamiliar image while you keep your reaction neutral. Then repeat with a different image and an observer who doesn't know what you expect to hear. Record what changes, and do not mistake this two-person exercise for a scientific trial.

The same habit matters when reading the history of mesmerism. Eighteenth-century observers described experiences sincerely; Franklin's investigators asked what remained when expectation and suggestion were separated from the proposed magnetic force.

One source can be honest and still leave the explanation open. A better experiment changes the conditions, not the story.
