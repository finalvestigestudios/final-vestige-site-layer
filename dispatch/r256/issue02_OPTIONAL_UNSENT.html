When an invisible treatment wasn't there

A man was blindfolded and told that an extraordinary force was being applied to him. He reported heat. But the procedure he believed was occurring had not actually taken place. The feeling was real to its witness. What produced it was another question.

Paris, 1784. Followers of Franz Anton Mesmer described an invisible magnetic fluid running through living bodies. Patients reported warmth, discomfort and sometimes dramatic crises in treatment rooms. An experience can be vivid without its explanation being right. How would anyone distinguish one from the other?

The French king appointed investigators, including Benjamin Franklin and Antoine Lavoisier, to examine animal magnetism as practiced by Charles Deslon. The target was Deslon's claimed agent—not every practice that would later be called hypnosis, and certainly not a modern meditation recording.

The commission altered what people knew about the intervention. At some points participants believed magnetization had occurred when it had not. At others, a procedure took place without their knowing. Blindfolding reduced clues. In the contemporary report, a participant described striking warmth while believing he was being magnetized even though the reported operation had not occurred. The commissioners also described participants who felt no corresponding effect during purported treatment.

That pairing is more interesting than a convenient verdict. The report did not establish the proposed magnetic fluid. Its authors considered imagination and expectation central to many reported sensations. But the conclusion did not make the witnesses liars. It asked investigators to distinguish a sensation from the explanation attached to it.

A second document, from the same year, belongs on another shelf. The Marquis de Puységur, an artillery officer, described what became known as magnetic somnambulism. His 1784 accounts are important to the later history of suggestion, but the Franklin commission was testing Deslon. Joining these separate archives into a single experiment would be an error.

Here is a three-line method worth keeping: write down what someone directly experienced; write the proposed cause on a different line; then ask what observation would separate that cause from an alternative. You can respect the person reporting an experience while scrutinizing the theory offered to explain it.

Our published investigation follows the actual experiments, their historical setting and the limits of what the commission could conclude. It is one of the clearest reminders that a strong story and a strong test are not the same thing.
