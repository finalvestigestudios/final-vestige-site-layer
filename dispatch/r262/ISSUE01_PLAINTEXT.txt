SUBJECT: The person changed. The witness kept talking.
PREHEADER: A 1998 street experiment asks what we mean when we say we saw someone.
STATUS: UNSENT / OPTIONAL NOT APPROVED

A person stops you on the street to ask for directions. You start answering. Two workers carrying a door pass directly between you. For a moment you cannot see the questioner. When the door clears, a different person is standing there. Would you notice?

That is the central arrangement of a real field experiment reported by Daniel Simons and Daniel Levin in 1998. An experimenter began a conversation with a pedestrian and was covertly replaced while a door interrupted the view. In the first experiment, only about half of the people noticed the change. The paper also explored how social grouping affected detection. That result belongs to the studied setting; it does not tell us that half of all people miss every identity change.

Here is the unsettling part: the pedestrian was not staring at a screen, and the person who changed was part of the conversation. Attention can support fluent interaction without preserving an accurate inventory of every detail. Earlier scene experiments by Rensink, O’Regan and Clark demonstrated that even substantial changes in alternating images can go unnoticed when the visual signal of the change is interrupted.

We investigate documents for a similar reason. An authentic photograph can establish that a scene existed, but it does not automatically establish the explanation someone later attached to it. A witness can report an experience sincerely while missing a relevant alteration. Neither observation nor memory is worthless; both benefit from a second source.

Try a small source check today. Choose one photograph or archival caption. Write down what the image alone actually establishes. Then write down the interpretation the caption asks you to accept. Mark the words that require an additional document. That gap is where investigation begins.

Our published change-blindness investigation traces the experiment and its limits without treating a quick visual trick as a diagnosis or a measure of intelligence. The story is about the gap between a record and the confident feeling of having noticed everything.
