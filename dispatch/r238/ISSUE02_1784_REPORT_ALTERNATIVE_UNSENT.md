**Subject:** The test that separated a sensation from its cause
**Preheader:** In 1784, investigators asked what happens when the subject doesn't know what's being done.

---

A person says they feel heat. Does that tell you what caused it?

In Paris in 1784, physicians and scientists investigating animal magnetism were faced with a problem like this. People reported strong sensations during treatments. The sensations were real as *reports*. But the proposed explanation — an invisible magnetic fluid directed by a practitioner — was a different kind of claim.

The royal commissioners, including Benjamin Franklin and Antoine Lavoisier, examined demonstrations associated with Charles Deslon (also written d'Eslon), a practitioner of Mesmer's system. One set of tests involved blindfolding participants. In the commissioners' account, some subjects reported sensations when they believed a procedure was occurring even though no magnetic procedure was being performed. At other moments, a supposed operation produced little or nothing when the subject was unaware of it.

The point wasn't that the people had invented their experiences. It was that **a reported experience and the explanation attached to it are not the same piece of evidence**.

The original French inquiry was conducted in **1784**. A surviving English translation was printed in **1785**. That one-year difference is a publication detail, not a second experiment.

There is another distinction worth preserving. The commissioners investigated Deslon's practice. Their report **was not a controlled test of the Marquis de Puységur's separate accounts**. Puységur's 1784 descriptions of what later historians call magnetic somnambulism form a related but distinct strand of the documentary record. We should neither treat his accounts as proof of an undiscovered force nor dismiss them as if the Franklin commission had directly tested each episode.

What does this change about an extraordinary story?

It gives us a useful three-column method:

**Observed or reported:** what someone said, did, recorded or measured.

**Inferred cause:** the explanation offered for that observation.

**Missing test:** what would distinguish that explanation from an alternative?

Try it on an interesting claim you encounter this week. If the first column is vivid but the third is empty, curiosity is warranted. Certainty isn't.
