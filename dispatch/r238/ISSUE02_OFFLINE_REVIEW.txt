Subject: The test that separated a sensation from its cause
Preheader: In 1784, investigators asked what happens when the subject doesn't know what's being done.

A person says they feel heat. Does that tell you what caused it?

In Paris in 1784, a royal commission involving Benjamin Franklin and Antoine Lavoisier examined animal-magnetism demonstrations associated with Charles Deslon, a practitioner of Mesmer's system. Blindfolded participants sometimes reported sensations when they believed a procedure was being performed, even though it was not; supposed operations could have little apparent effect when the participant did not know they were occurring.

A sensation reported by a person is not automatically evidence of the cause proposed for it.

The inquiry was conducted in 1784. The linked English translation was printed in 1785. The commission investigated Deslon, NOT the Marquis de Puységur's separate accounts of magnetic somnambulism. Puységur's writings are historical sources, not proof that an invisible force was present.

THREE COLUMNS FOR AN EXTRAORDINARY CLAIM

1. OBSERVED OR REPORTED: What was seen, stated or measured?
2. INFERRED CAUSE: What explanation was proposed?
3. MISSING TEST: What would distinguish it from an alternative?

Try that method on one surprising claim this week. If the evidence for the first column is strong but the third is missing, curiosity is warranted; certainty is not.
