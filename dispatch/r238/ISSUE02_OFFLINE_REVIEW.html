The test that separated a sensation from its cause

A person says they feel heat. Does that tell you what caused it?

In Paris in 1784, a royal commission including Benjamin Franklin and Antoine Lavoisier examined animal-magnetism demonstrations associated with Charles Deslon, a practitioner of Mesmer's system. Their report describes blindfolded subjects who sometimes felt sensations when they believed a magnetic procedure was underway, even though it wasn't.

A person's reported experience matters. It does not, by itself, identify what produced that experience. The original inquiry took place in 1784; the linked English translation appeared in 1785.

An important boundary: the commissioners tested Deslon's practice, not the Marquis de Puységur's separate accounts of magnetic somnambulism. Those writings require their own historical reading, not retroactive claims of validation or disproof.

Three columns for an astonishing claim

Reported: What was witnessed, written or measured?
Inferred: What cause did the observer propose?
Untested: What comparison would distinguish it from a rival explanation?

If the first column is vivid and the third is blank, the right response isn't cynicism. It's a better question.
