Subject: What a careful observer can still miss
Preheader: A 1999 attention experiment changes how we read a confident account of what happened.

Suppose you have one job: count the passes made by a particular group of players. You watch closely. You do what you were asked to do.

Now ask a second question. Did something unexpected cross the scene?

That was the question behind research by Daniel Simons and Christopher Chabris, published in 1999. People watched moving players while doing a monitoring task. Some observers failed to report an unexpected person moving through the scene. The probability of detection varied with the attended task and the similarity of the unexpected event to other events. The paper does not establish one universal rate at which everyone misses a gorilla.

It is easy to turn this into a sweeping verdict that witnesses cannot be trusted. That conclusion goes beyond the study. It explored attention under particular experimental conditions. It did not show that all eyewitnesses are unreliable, that any specific historical witness overlooked an event, or that a particular attention-training program solves the problem.

The useful historical question is narrower: a sincere account of what someone noticed is not necessarily a complete inventory of what was present. A witness saying nothing unusual happened may be reporting faithfully on the things they attended to, while leaving other possible events unresolved.

So before treating a silence in a record as proof that nothing occurred, try three questions. First, what was the observer trying to do? Second, what might lie outside that assigned task? Third, what independent record could check the inference without assuming anyone was dishonest?

None of this licenses us to invent events the record does not support. It simply tells us to distinguish absence of testimony from testimony of absence. The more confidently a single source is offered as the whole picture, the more useful that distinction becomes.

Our published investigation into change blindness looks at a related but distinct phenomenon: people failing to detect substantial changes between views. It is an excellent next place to examine the evidence while keeping the terms separate.
