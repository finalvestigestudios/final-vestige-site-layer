Your focus log isn't a verdict

Proposed preheader, not verified as native Jetpack functionality: Seven days of notes can reveal a pattern. Here is how to test what it means.

A notebook can look more conclusive than the week it describes.

Maybe Tuesday felt unusually productive. Maybe Thursday was lost to interruptions. By Sunday it is tempting to write a verdict beside the seven entries: it worked, or it didn't. But a record can tell you what happened without telling you why.

In 1997, psychologists Ronald Rensink, J. Kevin O'Regan and James Clark alternated original and modified photographs with short blank intervals in between. Even large changes were surprisingly difficult to spot. A cue about a change helped observers identify it sooner. The point is narrow but consequential: observation is selective.

Your seven-day attention log asks a different question. It may tell you when you practiced, what interrupted you and when it was easier to begin. That's useful information. But it isn't a controlled comparison. Sleep, deadlines, a quieter room or chance may also have changed the outcome.

TRY A THREE-COLUMN AUDIT

WHAT I RECORDED: Write one concrete observation. For example, I completed a twelve-minute routine on four days. Avoid labeling an entire week 'good' or 'bad.'

WHAT ELSE MIGHT EXPLAIN IT: Note differences in workload, sleep, distractions or even how you decided that the day counted as successful. You don't need a theory for every difference.

WHAT I COULD CHECK NEXT: On another week, record the same simple indicators at the same time. Look for a pattern that repeats, not a dramatic breakthrough. The diary is neither a clinical test nor proof that a product works.

A second study, published by Daniel Simons and Daniel Levin in 1998, moved the change-detection question into a real conversation. A person asking directions was sometimes replaced without the participant noticing. This work did not test meditation products. It shows how even a confident recollection may leave something out.

Final Vestige was built around the distance between an account and its explanation. We want readers to return to the source, look for the missing page and ask what else could explain the evidence.
