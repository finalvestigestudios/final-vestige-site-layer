**Subject:** The person who changed mid-conversation

**Preheader:** A 1998 street experiment shows how much a witness can miss—and what it does not prove.

**Editorial / noncommercial** — only one featured CTA; commercial Issue 03/05 positions unchanged.

In 1998, two psychologists described a strange interruption in an everyday conversation. A researcher asked a pedestrian for directions. Two workers carrying a door passed between them. Behind that door, the person asking directions was replaced. In the first experiment, about half the pedestrians noticed the switch.

The finding does not mean every eyewitness is wrong half the time. It describes a specific experimental situation, with its own participants and social conditions. It shows that an important change can sometimes go unnoticed even while someone is engaged with the person in front of them.

For anyone reading old records, that is a useful warning without being a verdict. A witness account establishes what the witness reported. It does not independently establish every detail that a later historian might infer from the scene. Compare a recollection with contemporaneous letters, photographs and other accounts; keep disagreements visible.

Try making three columns for a difficult claim: OBSERVED, RECALLED, INFERRED. Put each sentence in its proper column. If the source does not tell you which column fits, mark it uncertain instead of choosing the neatest conclusion.

Our published investigation examines change blindness and the limits of what the experiments show.
