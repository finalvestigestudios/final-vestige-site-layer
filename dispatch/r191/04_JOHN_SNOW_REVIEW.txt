Subject: The map wasn't the discovery
Preheader: In 1854 Soho, John Snow followed the water—and then checked the exceptions.

A map can look so convincing that the argument behind it disappears.

The familiar version of London's 1854 cholera outbreak is wonderfully simple: Dr. John Snow plotted deaths around a pump, removed its handle, and solved the mystery. The surviving record is more interesting—and more useful.

Snow already suspected drinking water before the famous map appeared in print. During the Soho outbreak, he investigated who drank from the Broad Street pump and who didn't. He traced an unusual case beyond the neighborhood: a woman who preferred water brought from Broad Street even though she lived elsewhere. He also acknowledged people who drank that water without becoming seriously ill. A real investigation has to keep those inconvenient details.

The 1855 map gave visible shape to a wider argument: interviews, locations, water sources, and comparisons. It did not, by itself, prove an entire disease theory or guarantee that a single intervention ended the outbreak. The historical evidence is stronger when the story is less tidy.

The same method works on smaller claims. When a product promises sharper concentration, a pleasant recording or one productive morning cannot tell you what caused the change. Write down the starting point, the conditions, and the exceptions. Compare before you conclude. That's how our Focus Fieldbook treats an experiment of one: a useful observation, not a clinical trial or a guarantee.
