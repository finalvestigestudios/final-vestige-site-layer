Apollo 1: the failure wasn't just a spark

An evidence-first investigation, not a product recommendation.

A fire needs oxygen, something to burn, and a source of ignition. In January 1967, the Apollo program believed it had controlled the third part well enough to treat a ground test as relatively safe. That assumption mattered as much as the hardware.

On January 27, Virgil “Gus” Grissom, Edward White and Roger Chaffee were inside the Apollo command module during a launch-pad test. The rocket carried no propellant for this test. The cabin, however, held 100 percent oxygen at 16.7 pounds per square inch absolute. Combustible materials were distributed through the spacecraft.

NASA's investigation later described a momentary power failure at 23:30:55 GMT and physical evidence of electrical arcing. Its formal finding was more careful than the familiar shorthand: investigators did not conclusively identify a single ignition source. They considered an electrical arc the most probable initiator. Probable is not proven.

An interim review placed the crew's fire report at approximately 6:31:04 p.m. Eastern and cabin rupture around 6:31:19 p.m. These are milestones in the observed escalation—not an exact clock for ignition or a measured interval to each astronaut's death. The fire spread rapidly in pressurized oxygen.

The innermost hatch opened inward. Rising cabin pressure prevented the crew from opening it. The board also found the test's hazard had not been recognized adequately and emergency provisions were inadequate.

The finding with the longest reach was organizational: a test believed to be routine had become dangerous before anyone recognized the chain of conditions. Changing one door alone would not have corrected the system.
