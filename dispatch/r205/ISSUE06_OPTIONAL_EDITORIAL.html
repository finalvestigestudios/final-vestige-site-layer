The stranger who changed in front of them

On a university campus, a researcher stopped a pedestrian to ask for directions. In the middle of the exchange, two workers carried a door between them. Behind that brief obstruction, a different person took the researcher's place.

In Daniel Simons and Daniel Levin’s 1998 field experiment, only about half of the pedestrians noticed the substitution. The surprise wasn’t that the participants were careless. It was that a plausible, uninterrupted conversation could continue while an important visual detail changed.

That result concerns visual change detection in one experimental situation. It does not show that half of everything you see is wrong, that attention is always failing, or that a recording can repair your concentration. Those would be different claims requiring different evidence.

Now take a look at your own seven-day focus log. The tempting summary is a verdict: “That worked,” or “I failed.” The useful summary is a record.

For each day, write down three things: whether you began the task, what interrupted you, and what helped you return. If you missed a day, record the interruption instead of making up a zero or treating the entire week as wasted. Separate what happened from what you think it means.

An honest log does not prove you improved compared with an untreated control group. It can, however, tell you whether the small practice fit into your week—and which obstacles deserve a different plan. This is a reflection exercise, not treatment advice or a promise of cognitive improvement.
