# THE DISPATCH — HISTORICAL EDITORIAL CANDIDATE, ISSUE 04

**STATUS: UNSENT REVIEW ONLY; NOT APPROVED AS A REPLACEMENT FOR MASTER #175**

**Subject:** Twelve sailors. Six remedies. One stubborn question.

**Preheader:** An eighteenth-century sea trial shows why comparison matters more than confidence.

**Kind:** editorial, no affiliate offer

---

On HMS Salisbury in May 1747, surgeon James Lind had twelve men suffering from scurvy and a collection of remedies that sounded plausible to people at the time.

He gave the patients a broadly common diet and compared six treatments in pairs: among them cider, vinegar, seawater, and citrus fruit. Lind later reported a striking improvement in the two men given oranges and lemons during the six days the fruit supply lasted. The experiment appeared in his 1753 Treatise of the Scurvy.

It would be tempting to call that one trial the moment medicine became scientific. The surviving account is more complicated, and more interesting. There were only two patients per treatment. Lind did not document random assignment. He even noted that the men assigned seawater were particularly ill. The result was important, but it was not a modern randomized trial and did not by itself explain the underlying cause of scurvy.

What changed was the quality of the question. Instead of asking which remedy sounded convincing, Lind compared what happened under different treatments. The answer was visible enough to matter, and the limitations were visible enough to report.

That discipline belongs in our own evaluation of modern promises. A short routine can be convenient. A recorded program can be easy to replay. Neither fact proves that any particular commercial audio product improves concentration, replaces a longer practice, or has a specific health effect. Those are separate claims requiring evidence about the actual product.

The distance between an observation and an explanation is also the story of an investigation we published this month. In 1784, Franklin and other commissioners examined reports about animal magnetism by changing what observers expected to happen. Their methods and limits deserve the same close attention as their conclusions.

Before accepting your next confident claim, try a simple question: compared with what? It does not settle every dispute. It does make the evidence harder to skip.
