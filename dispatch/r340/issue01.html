The person changed. The conversation did not.

A 1998 campus experiment and the gap between seeing and noticing.

On a university campus, a stranger asked a passerby for directions. Halfway through the conversation, two workers carried a door between them. For a moment, the questioner disappeared from view.

Behind the door, another person took his place. The passerby could now be speaking to someone else without the exchange itself breaking down.

The experiment appeared in a 1998 paper by Daniel Simons and Daniel Levin. In one real-world experiment, only about half of the pedestrians detected that the person asking directions had changed. That finding is not that every witness missed everything. It is narrower, and stranger: a substantial change to an attended person sometimes went unnoticed during an ordinary interaction.

Think about the last time a photograph or written report seemed clear on first reading. Did you actually remember every detail, or did the overall story feel coherent? Those are different things.

This is why Final Vestige treats the surviving trace as the beginning of an investigation, not its end. A witness statement tells us what someone reported. A photograph records what entered the frame. Neither is a complete inventory of everything that happened.

Try a modest experiment of your own. Before looking again at an image you saw earlier today, write down three details you are sure it contained. Check the image. Note what you remembered, what you inferred and what you cannot verify. Do not treat any mistake as a measure of intelligence or honesty. The point is to compare an impression with a record.

We have published a free account of the classic change-blindness experiments, including an original on-page flicker demonstration and the studies behind it. The demonstration illustrates an effect; it is not a clinical or personal ability test.

The question worth keeping is not, 'Can I trust my eyes?' It is, 'Which part of my conclusion can I actually check?'
