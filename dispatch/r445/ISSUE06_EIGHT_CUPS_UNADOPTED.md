**Subject:** Eight cups. One useful kind of doubt.

A cup of tea is a strange place to begin an investigation into attention. But in 1935, statistician R. A. Fisher used an ordinary tea-making claim to explain something that matters well beyond the kitchen: a confident impression is not yet a well-designed test.

## What Fisher actually described

A woman says she can tell whether milk or tea was poured first. Fisher's published example sets out eight cups, four prepared each way. The cups are presented in random order. The taster knows there are four of each and must assign each cup to one group.

There are 70 possible ways to assign four cups to one group. Under Fisher's proposed guessing model, a perfect classification occurs by chance in one of those 70 arrangements. That calculation is a property of the *specific test design*, not a universal probability that anyone can taste a difference.

The distinction matters. Fisher's book establishes how to *plan and interpret* this kind of test. Later historical accounts differ about what happened at an actual tea gathering and exactly how successful the purported taster was. We should not report the legendary outcome as a verified experimental result.

The point was not tea. It was to decide what would count as evidence **before** seeing a flattering result.

## The trap in seven good or bad days

Now imagine you spent a week trying a twelve-minute attention routine. Some mornings felt sharp. Others were full of interruptions. You may be tempted to declare that the routine transformed your concentration—or failed completely.

But what else changed? The difficulty of the work? Your sleep? A quiet room? How often you actually started? If the only observation was "I felt focused," you have an impression worth recording, not evidence that the routine *caused* it.

A one-person seven-day log cannot establish a clinically meaningful benefit, isolate placebo or expectation effects, or validate the claims of an audio product. It can still be useful for making the next decision less arbitrary.

## Make your next observation more honest

Before your next session, write down one modest prediction: "I expect to complete a twelve-minute practice on four of the next seven mornings." Then keep three columns:

**Prediction:** What did you expect before beginning?

**Observed:** Did you start, for how long, and what interrupted you?

**Other explanations:** What else might account for a change in how the session felt?

Keep missed days in the notebook; don't erase them. After seven days, report adherence separately from impressions about attention. If you repeat the process, use the same basic definitions instead of moving the goalposts. This is a practical reflection exercise, **not** a controlled study or a test of intelligence.
