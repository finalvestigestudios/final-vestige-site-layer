**Subject:** The tree that did nothing

**Proposed preheader (not a verified Jetpack inbox preheader):** In 1784, a blindfolded test changed the question about animal magnetism.

**Category:** Editorial, non-commercial. **Only primary CTA:** https://finalvestige.com/2026/11/02/mesmerism/

---

In June 1784, Benjamin Franklin’s garden near Paris became the setting for a strange test. A practitioner of animal magnetism had treated an apricot tree. A boy, described as sensitive to the supposed force, was blindfolded and led toward other trees.

The boy reported sensations as he moved through the garden. He became distressed at a tree that had not received the treatment. If an invisible fluid was doing the work, the commissioners had expected a different result.

The experiment was not a parlour trick. It was an attempt to separate a claim about the world from the expectation of the person experiencing it.

That spring, Louis XVI had appointed physicians and members of the Academy of Sciences, including Franklin and Antoine Lavoisier, to investigate the animal magnetism practised by Charles Deslon, an associate of Franz Mesmer. They watched public treatments, but the elaborate surroundings and powerful reactions made the proposed cause difficult to isolate.

So the investigators changed the conditions. Sometimes people believed they were being magnetized when they were not. Sometimes the supposed treatment was applied without their knowledge. Under those tests, the reported sensations and crises tracked expectation more convincingly than the invisible fluid.

The commission’s report, signed in August 1784, did not establish the proposed magnetic agent. But its evidence did not mean that subjects felt nothing, or that all unusual experiences were deliberate fraud. A reported sensation and its claimed cause are different propositions.

This distinction matters beyond eighteenth-century medicine. If an audio program promises to transform your focus, the first question is what the program actually contains. The second is whether a demonstrated effect is specific to the product, not merely to expectation, routine or someone else’s research.

We are keeping the historical investigation separate from product recommendations. The report belongs to a particular controversy: Deslon’s animal-magnetism practice. It did not test modern clinical hypnosis, Zen12, or Hypnosis Live, and it cannot be used to prove or disprove those products.

A source is useful not because it delivers a simple ending, but because it shows exactly how people tried to learn what was true.
