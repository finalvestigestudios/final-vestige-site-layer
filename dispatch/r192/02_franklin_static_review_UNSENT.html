The blindfold changed the evidence

In 1784, investigators tested a force people were sure they could feel.

In an eighteenth-century Paris room, a woman reported heat and pain as an investigator moved his hands near her. To the people present, the sensations were real. But did they reveal the invisible force he claimed to be directing?

A royal commission that included Benjamin Franklin and Antoine Lavoisier was examining what practitioners called animal magnetism. Its report, issued in French in 1784 and translated into English in 1785, described a revealing change to the test: the woman was blindfolded. Her reported sensations no longer corresponded to where the practitioner directed the supposed influence.

Then the commissioners separated expectation from the claimed intervention more sharply. They led her to believe the procedure was taking place while it was not. She nevertheless reported sensations. Later, when the practitioner actually directed his efforts at her without her knowing, the reported effects did not follow.

The commission interpreted these comparisons as evidence that expectation and suggestion explained the observed responses better than a newly discovered magnetic fluid. That is a conclusion about the claimed mechanism, not an allegation that people were lying about what they felt.

Their experiments were not designed like a modern preregistered clinical trial; the report was also written by participants defending their own conclusions. Still, the central question remains wonderfully useful: What changes when someone no longer knows which result to expect?

Try carrying that question into any confident claim—from an old photograph to a modern meditation audio advertisement. Ask what was observed, what was inferred, and what comparison could distinguish them. A vivid experience deserves an explanation that can survive a fair test.
