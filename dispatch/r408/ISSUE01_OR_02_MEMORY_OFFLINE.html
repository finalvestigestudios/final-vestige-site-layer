The one word that changed the witness

Two experiments in 1974 show why the question can become part of the evidence.

There was no broken glass in the film.

That detail matters more than the crash.

In 1974, psychologists Elizabeth Loftus and John Palmer published two experiments about what happens when people describe an event after seeing it. Their material was not an old manuscript or a disputed ship's log. It was short film of automobile accidents, shown to participants who were later asked to say what they remembered.

In the first experiment, the key detail was a verb. Some participants were asked how fast the vehicles were traveling when they smashed into each other. Others received a question using a less forceful word, such as hit or contacted. Their speed estimates differed systematically across the word choices.

The film had not changed. The question had.

In a second experiment, the researchers returned to the participants a week later. They asked about broken glass, which the film did not show. People who had initially been asked about vehicles that smashed into each other were more likely to report seeing broken glass than those who had received the hit wording.

This is striking, but it isn't permission to dismiss every eyewitness account. A difference in averages is not a guarantee that any particular person's memory will be distorted. The experiments do not show that witnesses deliberately fabricate evidence, or that all historical testimony becomes useless the moment someone asks a leading question.

They show something more precise, and in some ways more troublesome: a record of what someone said they saw may contain a trace of how the question was asked.

That is a problem historians know well. A newspaper interviewer, an investigator, an editor, and a later biographer can each change the wording around an account, even when none of them sets out to deceive. By the time two versions of a story are printed, the differences may reflect different sources, different questions, or different moments in time—not necessarily a single person lying.

Here is a small method you can use the next time a dramatic quote appears in your feed.

First, find the earliest available version of the account and write down its exact words. Next, note who asked the question and how it was phrased, if that survives. Then compare the later retelling. Mark what the witness originally claimed, what the interviewer or writer supplied, and what subsequent sources inferred. If the question itself is missing, mark that absence instead of filling it with an imagined conversation.

You do not have to decide in advance that the witness was right or wrong. The point is to preserve the distance between the event, the recollection, and the published record.
