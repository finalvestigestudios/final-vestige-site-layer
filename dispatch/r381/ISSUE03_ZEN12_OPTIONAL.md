## Twelve minutes is a question, not a promise

In 1890, William James opened his chapter on attention with a puzzle: how do we keep our minds on a thought when other things demand our notice? He distinguished the attention that arrives without effort from the attention we deliberately renew. He was describing a problem, not selling a stopwatch.

More than a century later, the problem is familiar. You sit down to work, open a document, and notice three other things you could do first. Twelve minutes suddenly seems like a large commitment.

A short session can be worth trying. It cannot, by itself, demonstrate that a particular audio program changes your brain, makes you productive, or replaces a longer practice. The NIH's National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health reports that studies of meditation and mindfulness vary widely in methods and quality; results can be difficult to compare and may be overstated. That is evidence about broad kinds of practice, **not an independent test of Zen12**.

Here is a free comparison you can run *before* considering any purchase. For three sessions, choose a specific, modest task, turn off nonessential alerts, set a twelve-minute timer, and note two things afterward: what you actually finished, and how often you had to bring yourself back. Repeat on comparable tasks if you want a useful baseline. Do not treat the result as a clinical measure or a scientific trial. It is a practical decision aid.

Zen12's own sales page describes a downloadable series using brainwave audio, with multiple levels and MP3 tracks. Those are **seller descriptions**. Its claims about compressing longer meditation into twelve minutes, or reliably producing focus and relaxation, have **not** been independently demonstrated for this product in our review. Nor should James's nineteenth-century observation be retrofitted into proof of a modern recording.

If you eventually compare a paid audio with your free baseline, ask: What exact file am I purchasing? How long is each session? Is there an easy nonpurchase alternative? What written refund conditions apply to the checkout I actually see? What would count as improvement—and what would simply be a good day?
