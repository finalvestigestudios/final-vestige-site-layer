What Apollo 1's fourteen seconds actually measure

On January 27, 1967, Virgil Grissom, Edward White and Roger Chaffee were inside the Apollo 1 command module for a ground test. A fire broke out. Within moments, the cabin became impossible to escape.

Fourteen seconds is a useful number here—but only if we explain what it measures.

In a February 3, 1967 memorandum to NASA Administrator James Webb, Deputy Administrator Robert Seamans recorded the first indication of fire at 6:31:03 p.m. and cabin rupture at approximately 6:31:17 p.m. That is fourteen seconds from first detection to rupture—not the measured duration of the fire, a precise ignition time, or a time of death.

A separate NASA historical overview describes the last crew communication as ending seventeen seconds after the first indication. The two sources mark different events. One number cannot quietly stand in for the other.

The cause was not simply a neat story about a spark. The test involved a pure-oxygen cabin atmosphere, flammable materials, electrical hazards and an inward-opening hatch that could not be opened quickly in the conditions that developed. The exact initial ignition source was not conclusively identified in the early official investigation.

Subsequent changes to spacecraft materials, test procedures and hatch design are part of the evidence, too. They show which hazards the investigation could establish even where the initiating circuit remained uncertain.

The same habit matters whenever we examine a witness account: say what was observed, what is inferred and what the record cannot settle.
