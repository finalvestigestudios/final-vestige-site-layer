A recovered notebook can be more useful than its author's grandest conclusion. It tells you what was written down at the time—not merely how the story felt afterward.

The same distinction matters when we ask whether a focus routine helped us.

Perhaps you tried the twelve-minute starting routine in the **Seven Day Focus Fieldbook**. Perhaps you downloaded the book and life intervened. Neither outcome deserves a victory speech or a guilty one.

Here's a way to look at a week without turning it into a verdict. On one page, make three columns: **attempted**, **observed**, and **unknown**.

In **attempted**, note the task and the small step you meant to take. In **observed**, write whether you started, how long you actually worked, and what interrupted you. In **unknown**, write what you cannot fairly conclude: whether a good day came from the routine, a deadline, less noise, or luck.

That last column is especially valuable. An entry like “I started twice in four days; I don't know why Thursday was easier” is a better record than “This technique changed my brain.”

There is a larger research literature on tracking progress. In a 2016 meta-analysis, Harkin and colleagues reviewed **138 randomized studies involving 19,951 participants**. Interventions that increased progress monitoring were associated, on average, with improved goal attainment. That finding does **not** test this Fieldbook, promise a personal result, or prove that one audio product works. [Read the study abstract and citation](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26479070/).

If you have no entries yet, start with one. If your notebook is already full, look for a pattern worth checking again. No purchase and no perfect streak required.
