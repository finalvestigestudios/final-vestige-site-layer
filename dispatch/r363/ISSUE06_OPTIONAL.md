The seventh page of a notebook can feel like a verdict. Seven tidy entries might look like progress; three unfinished entries can look like evidence that you failed. Neither judgment follows from the marks on a page.

A log can tell you what you wrote down. It cannot, by itself, establish that your concentration improved, that a meditation recording worked, or that a difficult task would have gone differently without the routine. That is a useful limit, not a reason to stop observing.

## The stranger behind the door

In a 1998 field experiment, Daniel Simons and Daniel Levin interrupted pedestrians who were giving directions. Workers carrying a door passed between the pedestrian and the person who had asked for help; the original questioner quietly switched places with another person. In the first small group, seven of fifteen pedestrians reported noticing the change. This was a study of change detection in a specific situation, not a measurement of anyone's intelligence or an argument that people cannot trust their senses.

The study is a reminder that the memory of a continuous, attentive experience is not the same as a complete record. It also shows why a surprising percentage from a small experiment deserves context rather than a universal claim.

## A twelve-minute evidence check

Take your next twelve-minute work session and record only three things: what you intended to do; what interrupted or redirected you; and what you actually finished or observed. If you prefer, repeat this twice on different days and compare the entries. Do not change everything at once and then attribute the result to a single technique.

Ask which parts of the record you can verify. Did the timer run? Is there a draft, a page read, a decision documented? Which impressions are just impressions? Your notes may help you choose a workable next step. They are not a clinical assessment, a controlled experiment, or proof of a product's advertised outcome.
