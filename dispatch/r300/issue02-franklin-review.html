What happened at the fourth tree?

A blindfolded experiment in 1784 tested what a witness could know.

In 1784, a boy was led blindfolded among trees in Benjamin Franklin's orchard outside Paris. One tree had received a supposed magnetic treatment. The boy did not know which one.

The commissioners' report says that he embraced four other trees, all untreated. His symptoms grew. At the fourth, he fainted. The tree that had been treated stood some distance away.

That is a striking scene. It is also a useful test of a claim, rather than proof that the boy was pretending. The commissioners were investigating whether an invisible magnetic fluid caused the reactions that mesmerists reported.

What mattered was not simply that an effect was described. It was whether the effect followed the alleged treatment when a person could not tell that treatment from its absence.

The 1784 commission examined the animal magnetism practised by Charles Deslon and conducted trials with another practitioner. Its report describes responses when subjects believed they were receiving treatment but were not, and absent responses when treatment was attempted without their knowledge.

The commission concluded that the proposed fluid had not been demonstrated. That conclusion did not establish that the participants felt nothing, that they were dishonest, or that every longer-term claim of cure had been investigated. A short investigation of a particular mechanism has boundaries.

The record offers a method worth keeping. First, find the original claim. Next, ask what a witness could actually know. Then ask which rival explanation the test can distinguish—and which questions it leaves open.

Those are also the questions we will use when considering modern attention and audio-product claims. A seller can describe an appealing experience. The description alone does not establish that its promised outcomes occur.
