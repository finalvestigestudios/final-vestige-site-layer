A clock can show twelve minutes. It cannot show you what those minutes accomplished.

The free Seven Day Focus Fieldbook asks you to pick something you've been putting off, shrink it to a startable task, and notice what happened. Its twelve minutes are a **work-starting exercise**. A meditation audio may also last twelve minutes, but it asks a different thing of you.

It's easy to let a number connect two ideas that ought to be judged separately. This is an old problem in historical research, too: a neat detail gives a story a satisfying shape, and suddenly the detail is doing more work than the evidence can support.

Before considering a recording, decide **what you actually want**. Are you trying to begin a piece of work? Would you rather take time to sit, listen, and practice attending? Or do you want a way to unwind? These aims overlap in ordinary language, but success with one doesn't establish success with another.

Here's a useful twelve-minute comparison you can run **without buying anything**:

1. **Choose the question first.** For a work-starting question, write down one specific step, then attempt it for twelve minutes. For a meditation-practice question, sit comfortably, attend to your breathing or ordinary sounds, and note when you wander and return.
2. **Write down only observations.** Record whether you completed the planned time and which interruptions you noticed. An intention to work is not completed work; a feeling of calm is not a measured change in brainwaves.
3. **Do not force an interpretation.** One day of self-observation cannot establish that an audio program caused an effect. If the exercise isn't useful to you, you can stop.
