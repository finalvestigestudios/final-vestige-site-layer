Subject: When a surgeon gave a mystery a new name
Proposed preheader (NOT verified inbox rendering): James Braid's 1843 book changed the vocabulary—not the standard of proof.

In 1843, a surgeon put a strange new word on the title page of a book: Neurypnology. James Braid used that book to offer an account of nervous sleep in relation to animal magnetism. Changing a name, however, was not the same as establishing a cause.

Decades before, a commission including Benjamin Franklin and Antoine Lavoisier had tested the proposed invisible magnetic fluid. It disputed the mechanism—not the fact that people reported sensations. Braid's book shows a debate still in motion; it is not independent evidence for any modern commercial audio's promised effects.

When reading an extraordinary claim, write three lines:
1. The event — what did someone actually observe?
2. The explanation — what did someone claim produced it?
3. The test — what would distinguish that cause from an alternative?

The earlier inquiry deserves a closer look because of what the commissioners tested, and what they could not establish.
