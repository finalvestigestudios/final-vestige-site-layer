When a surgeon gave a mystery a new name

Proposed editorial preheader: James Braid's 1843 book changed the vocabulary—not the standard of proof. Whether this appears in a real inbox is UNVERIFIED.

In 1843, a surgeon put a strange new word on the title page of a book: Neurypnology. Beneath it, James Braid offered an explanation of what he called nervous sleep, considered in relation to animal magnetism.

A new name didn't mean the mystery had been settled. People could report powerful experiences without knowing what produced them. The earlier animal-magnetism controversy made that boundary impossible to ignore.

In 1784, a royal commission including Franklin and Lavoisier tested the alleged magnetic fluid. Its work challenged the proposed mechanism, not the fact that people could feel something. Braid's later book is a historical trace of the changing conversation—not proof that any modern commercial audio has the effects it advertises.

Three lines to put beside an extraordinary claim

  1. What actually happened?
  2. What explanation was offered?
  3. What test would distinguish that explanation from an alternative?

Our investigation follows the 1784 tests and their limits. The evidence is more valuable than a tidy verdict.
