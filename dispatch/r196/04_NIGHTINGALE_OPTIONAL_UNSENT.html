Two circles. Thousands of deaths. One hard question.

In 1858, Florence Nightingale put two circles beside each other. The striking part was not the battlefield red. It was the blue.

Her Diagram of the Causes of Mortality in the Army in the East compared monthly British military death rates during two periods of the Crimean War. Blue represented diseases classified as preventable or mitigable; red represented wounds; black represented other causes. The blue sections dwarfed the red ones during the earlier period. That is a sobering way to encounter a war.

But there is a detail easily missed by anyone treating the picture as an ordinary pie chart: each colored area was meant to represent a rate, not a simple slice of a fixed whole. To read the argument properly, you must look at the legend, the army population estimates and the changing months—not just the flower-like silhouette.

Nightingale's chart helped make disease mortality impossible to ignore. It did not, by itself, prove which single reform caused the later decline. Army conditions, reporting practices, the changing population at risk and several public-health measures complicate any one-cause story. A memorable graphic is not the entire investigation.

Here is a small field exercise. Open a chart in a news report. Before reading the headline, identify its denominator, time period and unit. Then write two sentences: what the chart directly shows, and what you would still need to check before accepting the explanation attached to it. You have not solved the whole question, but you have separated observation from inference.

That distinction matters whenever we use a map, photograph, memory or experiment to reconstruct the past. The Piri Reis map is another example: an extraordinary historical object does not license every modern claim made about it.
