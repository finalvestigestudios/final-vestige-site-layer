## Before you buy a focus recording, ask this

In 1784, a French commission that included Benjamin Franklin investigated claims of animal magnetism. People described sensations they attributed to an invisible magnetic fluid. The commissioners asked a better question: were those sensations evidence for the proposed *cause*, or could the effects occur without it?

By varying what subjects knew and experienced, the investigators challenged the magnetic-fluid explanation. They did not have modern instruments, and they were not conducting a trial of present-day hypnosis. What makes the record useful is the method: a sincere experience does not automatically establish the story we tell about its cause.

That distinction matters before you buy a modern recording meant to change how you concentrate.

Consider *Total Focus*, a Hypnosis Live audio product. The official product page describes a **33-minute, 47-MB MP3** narrated by J. A. Amos. It also makes dramatic claims about productivity and concentration. Those descriptions are the **seller's promises**, not independent proof that this specific recording achieves them.

Scientific work on hypnosis does exist. For example, a 2025 study investigated hypnotic induction, suggestion, and attentional bias in a controlled experimental context. That result cannot be transferred wholesale to a specific commercial download, or to whether it will improve your work or study sessions. The same evidence-first standard applies whether the subject is a historical phenomenon or a new audio product.

Before spending anything, try a small exercise: pick one ordinary task, set a reasonable focus window, and note the minutes actually spent on it plus interruptions. Repeat on three separate days without a new recording. You now have a baseline. If you later try any new routine, the log can help you compare experiences. It does not prove cause and effect—sleep, expectation, workload, habit and motivation all vary—but it replaces a vague feeling with something you can inspect.

**Five questions worth asking before any purchase:**

1. What exactly is delivered—an MP3, ongoing access, or a bundle? Are those details written on the actual checkout page?
2. Are the strongest outcome claims supported by evidence on *that recording*, rather than hypnosis or suggestion in general?
3. What are the applicable terms? At inspection, the seller's FAQ described refund requests within fourteen days, limited to five refunded sessions per year. Verify the terms at your own purchase path.
4. What can I test for free first?
5. Is the recommending publisher compensated when a reader buys?
