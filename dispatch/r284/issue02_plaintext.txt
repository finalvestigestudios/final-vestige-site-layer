THE DISPATCH — ISSUE 02 (UNSENT REVIEW SPECIMEN)
Subject: The boy, the trees, and the missing force
Preheader: In Franklin's garden, one carefully staged test exposed a gap between sensation and cause.

In 1784, a boy was led into Benjamin Franklin's garden near Paris. His eyes were covered. The commissioners wanted to know whether he could detect a force that one of the trees was said to contain.

The magnetiser Charles Deslon had treated an apricot tree. According to the commissioners' report, the boy was then guided to four different, untreated trees. He embraced each one. His reactions grew stronger. At the fourth, he fainted—still some distance from the tree the magnetiser had treated.

The event was real enough to the people watching. But the explanation on offer was now in trouble.

This was part of a royal inquiry into animal magnetism, a supposed invisible fluid that practitioners claimed could affect the body. Franklin, Lavoisier, Bailly and their colleagues weren't conducting a modern clinical trial. They were asking a narrower, ingenious question: would the alleged effect still track the supposed cause when the subject couldn't see what was being done?

The report also describes occasions when people reacted without receiving the claimed treatment, and failed to react when they were treated without knowing it. The commissioners concluded that their tests furnished no proof of the proposed magnetic fluid. That was a verdict on this theory and these demonstrations. It wasn't a test of every later practice that would be called hypnosis, nor proof that the boy was pretending.

There's a useful habit in that distinction. When a surprising experience has several possible explanations, don't ask only whether something happened. Ask what would happen if the proposed cause were absent.

That question belongs in the archive. And it belongs wherever a confident modern sales page turns a subjective experience into a promise of measurable results.
