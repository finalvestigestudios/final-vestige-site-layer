The horse that knew when to stop

In Berlin, a horse appeared to do arithmetic. Clever Hans answered questions by tapping a hoof. His owner, Wilhelm von Osten, believed the animal was calculating.

Psychologist Oskar Pfungst asked a better question: What happens when the questioner does not know the answer?

In number-card trials, Pfungst reported 98% correct responses when the questioner knew the answer and 8% when the answer was hidden from that person. Hans's performance also depended on seeing the questioner.

The explanation was not necessarily conscious deception. Pfungst found that small, unintended cues could tell Hans when to stop tapping. The numbers describe those historical tests, not a universal law about perception.

The question persists in our examination of Mesmerism: what happened, what was inferred, and what changed when investigators controlled expectations?
