**Subject:** The horse that knew when to stop

**Proposed preheader:** In 1904, the crucial answer was not inside the horse. It was with the questioner.

**Type:** editorial — historical investigation; NOT an affiliate promotion

---

In Berlin, a horse appeared to do arithmetic.

Clever Hans answered questions by tapping a hoof. His owner, Wilhelm von Osten, believed the animal was calculating. Visitors watched apparently correct answers accumulate, and the spectacle drew scientific attention.

A more revealing question was not *Can Hans count?* It was *What changes when the person asking does not know the answer?*

Psychologist Oskar Pfungst tested that distinction. In one set of number-card trials, he reported 98% correct answers when the questioner knew the number and 8% when the answer was concealed from the questioner. Additional tests showed the importance of whether Hans could see the person asking.

The results did not require anyone to be deliberately cheating. Pfungst's explanation was subtler: the horse had learned to respond to small, unintended changes in a person's posture or expression, stopping when the questioner's tension eased.

This is a story about evidence that appeared convincing **until the experiment changed one hidden condition**. The numbers come from Pfungst's original report, not a modern randomized trial; they describe the conditions he actually tested. Hans was responding to something real. It simply was not arithmetic in the sense his admirers believed.

The useful question for the rest of us is not whether people are gullible. It is whether a result survives when the person conducting the test no longer knows what outcome to expect.

That is the same question we bring to eighteenth-century accounts of animal magnetism: which sensations were documented, which explanations were inferred, and what happened when investigators controlled what a subject could see or anticipate?
