Subject: The horse that knew when to stop
Preheader: In 1904, the crucial answer was not inside the horse. It was with the questioner.

In Berlin, a horse appeared to do arithmetic.

Clever Hans answered questions by tapping a hoof. His owner, Wilhelm von Osten, believed the animal was calculating. Visitors watched apparently correct answers accumulate, and the spectacle drew scientific attention.

A more revealing question was not "Can Hans count?" It was "What changes when the person asking does not know the answer?"

Psychologist Oskar Pfungst tested that distinction. In number-card trials, he reported 98% correct answers when the questioner knew the number and 8% when the answer was concealed from the questioner. Additional tests showed the importance of whether Hans could see the person asking.

The results did not require deliberate cheating. Pfungst argued that the horse responded to small unintended changes in the questioner's posture or expression. Hans was responding to something real—but not doing the arithmetic his admirers imagined.

The figures are from Pfungst's original account and describe his test conditions, not a modern randomized trial. The question to carry forward is whether a result survives when the investigator no longer knows what to expect.

That is also how we read eighteenth-century animal-magnetism claims: what was reported, what was inferred, and what changed under controlled conditions?
