The drawing stayed still. The animal changed.

An 1892 cartoon, an 1899 psychologist, and a small test of how attention shapes what we see.

Two animals. One drawing. Neither one moves.

A familiar duck-rabbit drawing can be read in two ways. The lines do not move; our interpretation does. A version appeared in the German humor magazine Fliegende Blätter in 1892. Psychologist Joseph Jastrow discussed it in his 1899 essay “The Mind's Eye.” He did not invent the drawing.

That difference matters in the archive. A photograph records something before a lens; a witness statement records somebody's report. Either may leave the explanation unresolved.

Two-minute practice: Find a responsibly sourced ambiguous drawing. Write your first interpretation, look for another and note what in the marks changed (perhaps nothing). If you cannot switch your reading, that is a useful observation too.

This exercise cannot establish that every witness is wrong or that any commercial audio program works. It is a small lesson in separating observation from interpretation.
