The man who answered while asleep

Puységur's 1784 account is a remarkable document—and an imperfect experiment.
In May 1784, a French nobleman described a young farmworker who appeared to fall asleep in his presence—and then began answering questions.
That account has a strange afterlife. The nobleman, the Marquis de Puységur, believed he was observing the effects of animal magnetism. The young man, identified in later historical writing as Victor Race, reportedly spoke while in an altered state and later remembered little or nothing of the episode.
Notice the crucial word: reportedly. We do not have a recording of the encounter. We have Puységur's written version, preserved in a work whose original volumes are catalogued as 1784–1785. One National Library of Medicine copy is listed with an apparent 1784 imprint corrected by its catalogue to 1785. That bibliographic wrinkle matters when someone tries to make the story more precise than the surviving sources allow.
It is tempting to turn the episode into a verdict: an early discovery of hypnosis, proof of a magnetic force, or proof that everybody involved was deceived. None follows automatically from the page. It is better evidence for what Puységur claimed and believed than for the physical explanation he proposed.
That distinction was not lost on everyone in his century. A royal commission that included Benjamin Franklin investigated claims about animal magnetism in 1784. Its report—available in a 1785 English translation—examined how expectation and imagination could shape reported effects. It did not directly test this particular farmworker, so it cannot be used as a simple verdict on the Puységur case either.
Here is a habit worth borrowing from the archive. Take a striking claim and sort it into three columns: what a person says happened; what surviving material independently establishes; and what explanation has been added afterward. Sometimes all three columns are full. Often the second is surprisingly thin.
This is why Final Vestige follows primary sources even when the story is irresistible. The surviving account is interesting precisely because its limits are visible. The unknowns are part of the history, not an inconvenience to edit out.
