The mind is not a camera. Here's the evidence.

Proposed preheader: A 1997 experiment shows why large scene changes can escape attention.

Picture two photographs of the same room. In the first, a chair stands beside a window. In the second, the chair is missing. You might expect the difference to jump out at once. But what if the two pictures keep alternating, with a brief blank screen between them?

In a 1997 study, Ronald Rensink, J. Kevin O'Regan, and James Clark investigated a version of precisely this problem. People could struggle to identify even large, repeatedly presented changes when a blank interval interrupted the transition. Pointing their attention toward a relevant part of the scene made finding a change faster.

That result does not mean our eyes stopped working. It suggests a distinction between looking at a scene and attending to the detail that changes. The paper is about visual change detection under particular experimental conditions; it is not a verdict that every photograph or witness is untrustworthy.

A different experiment makes the boundary clearer. In 1999, Daniel Simons and Christopher Chabris studied how observers watching a dynamic event could overlook an unexpected object while occupied with another task. Inattentional blindness and change blindness are related limits of attention, not interchangeable demonstrations.

Here's a small field note you can try without pretending to conduct a laboratory study. Look around an ordinary room for ten seconds. Turn away and write down three details you would confidently include in a description. Mark one detail you're less sure about. Then turn back and verify. The important observation isn't whether you scored three out of three; it's where confidence and the record diverge.

The same distinction guides our historical investigations. An archival photograph can show what fell within its frame; it cannot tell us everything happening outside it. A witness can sincerely report an experience without establishing every explanation attached to it. Rather than dismiss the witness or believe every inference, we ask: what was actually observed, how was it tested, and what remains unknown?
