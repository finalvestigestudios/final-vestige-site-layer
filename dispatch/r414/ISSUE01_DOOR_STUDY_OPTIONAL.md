**Proposed subject:** The stranger behind the door

**Proposed preheader, not verified as a native Jetpack field:** A 1998 experiment, eight missed substitutions, and one question every investigator should ask.

**Type:** Editorial (4 editorial / 2 commercial pending remains unchanged)

---

A stranger stops you on a campus and asks for directions. You look at the map, decide which street to suggest, and begin explaining the route. Then two workers carrying a door pass between you. For a second you cannot see the person who asked the question.

When the door passes, someone else is holding the map.

Would you notice?

In 1998, psychologists Daniel Simons and Daniel Levin put a version of that question into the real world. In their first experiment, an experimenter approached pedestrians for directions. During the conversation, workers carried a door between the two people; the original questioner was secretly replaced by another person. Seven of fifteen pedestrians reported noticing the switch. Eight did not.

That small result is memorable, but it is easy to abuse. It does not establish that half of all people will miss a stranger changing places. There were only fifteen participants in that first experiment, at a particular location, in a particular kind of interaction. The researchers conducted a second, related experiment; its conditions and results were different. The work did, however, challenge a reassuring assumption: paying attention to a conversation does not guarantee that we record the identity of the person in front of us.

This is why Final Vestige returns to documents, dates and original evidence. An eyewitness may be sincere and still fail to register a detail. A dramatic retelling can make that detail seem impossible to miss. Neither the intensity of the memory nor the smoothness of the story tells us exactly what was encoded at the time.

**Try a three-question evidence check** when a striking video or witness account crosses your screen:

1. What was visible continuously, and what was hidden by a cut, obstruction or delay?
2. Which detail did the witness have a reason to track at that moment?
3. What outside record could confirm the part that recollection alone cannot settle?

Those questions are not a reason to dismiss witnesses. They are a way to give testimony the careful reading it deserves.
