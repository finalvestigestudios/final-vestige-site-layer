In 1917, two cousins walked down to a stream in Yorkshire with a camera. What came back looked like photographic proof that fairies were real.

The camera was real. So were the girls, the stream, and the photographs. The fairies were another matter.

## What the camera actually recorded

Elsie Wright and Frances Griffiths made the first two Cottingley photographs in 1917 with a quarter-plate Midg camera belonging to Elsie's father. The Science Museum Group still catalogs that camera. A second camera, a Cameo, was used for later photographs in 1920.

That distinction is surprisingly important. No one had to invent an imaginary photographic process. The images were photographs of actual arrangements placed in front of actual lenses. Authentic photographic material was perfectly compatible with a staged scene.

## How a story acquired authority

The pictures circulated beyond Cottingley and attracted the attention of Arthur Conan Doyle. In the Christmas 1920 issue of The Strand Magazine, the author of Sherlock Holmes discussed the photographs in connection with fairy beliefs. The photographs traveled with a much larger story: what could be concealed in the natural world, what witnesses might know, and what a camera could settle.

The photographs were examined and discussed by people who took them seriously. But an expert's inability to establish exactly how an image was staged is not proof that the extraordinary explanation is correct. The question should have been: what other account can explain the objects visible in the frame?

## What the later record changed

In 1983, Elsie wrote to photographic researcher Geoffrey Crawley. The National Science and Media Museum preserves her letter and describes the use of paper cutouts, based on illustrations, held in place with hatpins. The University of Leeds archive includes photographic materials and a copy of her confession letter.

One disagreement should stay in the record. Elsie said all five photographs were staged; Frances continued to insist that the final photograph was genuine. The surviving accounts are not identical, even though the broader paper-cutout explanation for the famous photographs is well documented.

This is why our standard isn't simply to ask whether a source is real. It is to ask exactly what that source proves. The original negatives, the cameras, the later correspondence and the contradictory recollections carry different kinds of information.

## A two-minute evidence exercise

When a photograph makes an extraordinary claim, write down three separate propositions: (1) the image is an authentic photograph, (2) the scene was not arranged or manipulated, and (3) the interpretation attached to it is correct. Evidence for the first does not automatically establish the second or third.

Then ask what independent observation would have changed the conclusion. A photographer witnessed throughout the setup? Identifiable source material? A contemporaneous record that could be cross-checked? The strongest investigation looks outside the frame.
