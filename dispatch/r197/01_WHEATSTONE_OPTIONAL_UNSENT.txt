THE DISPATCH — OPTIONAL EDITORIAL 01 — REVIEW ONLY / UNSENT
Subject: Two drawings. One depth neither contained.
Preheader: In 1838, Wheatstone built a machine to test how sight assembles a scene.

In June 1838, Charles Wheatstone put a strange device before the Royal Society. Its most important pieces were not engines, lenses or elaborate pictures. They were two simple drawings, made from slightly different viewpoints.

Each drawing showed a flat arrangement of lines. Each eye received one of the two drawings, redirected by mirrors. The observer could perceive a solid form where neither drawing, taken alone, contained one. Wheatstone called the device a stereoscope.

That was more than a clever party trick. It gave an investigator a way to separate a physical input from the experience assembled from it. The drawings could be measured. Their differences could be adjusted. What changed was the viewer's perception of depth.

Think about that distinction the next time a photograph or confident eyewitness account seems to settle an argument. The incoming information matters enormously, but the experienced picture is not a perfect copy of a single input. Our visual system uses more than one cue; stereoscopic disparity is one important source of depth, not an explanation for every visual judgment.

A one-minute observation: Hold one finger about a forearm's length away and look past it at a distant object. Close your left eye, then your right. Your finger appears to shift against the background. You have not moved it. You have sampled two viewpoints. This demonstration illustrates a difference between viewpoints; it does not recreate Wheatstone's stereoscope or measure the strength of your depth perception.

Wheatstone's original paper was read in 1838, before photographic stereoscopy became a familiar form of entertainment. The point wasn't that sight is inherently deceitful. It was that a vivid visual result can be investigated with a device, drawings and a careful question: what information produced this impression?

At Final Vestige, that is the principle behind studying evidence. We look for the part we can inspect, and we don't pretend it exhausts the whole story.
