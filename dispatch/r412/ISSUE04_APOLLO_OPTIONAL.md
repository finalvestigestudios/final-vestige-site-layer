**Subject:** Apollo 1: the spark the record could not name

**Proposed preheader:** NASA traced the fire's spread without identifying one exact ignition point.

At 23:30:54.8 GMT on January 27, 1967, a voltage transient appeared in the records of an Apollo spacecraft on its launch pad. Ten seconds later, the crew's first audible report of fire began. Those two pieces of evidence sit close together on the official timeline. They are not proof that the voltage transient was the spark.

That difference is where the story begins.

Apollo 1 was a ground test, not a launch. Virgil Grissom, Edward White and Roger Chaffee were inside the command module. Their work was supposed to help prepare for the first crewed Apollo flight. Instead, a fire swept through a cabin pressurized with pure oxygen.

The Apollo 204 Review Board reconstructed the spread of the fire and identified conditions that made it catastrophic: combustible material close to possible ignition sources, the high-pressure oxygen atmosphere, wiring hazards and a hatch that could not be opened against rising internal pressure. The board judged an electrical arc the most probable initiator, but it could not conclusively identify a single ignition source.

There is also a trap in the numbers. A February 3 preliminary statement described the cabin rupture as occurring fourteen seconds after the fire was first detected. A later NASA chronology records the crew's initial report at 23:31:04.7 GMT and the rupture at 23:31:19.4 GMT—about 14.7 seconds. These are different documentary snapshots, not a reliable stopwatch for when the fire actually ignited. The first visible or audible sign was not necessarily the beginning.

The hatch took far longer to open. The final investigation documented the difficulties of reaching and removing the hatches amid heat and smoke. The inability to escape was not a detail separate from the fire; it was part of the system the investigators had to explain.

A one-sentence account—*a spark caused the Apollo 1 fire*—sounds decisive. The record is more useful: it identifies a probable initiator, a set of documented lethal conditions, and design decisions that were changed afterward. A careful explanation can admit a missing first cause and still explain an immense amount.

The point of The Dispatch is not to make uncertainty mysterious. It is to show exactly where the records stop and what they establish before that boundary.
