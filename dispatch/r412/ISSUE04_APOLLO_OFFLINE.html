Apollo 1: the spark the record could not name

NASA traced the fire's spread without identifying one exact ignition point. Proposed preheader, not a verified Jetpack control.

At 23:30:54.8 GMT on January 27, 1967, a voltage transient appeared in the records of an Apollo spacecraft on its launch pad. Ten seconds later, the crew's first audible report of fire began. Their proximity does not prove that the transient was the spark.

Apollo 1 was a ground test, not a launch. Virgil Grissom, Edward White and Roger Chaffee were inside the command module. A fire swept through its pressurized pure-oxygen atmosphere.

The Apollo 204 Review Board found combustible material near possible ignition sources, dangerous test conditions and an inward-opening hatch that could not be opened against rising internal pressure. An electrical arc was the probable initiator; no single ignition source was conclusively identified.

Even the attractive “fourteen seconds” headline needs context. NASA's February 3 preliminary statement used that interval from first detection to cabin rupture. A later timeline lists the initial crew report at 23:31:04.7 GMT and rupture at 23:31:19.4 GMT: approximately 14.7 seconds. Neither timestamp tells us exactly when ignition began.

The investigators could describe why the conditions were lethal and what needed to change without claiming to know the precise first spark. That is the value of a careful historical record.
