THE DISPATCH · ISSUE 05

Imagine finding a painted fifteenth-century card with symbols that people now associate with secrets, fate and mystical knowledge. It would be tempting to assume those meanings had always been there.

The surviving record is more interesting. As the Metropolitan Museum and the V&A explain, early European tarot was a card game, associated with fifteenth-century Italian courts. Later writers connected tarot with divination and esoteric systems; those interpretations belong to a different historical period.

The cards themselves became objects with layered histories: materials, patrons, printing, iconography, changing rules and changing beliefs. A museum catalog can tell you about a card's date and provenance without proving any supernatural property.

That is what attracts us to tarot as an artifact. The historical evolution of its symbols is worth examining even if we suspend judgment about claims of predictive power.

The V&A has an excellent historical overview at https://www.vam.ac.uk/articles/tarot-cards/ . Our own Library collects the same sort of documentary trails across maps, objects and human experience.

Next week we'll ask a question that belongs at the door of every museum—and every private collection: what would it mean to hold a piece of history, and how could you know its story was genuine?
