THE DISPATCH · ISSUE 07

Imagine two witnesses. They agree on almost everything. Same place. Same hour. Same ordinary sequence of events.

Except for one detail.

One says the door was already open. The other says it was locked when they arrived. Both seem convincing. Your job is not to choose the better storyteller. Your job is to find what the record can independently establish.

Here's the method we've been practicing across The Dispatch. First, write down what each witness actually said. Second, separate the statements from the conclusions attached to them. Third, look for a dated record, physical trace or independent observation. Fourth, write the strongest claim the available evidence supports—and one claim it does not.

Try the same exercise with our Apollo 1 Evidence Atlas. The published chronology contains recorded markers, but the intervals between them are not invented sensor measurements. It is an exercise in resisting a tempting explanation when the evidence has limits.

A real historical investigation is not a game. We owe the people and events involved a disciplined reading of their record.

Fictional cases can offer a different opportunity: a place to practice comparing clues without mistaking entertainment for history.
