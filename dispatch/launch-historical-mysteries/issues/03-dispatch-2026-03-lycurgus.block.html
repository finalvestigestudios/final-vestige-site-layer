THE DISPATCH · ISSUE 03

Hold the Roman Lycurgus Cup in one light and it looks greenish. Change the way light passes through it and the vessel appears red. The contrast is striking enough to feel like a trick.

Yet the effect is physical. Research on the glass points to tiny metal particles interacting with light. The museum object is extraordinary because it survives, not because we must invent a forgotten supernatural process behind it.

What matters in an investigation is the difference between the observation and its explanation. The color change is observed. The material analysis helps explain it. Claims about what every Roman craftsperson knowingly understood require a separate level of evidence.

One habit can make a reader better at spotting these gaps: ask what new observation would change the explanation. Then look for an independent source that offers it.

We'll use that skill next week in a very different setting—an accident investigation where the records are extensive, but the exact initiating event remains unresolved.

Next week we follow an official accident investigation where the surviving hardware and records matter more than a tidy story. Later, we'll return to a question every collector should ask: what can an object itself establish?
