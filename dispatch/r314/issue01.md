**Proposed subject:** The change you didn't see

**Proposed preheader:** An experiment in attention—and why observation deserves a second look.

A scene cuts away. It returns a moment later, almost identical. Something important has changed—but you don't notice it.

That's the unsettling part of change blindness. Noticing a difference depends partly on where attention is directed. The 2005 research review by Daniel Simons and Ronald Rensink examines why even conspicuous scene changes can escape notice. It challenges the comforting idea that we carry a complete visual record of everything around us.

Failing to detect a change doesn't mean the eyes failed. It doesn't mean that every memory is false, either. Noticing, remembering and explaining are different jobs. The certainty we feel after someone points out a change is not proof that we noticed it the first time.

Try a small exercise with no special equipment. Look around an ordinary room for ten seconds. Turn away and write down five objects you're certain you saw. Look back and check. Mark what you genuinely recalled and what you supplied from expectation. This is not a psychological diagnosis or an intelligence test. It is a reminder that confidence and coverage are not the same.

For a historical investigation, the stakes are higher. A photograph or eyewitness account can be valuable without confirming every explanation attached to it. We ask what is visible, who recorded it and what may still be missing.

Our Change Blindness investigation follows the research and why the effect matters when reviewing evidence. Next week's Dispatch turns to Puységur's eighteenth-century testimony, a separate question about suggestion and how claims survive on paper.
