## Proposed Issue 01 editorial candidate — exact one CTA, no merchant content
**Proposed subject:** The person changed. The conversation went on.  
**Proposed planning preheader, not verified as a Jetpack control:** In a 1998 field experiment, an unnoticed substitution exposed a gap in what we register.  
**Proposed custom excerpt, NOT configured in WordPress:** In a 1998 field experiment, people were asked for directions, briefly interrupted by a moving door, and sometimes missed a change of speaker. That finding is not a verdict on all eyewitnesses. Our newly published evidence guide explains the result and includes an original demonstration you can try.

A stranger asks for directions. While you're speaking, two people carry a door between you. By the time it passes, a different person stands there asking the same question.

Would you notice?

In a 1998 study, Daniel Simons and Daniel Levin arranged an encounter much like this on a college campus. About half of the pedestrians in their first experiment detected that the person seeking directions had been replaced. It was a field experiment with a particular setup, not a measurement of how often **all people** miss important changes.

The striking part is not that our senses are useless. It's that the sensation of seeing the scene can be more complete than the information we actually register. The researchers asked what happens to person perception when attention is engaged and an interruption creates an opportunity for substitution.

That poses a familiar problem for an investigator. A confident observation is a starting point, not an authenticated catalog record. A compelling story tells us where to investigate, not what the record must say.

Here's an exercise that costs nothing. Before walking into a familiar room, write down the location of five ordinary objects. Then check. Which were exactly right? Which were almost right? What did you fail to list? This is a **personal observation exercise**, not a clinical test or a scientifically comparable reproduction of the 1998 study. It's a way to notice the difference between recall and checking.

We've just published a guide to the underlying research, with an original flicker demonstration that you can explore at your own pace. Our illustration is **not a replay of the original experiment**, and finding the difference on the page cannot diagnose anything about your memory.

**One next step:** [Explore the change-blindness evidence and demo](https://finalvestige.com/2026/11/02/change-blindness/).
