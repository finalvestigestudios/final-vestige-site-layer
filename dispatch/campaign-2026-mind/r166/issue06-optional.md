**Subject:** The stranger who changed in plain sight

**Preheader (editorial intent; NOT verified inbox metadata):** A real-world experiment shows why a confident memory is not a complete record.

Someone stopped a pedestrian to ask for directions. Two people carrying a door passed between them. When the door cleared, the person asking was no longer the same person.

That was the crucial manipulation in a 1998 field experiment by psychologists Daniel Simons and Daniel Levin. In one experiment, only about half the pedestrians noticed the swap. The result does not mean half of people are generally oblivious, or that every witness is unreliable. It means that, under those specific conditions, even a change to the person in an ongoing conversation could escape notice.

Here is the uncomfortable distinction: looking is not the same thing as retaining every feature, and a vivid feeling of having seen something is not proof that you noticed the change. Historical investigations have a version of this problem. A surviving account can be sincere and still be incomplete.

If you used our Seven Day Focus Fieldbook, try one small revision to your log. Before repeating a familiar task, write down what you expect to notice. Afterward record what you actually noticed, then a plausible detail you might have missed. Keep those three columns separate. This is a reflection exercise, not a diagnostic test or evidence that a method has improved attention.

The useful outcome is not a perfect score. It is a habit of distinguishing your observation from your explanation. That is also how we approach an incomplete historical record: preserve the source, mark the uncertainty, and resist making it tidier than it is.

Our published Change Blindness investigation reviews the experiment's conditions and limits and includes an original, clearly labeled flicker demonstration. The demonstration is not the researchers' original stimulus, and trying it is not an assessment of your abilities.
