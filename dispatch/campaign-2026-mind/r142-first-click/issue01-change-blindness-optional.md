**Subject:** The picture changed. Did you see it?
**Preheader hypothesis:** Try the research-inspired demonstration and follow the original sources.
**Slot:** Editorial — optional alternative to original PR #175; no email or post authorized.

A picture disappears. The next version returns. Something obvious has changed—and for a moment you can't find it.

That's the unsettling part of change blindness. We often feel we've taken in a complete scene, even when our attention has never catalogued every detail.

In a 1997 study, Ronald Rensink, J. Kevin O'Regan and James Clark inserted brief blanks between versions of images. Participants often struggled to detect even sizable changes without the motion signal that normally makes a difference stand out. Daniel Simons and Daniel Levin later brought a related question into the street: a person asking a pedestrian for directions was replaced mid-conversation. The substitution sometimes went unnoticed.

These were particular experimental situations. They do not establish that every eyewitness is unreliable or that human perception is useless. What they do show is how much confidence can outpace observation.

That is why our investigations distinguish a record from an interpretation. An archival photograph shows what it captured, not all the events outside the frame. A recollection can be sincere without being complete. And the most interesting question is not "who was fooled?" but "what could the observer actually notice?"

We've built a clearly labeled demonstration of change blindness into one of our published investigations. It is an illustration, not a diagnostic test, and we show the supporting research.
