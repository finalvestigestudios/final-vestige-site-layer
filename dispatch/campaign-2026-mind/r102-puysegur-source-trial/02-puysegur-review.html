The failed cure that belongs in the file

What the 1784–85 books preserve—and what Franklin's commission did not test.

An eighteenth-century writer could have left the failed cure out. The Marquis de Puységur did not. In the record Final Vestige is examining, a promised change, an unreliable prediction, a patient's refusal, and the operator's own doubts all survive alongside the striking stories. That's where an investigation begins: not with the finest anecdote, but with the material that complicates it.

Puységur's Mémoires pour servir à l'histoire et à l'établissement du magnétisme animal appeared in 1784, followed by a second volume in 1785. The Bibliothèque nationale de France catalogs them as two volumes, 232 and 256 pages. These are not clinical studies in the modern sense. They are a participant's accounts of an extraordinary practice, assembled in a world with different standards of consent, proof, diagnosis and publication.

The patient, the operator, the witness

One early case concerns Victor, whose altered behavior Puységur described in the language of magnetic somnambulism. The account raises several questions at once. What did the patient say and do? Who prompted him? What could an outside witness hear or see? When does the author's interpretation enter the description? A record can preserve a person's words without proving the mechanism the author proposed.

Other cases are less tidy. Final Vestige's source-trail ledger retains Madeleine's unsuccessful cure, a prediction attributed to Louis de Rieux that did not come true, and episodes involving requests to be left alone. Those details are not incidental blemishes to edit away. They help show how the system responded when the patient's interests, the operator's confidence and events did not align.

Two investigations, often collapsed into one

In the same year as Puységur's first volume, royal commissioners including Benjamin Franklin and Antoine Lavoisier examined claims of animal magnetism as practiced by Charles Deslon in Paris. Their 1784 report asked whether the supposed magnetic agent could be distinguished from expectation and imagination under controlled circumstances. The surviving report is a different source, addressing a different setting.

That distinction is crucial. The commission was not a controlled trial of every episode described in Puységur's memoirs. Nor does the survival of Puységur's detailed papers overturn the commission's findings. The two documents can be read together as a historical contrast in how evidence was gathered; they cannot responsibly be treated as the same experiment.

A contemporary scholarly account describes Puységur's 1784 work as an important shift toward psychological interpretations of apparently extraordinary behavior. That historical significance does not make the promised cures, clairvoyance or an invisible magnetic fluid established facts. Influence on a later field and proof of an original claim are different questions.

A small method you can borrow

When an account—historical or modern—sounds impossibly persuasive, make four brief entries before deciding what happened:

  1. Claim: What exactly is alleged? Preserve the writer's wording without adopting it as fact.
  2. Observation: What could a named observer actually see, hear or measure?
  3. Source: Who recorded it, when, in what edition, and from whose perspective?
  4. Counterevidence: Which failure, alternative explanation or missing comparison matters most?

Try the method on a modern wellness advertisement: the product's format may be verifiable, a person's testimonial may be sincere, and a claim of extraordinary results may still lack an appropriate comparison. None of those categories cancels the others. Keeping them separate is what makes curiosity useful.

Why we made the archive

The Final Vestige Puységur file does not offer historical treatment advice. It separates the 1785 original-edition reference from the later scanned version used to verify page images, identifies when claims come from the operator rather than independent witnesses, and retains inconvenient outcomes. It gives the reader a source trail rather than a verdict disguised as a story.

If you've ever wondered how a compelling witness account becomes a claim of proof, this archive is the place to examine the paper trail. The surviving record is more interesting—and more human—when its limits remain visible.
