Subject: The boy who fainted at the wrong tree
Preheader: In 1784, a royal commission tested what a magnetic force was supposed to do.

A boy stood blindfolded in Benjamin Franklin's orchard in 1784. A physician had supposedly infused one apricot tree with an invisible magnetic force. But the boy was led to other trees. By the fourth untreated tree, the report says, he had fallen into a crisis and fainted.

The commissioners had arranged a simple question with sharp edges: if the treatment's physical force caused the reaction, would the reaction follow the treated tree—or the boy's expectation?

The account survives in a report commissioned by Louis XVI. Franklin worked with physicians and members of the French Academy of Sciences, including Antoine Lavoisier. Their target was the claimed 'animal magnetic' fluid as practiced by Charles Deslon, a physician associated with Franz Anton Mesmer. The commission did not conduct a comprehensive investigation of every Mesmer treatment, and it was not a trial of the later claims associated with the Marquis de Puységur.

The orchard was not their only test. In another episode, a woman previously said to respond strongly was blindfolded. She reported sensations, but they did not reliably coincide with where the operator directed the supposed magnetism. And the report describes symptoms when she believed a treatment was occurring although no corresponding treatment was given.

The commissioners concluded that the supposed fluid lacked evidence. Crucially, they did not need to accuse every participant of pretending. A reported experience can be real to the person experiencing it without proving the mechanism offered to explain it.

That's the distinction we want to preserve at Final Vestige: a witnessed event, an interpretation of the event, and an independent test of the interpretation are three different things.

Try this whenever a remarkable claim finds you. Write down the claimed cause. Ask what should happen if the claim is true. Then ask what would happen if expectation alone were enough. If both explanations predict the same result, the test hasn't separated them.

The report belongs to the eighteenth century, not to a clinical trial of modern audio products. We can't use it to promise that a contemporary meditation or hypnosis recording works—or to dismiss every modern therapeutic practice without separate evidence. What we can borrow is the discipline of testing the explanation.
