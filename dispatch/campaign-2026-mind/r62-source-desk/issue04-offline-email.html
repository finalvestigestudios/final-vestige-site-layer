The 17.1-second window

The first crew report of the Apollo 1 fire began at 23:31:04.7 GMT on January 27, 1967. The last voice transmission ended at 23:31:21.8. Those are 17.1 seconds apart. But the radio record contains only 10.3 seconds of actual transmission, divided by a 6.8-second silence.

NASA’s Apollo 204 Review Board analyzed the tape, but the final transmission was too garbled for a definitive transcript or speaker identification. Those timestamps tell us when a fire was reported—not exactly when it ignited, how it was experienced, or when an individual system failed.

Investigators documented a chain of hazards without recovering one indisputable initiating spark: a 16.7-psia pure-oxygen cabin, combustible material, an inward-opening hatch that could not be opened against rising pressure, and rescue procedures poorly suited to this pad fire.

A short timeline is emotionally powerful. It becomes more useful when we ask what it leaves out: engineering assumptions, material choice, hatch geometry, rescue planning, and uncertainty about ignition.

Try the investigator’s three columns: recorded observation, reasonable interpretation, and unresolved question. A precise timestamp in the first column cannot make the other two certain.
