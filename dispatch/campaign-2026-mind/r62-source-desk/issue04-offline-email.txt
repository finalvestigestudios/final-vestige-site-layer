SUBJECT: Seventeen seconds. A much longer explanation.
PREHEADER (planning only; not a verified Jetpack field): NASA recorded two transmissions, a silence, and more unanswered questions.

THE 17.1-SECOND WINDOW

The first crew report of the Apollo 1 fire began at 23:31:04.7 GMT on January 27, 1967. The last voice transmission ended at 23:31:21.8. Those are seventeen point one seconds apart. But the radio record contains only 10.3 seconds of actual speech, divided into two transmissions by a 6.8-second silence.

NASA's Apollo 204 Review Board analyzed the tape, but the final transmission was too garbled for a definitive transcript or speaker identification. Those timestamps tell us when a fire was reported, not when it ignited, how the astronauts experienced it, or the exact moment any particular system failed.

The investigators could document a chain of hazards without recovering one indisputable initiating spark. The cabin had been pressurized with pure oxygen at 16.7 pounds per square inch absolute. Combustible materials were present. Rising pressure made the inward-opening hatch impossible to open in time. The unfueled pad test had been treated as less hazardous, with rescue provisions ill-suited to a sudden cabin fire.

A short timeline is emotionally powerful. It becomes more useful when it points us toward the records that the timeline alone cannot contain: engineering assumptions, materials, hatch geometry, emergency procedures and the report's uncertainty about ignition.

Here is the research habit we take from it: place a time-stamped observation in one column, the investigators' interpretation in another, and the unresolved question in a third. Precision about the first column must not turn into certainty about the other two.
