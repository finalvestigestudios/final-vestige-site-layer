## Issue 01 — The door and the witness
**Working subject:** The mind is not a camera *(retain canonical)*
**Working preview only:** A person changed behind a door. What did a witness actually notice?
**Evidence:** https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03208840

**Optional 202-word replacement of the evidence transition (not the whole issue):**

The door did not conceal a stolen object. It concealed a change in the person asking the question. Once it had passed, one experimenter had quietly taken another's place.

When Daniel Simons and Daniel Levin published the study in 1998, about half the pedestrians in the first real-world experiment detected the switch. That figure is memorable. Its conditions are even more important. The researchers also reported an effect associated with whether pedestrians belonged to the experimenters' social group. The experiment therefore gives us a warning about attention, not a universal error rate to paste onto an eyewitness.

A photograph has a different problem. It captures something, but a viewer supplies a great deal of the story: where it was taken, who appears in it, what happened just outside the frame. We can be certain about the pixels and uncertain about the provenance at the same time.

Try this with any historical image: write three things you can actually point to; add two proposed interpretations; then identify the record that could distinguish between them. Do you need the negative? The neighboring frame? A building directory? A contemporaneous caption?

That difference—between the visible thing and the attractive explanation—is why an archival record deserves more than a quick glance.

## Issue 02 — The observer and the explanation
**Working subject:** Before hypnosis had a name *(retain canonical)*
**Working preview only:** A French aristocrat recorded a remarkable event. What did he prove?
**Evidence:** https://wellcomecollection.org/works/es4ayp6q ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31273811/

**Optional 202-word insertion after the opening report:**

The surviving record gives us two very different kinds of information. One is that Puységur published his account of a patient's behavior: apparently sleeping, yet speaking and responding, with no later memory according to the observer. The other is the explanation he gave it. The first is an eighteenth-century testimony. The second is an eighteenth-century theory.

If we write the scene as though a modern instrument measured a special state, we have added a certainty the record does not contain. If we dismiss the document because the proposed magnetic fluid lacks support, we risk making a different mistake: forgetting that the history of an idea can be studied without accepting the idea.

The year 1784 also produced the French royal investigation of Mesmer's animal magnetism. That inquiry matters for the theory's context; it does not function as a laboratory retest of Puységur's May encounter. Nor does a library's catalog entry verify a particular patient's cure. It tells us an edition of the work exists.

One practical way to read extraordinary claims is to divide a page into three columns: what a named witness recorded, what that witness concluded, and what independent record would be needed to test the conclusion. Leave the third column blank if the evidence is missing.

**Specific proof still needed before retaining 'May 4':** read the relevant 1784 passage/page directly, or find a scholarly source that traces that day to the original. A date repeated in summaries does not become a primary record.

## Issue 04 — Seventeen seconds of a larger timeline
**Working subject:** Seventeen seconds. A much longer explanation. *(retain canonical)*
**Working preview only:** NASA's tape has two transmissions, a silence, and no single proven spark.
**Evidence:** https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/static/history/Apollo204/invest.html

**Optional 227-word timeline clarification near the beginning:**

The recorded interval begins at 23:31:04.7 GMT and ends at 23:31:21.8. Subtract the timestamps and you get 17.1 seconds. But this wasn't seventeen uninterrupted seconds of speech. The final radio record contains two transmissions totaling 10.3 seconds, separated by 6.8 seconds without transmission.

The Review Board could identify a fire report. It could not reconstruct a flawless transcript or identify every speaker with certainty. The last transmission was especially garbled. The clocks mattered because they gave investigators anchors. The intervals between those anchors still required interpretation.

Nor can the starting timestamp be mistaken for the exact spark. The board's investigation treated an electrical arc as a possible cause but could not conclusively identify a single source of ignition. What it *could* establish was an environment capable of turning a local ignition into a catastrophe: pure oxygen under pressure, combustible material, an inward-opening inner hatch, and emergency procedures poorly suited to a cabin fire.

This is the strange strength of a rigorous accident inquiry. Investigators can know enough to demand sweeping changes without claiming to know every second of a disaster. A dramatic image of a hatch is one record. The telemetry, materials, training assumptions, and rescue constraints supply others.

The official review does not give us the comfort of one villain. It gives us something more useful: a map of interacting hazards and of the limits of what the instruments recorded.

## Issue 06 — A missing entry is data
**Working subject:** What did seven days of attention reveal? *(retain canonical)*
**Working preview only:** What a diary can tell us—and what it cannot prove.
**Evidence:** https://www.bmj.com/content/324/7347/1193

**Optional 214-word experimental-literacy insertion:**

A paper diary can look complete even when it wasn't filled out when the event happened. In a 2002 study of patients recording health symptoms, the paper entries reported 90 percent compliance. A sensor attached to the diary binder gave a different answer: only 11 percent of the entries met the scheduled time window. Widen the window to ninety minutes and that figure became 20 percent.

Those numbers belong to a particular clinical research setup. They are not evidence that ninety percent of personal journals are unreliable, and they tell us nothing directly about the benefits of meditation. Their lesson is about the difference between a record and the timing of its creation.

The practical response is not to turn a free attention exercise into a surveillance project. It is to make the notebook honest. Write the time when you know it. Write 'later from memory' when you don't. Record 'not done' instead of recreating a perfect streak after the fact.

Seven days may reveal when a routine fits into a life. They can reveal that an interruption recurs at the same hour. They cannot, without controls, establish what caused a change in concentration. A missed day isn't a character judgment. It's an observation, and an honest observation is far more useful than an invented one.
