### Four particularly consequential copy-review findings

**01 — The mind is not a camera (Simons and Levin).** The 1998 *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* abstract says only half of observed pedestrians detected the substitution of the person giving directions in the first experiment. The experiment also found that social-group relation affected detection; the original published abstract explicitly describes this limitation. The existing first paragraph is accurate, but a reader could mistake 'about half' for a stable universal estimate for eyewitness accuracy. **Editorial upgrade:** 'about half in this experimental setup' plus the observed role of social-group membership, without using the experiment to declare any particular witness mistaken. *Primary source:* Daniel J. Simons and Daniel T. Levin, 'Failure to detect changes to people during a real-world interaction' (1998), https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03208840 . Independent original publisher abstract verified 2026-11-02. This is a study of change detection, not a study of historical photographs or memory-recall precision; the archival connection is our analogy, labelled as such.

**02 — Before hypnosis had a name (Puységur).** The source-controlled issue asserts May 4, 1784 and names Victor Race at Buzancy. Those details circulate in historical accounts; an openly accessible 1784 *bibliographic catalog* cannot independently corroborate the exact day and encounter. Wellcome records the 1784 publication of Puységur's *Détail des cures*, but that catalog says nothing about what happened in the treatment room. Adam Crabtree's historical writing quotes Puységur's description of Victor's apparent sleep, speech, and later reported amnesia as **Puységur's account**, not a medically verified observation. **Editorial upgrade:** keep 'Puységur later reported' next to every disputed behavioral claim and consider 'in May 1784' unless the original date is cross-checked against a direct edition/page. Distinguish the 1784 French royal commission's investigation of Mesmer's theory from a test of Puységur's *particular* encounter. A critique of an invisible fluid is not a claim that all suggestion/hypnosis effects are fictitious; avoid either overreach. *Historical supports:* Wellcome 1784 catalog https://wellcomecollection.org/works/es4ayp6q ; Crabtree's historical discussion https://regressionjournal.org/jrt_article/hypnosis-and-the-alternate-consciousness-paradigm-adam-crabtree-is-31/ ; related scholarly history https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31273811/ . No medical claim. **FLAG: exact May 4 date is not yet independently verified against directly examined 1784 pages.** This is an editorial evidence flag, not a conclusion that the date is false.

**04 — Seventeen seconds (NASA Apollo 204).** The Apollo 204 Review Board's original accessible transcription analysis identifies **two** final S-band transmissions: 23:31:04.7–23:31:10.0 GMT and 23:31:16.8–23:31:21.8 GMT. The span between beginning of the first and end of second is **17.1 seconds**; actual recorded transmission time totals **10.3 seconds**, separated by a **6.8-second gap**. The current draft correctly frames 17.1 as a *timestamp span*, but the headline and lede could misleadingly imply 17 seconds of continuous radio traffic or the time from ignition to death. The report explicitly says transmissions reveal the initial fire **report**, not the fire's ignition instant. NASA's subsequent safety analysis describes oxygen at **16.7 psia**, combustible material, pressure, inward-opening hatch, and absence of appropriate pad rescue planning. Board investigators did not conclusively identify one ignition source; evidence for a possible electrical arc is not absolute proof. **Editorial upgrade:** put 'the 17.1-second window contained only 10.3 seconds of actual transmission' in the body and note the 6.8-second silence; do not advertise 'the fire lasted 17 seconds.' The actual timing makes the historical reconstruction more gripping, not less. *Primary source:* NASA Review Board, 'Investigation and Analysis' https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/static/history/Apollo204/invest.html ; NASA original report https://www.nasa.gov/missions/apollo/apollo-1/report-of-the-apollo-204-review-board/ . NASA's accident synthesis: https://sma.nasa.gov/sma-disciplines/accllp/apollo-1 . Do **not** claim an exact ignition source or exact moment of loss of consciousness.

**06 — What did seven days reveal? (Stone et al.).** The source-controlled 2002 BMJ report is from a specific clinical symptom-diary study and reports **90% self-reported paper diary compliance vs 11% electronically evidenced within the tight scheduled window**. A wider 90-minute window gave 20%; the electronic diary group had 94% actual compliance. This is not an experiment comparing focus methods, nor evidence that a meditation routine improves cognition. **Editorial upgrade:** mention the measurement window and clinical setting once in the copy so the counterintuitive percentage does not sound like a universal indictment of handwritten records. Encourage recording *missing days as missing*, not reconstructing precise times later. *Primary source:* Stone et al., 'Patient non-compliance with paper diaries', *BMJ* 324 (2002), https://www.bmj.com/content/324/7347/1193 . Corroborating expanded journal report: https://doi.org/10.1016/S0197-2456(02)00320-3 . The canonical issue already observes the important distinction between compliance and causal efficacy; retain it.
