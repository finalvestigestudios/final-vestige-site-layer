If you used the Seven Day Focus Fieldbook that came with your welcome message, you have already tried separating an impression from a record. There is a historical document that rewards exactly that habit: a damaged, brightly colored map made by an Ottoman navigator in 1513.

The Piri Reis chart is often presented online as an impossible object. Depending on the storyteller, it becomes evidence of an ice-free Antarctica, a lost civilization, or a secret map from Columbus. Yet the surviving fragment contains something more useful than a sensational headline. Its maker left notes about the sources he assembled.


## A map with its footnotes still attached

An inscription attributes the work to Piri Reis and dates it to 1513. Other notes describe the maps he says he used, including Portuguese charts and a chart attributed to Columbus. That is a remarkable window into how one cartographer assembled reports from different voyages. It is not the same as possessing the lost Columbus chart or verifying every coastline on the finished map.

The source claim and the physical object must remain separate. The surviving map is incomplete; its eastern portion is missing. We can read what Piri Reis claimed about his inputs, but we cannot inspect all those source maps or reconstruct the lost portion with certainty. Gregory McIntosh's scholarly study challenges the popular Antarctica reading rather than treating it as a recovered survey.


## A practical way to close an evidence log

Try writing three lines after any intriguing claim. Line one: what the object or witness directly says. Line two: what an interpreter concludes from it. Line three: what document, observation, or competing explanation would change your view. Do not erase the third line merely because the first two make a good story.

For the Piri Reis map, the first line includes the cartographer's source notes. The second includes conflicting modern explanations for the southern coast. The third includes the missing source charts and the surviving geographic evidence. That practice makes curiosity more durable, not less exciting.
