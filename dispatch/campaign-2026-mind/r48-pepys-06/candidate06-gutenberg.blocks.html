He saw the fire. Then he went back to bed.

At about three in the morning on Sunday, 2 September 1666, a servant named Jane woke Samuel Pepys. There was a fire somewhere in London. He looked from his window, judged it to be some distance away, and went back to bed.

That decision looks incredible to anyone who knows what happened next. But that's precisely the trap. We know the Great Fire became a catastrophe. Pepys, at three in the morning, did not. The entry preserves an estimate made before the scale of the event was clear.

By morning, the information had changed. Jane brought news that hundreds of houses were gone. Pepys then heard from the Lieutenant of the Tower where the blaze was said to have begun. Later, from the riverside, he saw the spreading fire for himself. Within one dated diary entry sit at least three different kinds of material: his observation, another person's report, and his own changing interpretation.

What a dated page can and cannot tell us

The National Archives reproduces extracts from Pepys's account of 2 and 4 September. A separate National Archives teaching collection reproduces entries by John Evelyn, who viewed the disaster from other places and times. Comparing the records helps identify overlap, contradiction and vantage point; it doesn't automatically tell us which witness was right about every detail.

The surviving Pepys diary is more than a modern quotation on a webpage. The Great Fire collection identifies an actual manuscript page in Pepys's shorthand, held by the Pepys Library at Magdalene College, Cambridge. Its date identifies the episode described. The displayed museum record does not, by itself, tell us the exact hour Pepys wrote each sentence. We should not turn a dated narrative into a minute-by-minute certified recording.

The same discipline matters to anyone keeping a personal log. An entry can be sincere, helpful and still mix things noticed in the moment with things learned later.

A notebook that caught its own weakness

In 2002, Arthur Stone and colleagues compared reported and observed diary timing in a study of 80 adults with chronic pain. Forty used paper diaries fitted with light sensors; forty used an electronic diary with reminders and time restrictions. In the paper group, reported compliance within the specified thirty-minute windows was 90 percent. Sensor-based compliance was 11 percent; with a more generous ninety-minute window it was 20 percent.

These numbers do not measure memory accuracy in the general population. It does not compare meditation methods or establish that electronic diaries inherently make people more truthful. It measured compliance with a particular schedule in a particular study. The researchers also disclosed relationships with an electronic-diary supplier.

The narrower lesson is useful: a notebook may record what we *intend* to have done, what we *remember* doing, or what we can actually document. Those are different claims.

Four lines, seven days

If you've been using our Focus Fieldbook, try this each day. Make the entry brief enough that you can actually keep it.

Observed: What did I do? Record the duration only if you know it, and mark a missed session as missed.

Reported: What was I told, or what am I remembering later? Give uncertain recollections their own place rather than rewriting yesterday's note.

Conditions: Was it loud, quiet, rushed, interrupted or easy to begin? An obstacle is data, not a moral failing.

Next test: Choose one modest adjustment for tomorrow. Keep your prediction separate from what eventually happened.

At week's end, ask whether your routine was feasible and what repeatedly interrupted it. A seven-day self-log is not a controlled trial showing that a specific audio program improved concentration. Sleep, workload, expectations and ordinary variation may all contribute. The NIH's evidence overview likewise does not establish an outcome for any single commercial recording.

Pepys's early estimate of a distant blaze wasn't a character flaw. It was an observation made with limited information. What makes his diary valuable is not perfect foresight, but a trace of what he thought, heard and saw as the situation changed.

A useful focus log can do the same. It needn't tell a triumphant story. It only needs to preserve enough truth that tomorrow's decisions have something real to work with.
