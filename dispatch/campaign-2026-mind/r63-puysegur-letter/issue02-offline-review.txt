FROM THE RECORD · THE DISPATCH 02

On a Tuesday evening in May 1784, a French aristocrat said he visited a sick young man and witnessed something he did not know how to explain. Almost two and a half centuries later, the striking part is not a claim about magnetic forces. It is what survives on the page—and what does not.

Armand-Marie-Jacques de Chastenet, marquis de Puységur, sent an excited letter from Buzancy. He described a twenty-three-year-old peasant who had been ill in bed, reported a sleep-like episode while the young man continued to speak, and subsequently claimed the patient did not remember his visit. These are statements from one interested observer. They are not modern measurements of a medical cure or a special brain state.

The date is less mysterious than the event

An archival uncertainty from our earlier review can now be narrowed. The letter is identified as dated 8 May 1784. In it, Puységur placed the visit on the previous Tuesday, the fourth of the month, at eight in the evening. The digitized original edition contains that first-person date statement; a specialist textual guide independently identifies the same letter and date. In this sense we can say something precise: Puységur wrote that the event happened on 4 May. We cannot turn his dated account into a witness independent of himself.

One patient, two different clocks

Before reprinting the letter, Puységur summarized the apparent change as happening after about five minutes. In the letter itself, he described an interval of half a quarter hour—roughly seven and a half minutes. Neither is a stopwatch reading. The difference might reflect rounding, recall, later editing, or something else. The source does not tell us which. A responsible historian leaves the two descriptions on the table instead of silently picking the more dramatic one.

There is a second complication to the familiar miracle narrative. A few pages later, the same writer admits that his attempts to relieve a seventeen-year-old with fever and severe head pain failed through an entire day, and that the relief he later perceived did not last. The confession does not verify his apparent successes. It does show that his own published version was not a continuous string of triumphs.

How to investigate a compelling story

Try an archival exercise. Make three columns. In the first, record what the document says: the author's date, the reported illness, the described behavior. In the second, record the author's interpretation: what he believed caused the event. In the third, write what would be needed to decide between explanations: an independent account, a medical record, a reliable time measurement. Leave a space blank rather than filling it with confidence.

This is how Final Vestige treats the extraordinary. An old claim can matter profoundly in the history of suggestion and hypnosis without the cause proposed by its author being correct. The patient, the witness, the letter, and the later theory belong to the same story—but they are not the same kind of evidence.
