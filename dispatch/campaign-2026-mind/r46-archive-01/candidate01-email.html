The stranger changed. Who noticed?

A 1998 field study and a three-pass method for reading old photographs.

The interruption lasted only as long as it took two workers to carry a door across a pedestrian's path. A stranger had been asking for directions. When the door cleared, the conversation continued — but the person asking was no longer the same person.

Psychologists Daniel Simons and Daniel Levin arranged this encounter for a 1998 study of change blindness. In one experiment, an experimenter speaking to a pedestrian was covertly replaced by another. Only half the pedestrians detected the swap. The researchers also reported that detection depended on social-group relationships in their study conditions. Their result is neither a universal eyewitness error rate nor evidence that a particular witness is unreliable.

The experiment exposes a narrow, unsettling distinction: paying attention to a conversation is not the same as storing a complete record of the scene. That is worth remembering when an old photograph arrives with a confident caption and almost no provenance.

Suppose the photograph shows a shopfront, three people and a sign you can only partly read. It is tempting to identify the place from a similar-looking street or assign a decade from the clothing. Those are hypotheses. The visible sign is an observation; the proposed town is an inference; a dated directory or original catalog card could serve as an independent check.

Archivists use this separation deliberately. The U.S. National Archives' photograph-analysis worksheet asks readers to examine what is visible, reason about context and identify other documents needed to understand the image. The Library of Congress teaches a related practice of observing, reflecting, questioning and investigating further. Neither method turns a hunch into a fact.

Here is a three-pass exercise for the next photograph that looks immediately familiar:

  1. Observation: List five details you can point to in the image, including words that are incomplete or hard to read. Avoid naming an event that is not directly pictured.
  2. Hypothesis: Write one possible date, location or identity. Mark it explicitly as a possibility and say what in the photograph suggested it.
  3. Cross-check: Identify one separate source — an original negative, accession record, directory, map or contemporaneous report — that could actually falsify your hypothesis. If you do not have it, leave the caption uncertain.

A photograph can be authentic while the caption attached to it is mistaken. A perceptual experiment does not prove that captions fail for the same psychological reason; it gives us a reason to distrust the feeling that we've taken in everything. The archival method is how we move beyond that feeling.

Our published guide, The Missing Caption, follows the trail from picture to object, catalog entry and surrounding archive. It is a method for doing better historical detective work, not a claim that every unidentified photograph can be solved.

One honest question is a better start than a neat but unsupported caption.
