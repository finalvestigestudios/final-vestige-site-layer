The witness who missed the switch

Proposed preheader: A 1998 campus experiment offers a better ending than another focus promise.

A student asks a stranger for directions. A large object passes between them. The stranger who asked the question is replaced by someone else.

Would you notice?

In a 1998 experiment, Daniel Simons and Daniel Levin arranged just such a switch during conversations with pedestrians. Only half the pedestrians in their first experiment noticed that the person speaking to them had changed. The researchers also reported differences in detection associated with social group membership. The finding wasn't that half of everyone, in any setting, will miss a swapped person. It was evidence that a substantial change can escape notice during an ordinary interaction.

That is an uncomfortable lesson for anyone who writes down what they witnessed. It is also a useful way to end our first set of Dispatch letters about attention.

If you tried the Seven Day Focus Fieldbook, perhaps the most valuable result isn't a before-and-after verdict on your brain. It's a better record of ordinary behavior. You may have noticed that you started later than planned; that a certain interruption kept returning; or that you finished a routine more often when you made it smaller. Those are observations you can revisit. They are not a controlled experiment proving a treatment or an audio product works.

Here is a small archival habit worth keeping: write down what happened, then on a separate line write down what you think it means. The first line can still be mistaken; the second can still be helpful. Keeping them apart makes both easier to inspect.
