Imagine finding a historical account in which a witness says they felt more confident after a curious demonstration. Would we report a change in confidence? Certainly, with attribution. Would we say the demonstration had *proved* a mysterious mechanism? No. The record would not allow it.

Audio products deserve that same distinction. A recording can give you a structured experience; that does not establish that its promised results will follow.

Before buying a self-hypnosis recording, I would run three checks.

**1. What are you actually buying?** The official listing identifies **Hypnosis Live — Total Focus** as a *33-minute, 47-MB recording*. This is a specific product, not the seller's different **Hypnotize Yourself** course. Don't substitute the course's promises, features, or terms for this recording.

**2. What kind of claim is on the page?** The seller advertises stronger concentration, faster work, and near-total freedom from distraction. Those are the seller's assertions. We have not independently demonstrated that this recording produces them, and we will not present 'reprogramming the brain' or a promised outcome as established scientific fact. You can reasonably evaluate its length and available sample without accepting its outcome language.

**3. What happens if it isn't useful?** Check the price actually displayed at checkout, file access, and the current refund instructions before paying. Promotional price presentations have varied; we won't promise a fixed discount. The seller advertises a 14-day refund request window, but we have not independently tested how that process operates.

Even without buying anything, a useful exercise is to write down the exact improvement you hope to notice, and what observation would convince you that you **didn't** get it. That makes your expectation testable rather than inevitable.
