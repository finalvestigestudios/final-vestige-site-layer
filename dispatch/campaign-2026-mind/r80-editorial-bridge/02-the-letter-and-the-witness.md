PROPOSED CUSTOM EXCERPT (NOT A RECEIVED EMAIL): On May 4, 1784, the Marquis de Puységur said he observed an unusual state in a young man he was treating. In a letter dated May 8, he tried to describe it. His account survives. That makes it a valuable historical source, not a verified explanation.

---

On May 4, 1784, in Buzancy, an aristocrat experimenting with animal magnetism described something he had not expected. A young man later identified by historians as Victor Race appeared to enter a quiet, responsive state rather than the crisis-like reactions associated with some of Franz Mesmer's practices.

We know this first as **Puységur's account**. The description appears in a letter dated May 8, later associated with his *Mémoires pour servir à l'histoire et à l'établissement du magnétisme animal*. That chronology matters. The alleged experience, the subsequent written report and the printing of the work are related but not identical events.

It is easy to retell the story as either the birth of a miracle or the exposure of a fraud. Neither headline is earned by the surviving document.

Puységur believed that something significant had happened. He described observations and inferred causes, shaped by the vocabulary of animal magnetism available to him. Later historians have examined the episode as a turning point in the development of practices eventually associated with hypnotism and the study of suggestion. That later importance does **not** prove that Puységur's claimed magnetic mechanism existed or that the account demonstrates a reliable therapy.

The French national library catalogs the work as a two-volume publication spanning **1784–1785**. The dating is a useful antidote to a neat but inaccurate single-year story. The letter, the editions and the later interpretation should not be collapsed into one source.

**Three questions to try on the evidence**

First: who witnessed the event directly, and who knew it through Puységur's narration?

Second: what behavior did he say he saw, and which mental, physical or supernatural explanation did he add?

Third: what independent comparison, if it had existed, would have made the explanation stronger—or exposed an alternative?

This is the same method we would use for a shipwreck log, a disaster inquiry or an archaeological report. A document can be authentic and fascinating while the explanation offered inside it remains unproven.

There is a modern echo: when any contemporary seller presents a powerful subjective experience as proof that a particular recording changes the brain, we should ask the same third question. That is a standard of evidence, not a judgment about what someone felt.
