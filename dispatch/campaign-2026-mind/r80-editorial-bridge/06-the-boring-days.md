SUBJECT: Your focus log should include the boring days
PROPOSED PREHEADER (NOT VERIFIED AS JETPACK FEATURE): A seven-day field note is useful even when nothing dramatic happens.
PROPOSED CUSTOM EXCERPT (NOT A RECEIVED EMAIL): The most useful line in an attention log may be “nothing changed.” A spectacular success story is hard to check. Seven ordinary observations, including missed days and interruptions, give you something better: a record you can inspect. Here is a simple way to make one without buying anything.

---

Here is a small temptation that catches good investigators: keep the result that makes a tidy story, and quietly forget the day when the experiment failed.

In a historical investigation, we would call that selection a problem. In a notebook about our own attention, it can look like encouragement. One day felt extraordinary, so we remember it. Four ordinary days fade. An interruption becomes evidence we “couldn't focus,” even when the more important fact was that the phone rang during the attempt.

A field log is not a diagnosis and should not be a verdict about your character. Its job is to preserve enough context that future-you can read it without rewriting the past.

**A seven-day record you can actually keep**

Before beginning a short period of reading, planning or quiet attention, write down a task and a proposed duration. Then use three fields:

**1. Attempted.** Did you begin? Write the actual number of minutes, including zero. “No attempt today” is data, not a failure to hide.

**2. Interrupted.** Record one observable interruption—notification, noise, changed priority, discomfort—or write “none noticed.” Avoid turning a distraction into a diagnosis of why your mind behaved that way.

**3. Next adjustment.** Write one small change you can realistically test next time: phone in another room, shorter session, different chair, more forgiving time of day. Do not change five conditions at once if you want to learn which change mattered.

At the end of the week, compare the actual entries with your expectation. Did you start more often than you thought? Was a particular distraction frequent? Did the proposed duration exceed what your schedule allowed? And did you record the quiet, unspectacular days just as carefully as the successful ones?

This exercise is deliberately modest. Seven days cannot establish that an audio product “rewires” anyone, and a subjective log cannot diagnose an attention disorder. It can tell you whether a routine fits your life and help you ask a clearer question the next week.

The habit has a close relative in the archive. A damage survey, an engineer's notes and a witness statement become stronger when they preserve inconvenient details. We shouldn't demand a stricter standard from a hundred-year-old photograph than from our own most persuasive memory.
