Issue 01: The person changed. The conversation went on.

Proposed preheader: A sidewalk experiment asks what the mind leaves out. (not a confirmed Jetpack field)

Proposed custom excerpt: In a 1998 sidewalk experiment, a conversation continued even after the person asking for directions was replaced. That is not proof that your memory is unreliable in every situation. It is a reason to ask what any witness could actually notice. Follow the evidence before accepting the story.

A stranger stops you to ask for directions. While you answer, two workers carry a door between you. When the door has passed, the person who asked the question isn't the same person.

Would you notice?

In an experiment reported by Daniel Simons and Daniel Levin in 1998, a conversation on a college campus was interrupted by a door being carried through the scene. Behind it, one experimenter changed places with another. The researchers reported that only about half the pedestrians in the first experiment detected the substitution. That does not mean half of all people are blind to changes, or that everyday memory is worthless. It means something unusually striking can slip by when the scene still seems to make sense.

The paper is worth reading because the details resist the sensational version. This was not a claim that people cannot recognize faces. The researchers examined how a real-world exchange and the apparent social relevance of the other person shaped detection. A specific, arranged encounter tells us something about attention under those conditions; it does not quantify how any particular eyewitness will perform tomorrow.

Consider the ordinary error that follows: once someone tells us that the conversation partner changed, we imagine we would certainly have spotted it. But hindsight is not an observation recorded at the time.

A field exercise, not a laboratory test

Pick a familiar room. Without looking, write down five objects and where you believe they are. Then look. Mark each description as accurate, partly accurate or incorrect. Add the objects you did not think to list. Do not recruit an unsuspecting person, change a scene secretly or treat one attempt as a scientific measurement. The purpose is to practice distinguishing what you checked from what you assumed.

This is how we want to approach a new kind of evidence at Final Vestige. When an archival witness describes something extraordinary, the first question is not whether the witness was foolish or visionary. It is: what did the record permit that person to know?

Issue 02: A sleeping witness, a letter, and a 1784 puzzle

Proposed preheader: A remarkable claim survives on paper; proof is another matter. (not a confirmed Jetpack field)

Proposed custom excerpt: On May 4, 1784, the Marquis de Puységur said he observed an unusual state in a young man he was treating. In a letter dated May 8, he tried to describe it. His account survives. That makes it a valuable historical source, not a verified explanation.

On May 4, 1784, in Buzancy, an aristocrat experimenting with animal magnetism described something he had not expected. A young man later identified by historians as Victor Race appeared to enter a quiet, responsive state rather than the crisis-like reactions associated with some of Franz Mesmer's practices.

We know this first as Puységur's account. The description appears in a letter dated May 8, later associated with his *Mémoires pour servir à l'histoire et à l'établissement du magnétisme animal*. That chronology matters. The alleged experience, the subsequent written report and the printing of the work are related but not identical events.

It is easy to retell the story as either the birth of a miracle or the exposure of a fraud. Neither headline is earned by the surviving document.

Puységur believed that something significant had happened. He described observations and inferred causes, shaped by the vocabulary of animal magnetism available to him. Later historians have examined the episode as a turning point in the development of practices eventually associated with hypnotism and the study of suggestion. That later importance does not prove that Puységur's claimed magnetic mechanism existed or that the account demonstrates a reliable therapy.

The French national library catalogs the work as a two-volume publication spanning 1784–1785. The dating is a useful antidote to a neat but inaccurate single-year story. The letter, the editions and the later interpretation should not be collapsed into one source.

Three questions to try on the evidence

First: who witnessed the event directly, and who knew it through Puységur's narration?

Second: what behavior did he say he saw, and which mental, physical or supernatural explanation did he add?

Third: what independent comparison, if it had existed, would have made the explanation stronger—or exposed an alternative?

This is the same method we would use for a shipwreck log, a disaster inquiry or an archaeological report. A document can be authentic and fascinating while the explanation offered inside it remains unproven.

There is a modern echo: when any contemporary seller presents a powerful subjective experience as proof that a particular recording changes the brain, we should ask the same third question. That is a standard of evidence, not a judgment about what someone felt.

Issue 06: Your focus log should include the boring days

Proposed preheader: A seven-day field note is useful even when nothing dramatic happens. (not a confirmed Jetpack field)

Proposed custom excerpt: The most useful line in an attention log may be “nothing changed.” A spectacular success story is hard to check. Seven ordinary observations, including missed days and interruptions, give you something better: a record you can inspect. Here is a simple way to make one without buying anything.

Here is a small temptation that catches good investigators: keep the result that makes a tidy story, and quietly forget the day when the experiment failed.

In a historical investigation, we would call that selection a problem. In a notebook about our own attention, it can look like encouragement. One day felt extraordinary, so we remember it. Four ordinary days fade. An interruption becomes evidence we “couldn't focus,” even when the more important fact was that the phone rang during the attempt.

A field log is not a diagnosis and should not be a verdict about your character. Its job is to preserve enough context that future-you can read it without rewriting the past.

A seven-day record you can actually keep

Before beginning a short period of reading, planning or quiet attention, write down a task and a proposed duration. Then use three fields:

1. Attempted. Did you begin? Write the actual number of minutes, including zero. “No attempt today” is data, not a failure to hide.

2. Interrupted. Record one observable interruption—notification, noise, changed priority, discomfort—or write “none noticed.” Avoid turning a distraction into a diagnosis of why your mind behaved that way.

3. Next adjustment. Write one small change you can realistically test next time: phone in another room, shorter session, different chair, more forgiving time of day. Do not change five conditions at once if you want to learn which change mattered.

At the end of the week, compare the actual entries with your expectation. Did you start more often than you thought? Was a particular distraction frequent? Did the proposed duration exceed what your schedule allowed? And did you record the quiet, unspectacular days just as carefully as the successful ones?

This exercise is deliberately modest. Seven days cannot establish that an audio product “rewires” anyone, and a subjective log cannot diagnose an attention disorder. It can tell you whether a routine fits your life and help you ask a clearer question the next week.

The habit has a close relative in the archive. A damage survey, an engineer's notes and a witness statement become stronger when they preserve inconvenient details. We shouldn't demand a stricter standard from a hundred-year-old photograph than from our own most persuasive memory.
