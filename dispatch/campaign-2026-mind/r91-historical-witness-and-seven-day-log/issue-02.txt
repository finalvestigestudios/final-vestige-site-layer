Subject: The witness slept. Who verified the story?
Planned preheader (Jetpack rendering unverified): A 1784 case, a 1785 catalog puzzle, and the difference between testimony and proof.

On a spring day in 1784, a French aristocrat wrote that a young man had fallen into an unusually quiet sleep and could still respond to questions. The observer was the Marquis de Puységur; the man he described is often identified in later histories as Victor Race. It is a striking scene. It is also a claim made by the person conducting the demonstration—not an independently verified test.

THE RECORD BENEATH THE STORY
Puységur's accounts came from the world of animal magnetism, a movement that supposed an invisible influence could affect the body. He described states of calm responsiveness rather than the convulsive crises other practitioners emphasized. Readers should distinguish what he reported—the subject slept, answered, and was said to forget—from his explanation for why it happened. The words of an interested witness establish that he made the claim; they do not settle its cause.

A REVEALING DATE PROBLEM
Even the book's date requires care. France's national library catalogs the two-volume Mémoires under 1784–1785. The U.S. National Library of Medicine records a related copy as printed '1784 [i.e. 1785]' and notes a closing date of February 4, 1785. A first reported event, a later written account, a publication or imprint date, and a later collected edition are not interchangeable. That distinction is ordinary historical method, not a reason to dismiss the entire record.

WHAT THE COMMISSIONERS COULD AND COULD NOT ANSWER
In 1784, a royal commission including Benjamin Franklin investigated the supposed magnetic agent through trials of treatments associated with Charles Deslon. Its report challenged the proposed fluid explanation and emphasized expectation and imagination. It was not a controlled re-creation of Puységur's specific Victor narrative, nor evidence that every later claim about suggestion must be either true or false. Different studies answer different questions.

TRY THE HISTORIAN'S THREE-COLUMN TEST
Take one dramatic claim—historical or modern—and write three headings: (1) What the original witness says happened; (2) What an independent source can corroborate; (3) What remains an explanation, prediction, or inference. If the second column stays blank, record that blank. The temptation to fill it with certainty is exactly why the method matters.

WHY THIS BELONGS IN THE DISPATCH
The history of suggestion is a history of people searching for an explanation, but also of how easily testimony becomes promotion. None of these documents demonstrates that a contemporary hypnosis recording works, treats a condition, or improves anyone's concentration. Our linked archive explains the surviving sources and disagreements rather than prescribing a historical treatment.
