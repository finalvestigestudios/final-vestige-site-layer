Subject: Seven days of focus: what actually changed?
Planned preheader (Jetpack rendering unverified): A five-field log that makes ordinary days useful, without claiming a miracle.

A week can feel different depending on which hour you remember. One interrupted morning may erase the memory of a productive afternoon. A successful day can make a routine seem unstoppable. Neither impression is a reliable substitute for what you wrote down.

A USEFUL EXPERIMENT HAS A BEGINNING
Choose a modest routine that doesn't require a purchase: for example, sit down at the same time each day for a brief, distraction-limited reading or planning session. Decide in advance what you will record. If your routine already exists, start with the record rather than trying to manufacture a perfect baseline after the fact.

THE FIVE FIELDS
For each day, note (1) whether you started; (2) approximate minutes spent; (3) your main interruption; (4) the condition that made starting easier or harder; and (5) one small change to try next time. A missed session isn't a failed person or a worthless data point. Write 'did not start' and the obstacle you can identify; guesses are marked as guesses.

WHAT THIS LOG CANNOT SHOW
A single person's seven-day notes cannot establish that meditation audio works, that suggestion changes the brain, or that one method is superior to a control condition. Sleep, workload, mood, expectations, and competing obligations change at the same time. The record can still inform your own next practical choice; it cannot become a medical or scientific efficacy claim.

HOW TO READ THE WEEK
Look at all seven rows, not just the best and worst. Did the intended start time conflict with your actual responsibilities? Did interruptions occur at a predictable moment? Was a simpler routine more repeatable than a complicated one? Pick a next-week adjustment you can describe in one sentence. A useful outcome may be deciding to stop an unhelpful ritual.

Our next investigations will keep crossing between archival evidence and the mind that interprets it. The same rule applies whether the source is a historic witness, a photograph, or your own notebook: say what the evidence shows, note what it cannot, and keep the uncertainty visible.
