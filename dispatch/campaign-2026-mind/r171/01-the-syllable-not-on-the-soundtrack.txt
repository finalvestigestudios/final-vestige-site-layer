Subject: The syllable that wasn't on the soundtrack
Preheader: In 1976, researchers showed how seeing a voice can change what we think we hear.

THE DISPATCH | FINAL VESTIGE STUDIOS

There was no "da" on the soundtrack.

That is what makes an experiment published in December 1976 so unsettling. Researchers Harry McGurk and John MacDonald filmed a speaker producing one syllable, then paired the moving lips with audio of another. In a key condition, participants heard a recording of "ba" while watching lips articulate "ga". Many reported hearing "da"—a syllable that was not present in the recorded sound.

The researchers also checked what happened when people heard the audio on its own and when they saw speech with matching sound. Those controls mattered. Without them, an unusual answer might have been blamed on a poor recording or an unclear instruction. With them, the observation pointed to an interaction between what people saw and what they heard.

This was not proof that every person always hears the same illusion. It was not evidence that sensory impressions are worthless. It showed that even something as immediate as a spoken syllable can depend on information arriving through another sense.

For a historical investigator, that is more than a laboratory curiosity. An eyewitness may be sincere and attentive yet still be interpreting a scene rather than preserving a perfect copy of it. The lesson is not "trust nobody." It is to ask what was observed, what was inferred, and which independent records might separate the two.

Try it with your own notes today. Record one thing you heard, one thing you saw, and one conclusion you drew from both. Put the conclusion on a different line. You may discover how quickly a description becomes an explanation.
