**Subject:** The mind is not a camera

A stranger stops you to ask for directions. You point toward the street. Something large passes between you. When it clears, the person in front of you is no longer the person who asked.

Would you notice?

In a 1998 field experiment, Daniel Simons and Daniel Levin arranged a real-world interruption during a conversation with pedestrians. One experimenter was replaced by another while the pedestrian's view was blocked. In one experiment, **only about half noticed the substitution**. Recognition also varied with whether the pedestrians identified with the experimenters' social group.

That result is strange enough without dressing it up. It does **not** mean half of all eyewitnesses are wrong. It does not establish that a particular witness to a historical event failed to see something. The finding belongs to an arranged encounter, with specific people, conditions and measures.

A different experiment, published by Simons and Christopher Chabris in 1999, asked participants to attend to a moving scene while an unexpected person crossed it. Detection varied with task demands and the unexpected object's appearance. Researchers call this kind of failure to notice something unexpected **inattentional blindness**; missing a change between views is called **change blindness**. Related phenomena, but not interchangeable labels.

The unsettling lesson is subtler than "you can't trust your eyes." Attention has a job, and that job can consume the capacity we imagined was available to everything else. What feels like a complete record may be a record of what mattered to us at the time.

That distinction becomes urgent when we leave the laboratory and enter an archive.

A photograph can preserve light without preserving the reasons someone stood outside the frame. A witness statement can faithfully record what the witness remembers without settling what happened at the edge of attention. And a later caption can sound certain while carrying more interpretation than the original record.

**A two-column exercise for your next historical photograph:**

1. Look at the image for thirty seconds, then hide it.
2. Write what you can directly describe: the visible people, objects, orientation, and any legible markings.
3. In a second column, write what you inferred: who the people were, why an object was there, what happened immediately before or after.
4. Look again. Mark one entry you classified incorrectly. Ask which source—not which instinct—could resolve it.

This is an editorial evidence exercise, not a validated diagnostic test of perception. The point is to train the habit of labelling an observation as an observation and an interpretation as an interpretation.

We use that same distinction in Final Vestige investigations. A haunting account does not become stronger when we conceal uncertainty. It becomes worth reading when a reader can see the evidence, the assumptions, and the places where the story could change.
