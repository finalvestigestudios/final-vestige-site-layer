Seven days can leave a strong impression. The difficult part is knowing what the impression means.

That's a familiar problem at Final Vestige. A historical witness might recall a dramatic moment but forget what came before it. A photograph captures a scene but not the decisions outside its frame. In both cases, the record is more useful when we know what it does—and does not—show.

A focus routine deserves the same ordinary scrutiny. It doesn't need a miracle story.

**Try a five-field log for your next session:**

1. **Started?** Record yes or no, without explaining it away.
2. **Time:** Record actual minutes attempted, not the minutes you meant to practice.
3. **Interruption:** Note what interrupted you, if anything.
4. **Conditions:** Record one relevant feature of the environment—time of day, noise, or a competing demand.
5. **Next adjustment:** Choose only one change worth trying next time.

After seven days, look for a pattern you can describe without exaggerating. Perhaps you started more often after moving your phone away. Perhaps the routine was impractical on workdays. Perhaps your notes are too sparse to conclude much. Each is more useful than pretending the experiment proved a transformation.

This isn't a clinical test. A personal log cannot establish that an audio program alters brainwaves, cures a condition, or outperforms another practice. It can help you remember what you actually tried.
