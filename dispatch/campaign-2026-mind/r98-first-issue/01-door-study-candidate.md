A stranger stops you and asks for directions. You begin explaining the route. Two workers carry a door between you.

On the other side, a different person is listening.

Would you notice?

In 1998, psychologists Daniel Simons and Daniel Levin investigated a version of that question in everyday interactions. During conversations with pedestrians, the person requesting directions was secretly replaced while a door passed between them. **In their first experiment, only about half the pedestrians noticed the substitution.** That is a finding about the people and conditions studied—not a universal probability for every conversation.

The study makes a distinction worth carrying into any archive: *having looked at a thing* is not the same as *having preserved a detailed record of it*. A witness can be sincere, involved in an event and still miss something substantial.

A separate 1997 experiment by Ronald Rensink, J. Kevin O'Regan and James Clark used alternating versions of a scene with short blank intervals. Even conspicuous changes were difficult to identify when the interruption obscured the normal local signal that something had changed. A cue could make a difference. The work tested attention and change detection; it did not establish that all memories are false or that nobody sees what's in front of them.

**Try this when you encounter a startling claim:** write down (1) what the witness could actually observe, (2) what changed or might have happened outside their attention, and (3) which part of the account is inference rather than observation. It won't settle every disputed report. It will make the unanswered questions visible.

That is the method we're bringing from maps and original documents into the Mind & Perception Archive. It is also why this first Dispatch points to an investigation readers can visit *now*, rather than another promised future page.
