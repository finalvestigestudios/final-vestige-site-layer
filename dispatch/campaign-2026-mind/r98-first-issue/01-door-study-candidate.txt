THE DISPATCH / ISSUE 01 — ALTERNATE CANDIDATE

Subject: The stranger changed. The conversation didn't.
Preheader proposal ONLY (not confirmed Jetpack capability):
In a real-world experiment, even a face-to-face change was easy to miss.

A stranger stops you and asks for directions. You begin explaining the route.
Two workers carry a door between you.

On the other side, a different person is listening.

Would you notice?

In 1998, Daniel Simons and Daniel Levin tested this question during real-world
interactions. During conversations with pedestrians, the person asking for
directions was replaced while a door passed between them. In their first
experiment, only about half noticed the substitution. That finding applies
to those study conditions; it isn't a universal rate for every conversation.

Seeing an event isn't the same as preserving a complete record of it. A
witness can be sincere and still miss a substantial change.

In a separate 1997 experiment, Ronald Rensink, J. Kevin O'Regan and James
Clark alternated versions of a scene with short blank interruptions. Large
changes could be difficult to detect when the interruption removed the usual
motion cue. Their work concerns visual attention, not proof that every
memory is false.

A useful three-part notebook exercise:
1. What could the witness actually observe?
2. What might have changed outside their attention?
3. Which parts of the account are inferences?

This is the same discipline we apply to archival evidence, and we'll use it
when we turn to Puységur's eighteenth-century witnesses in Issue 02.
