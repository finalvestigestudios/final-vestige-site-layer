FINAL VESTIGE STUDIOS · THE DISPATCH

The stranger changed.
The conversation didn't.

A stranger stops you and asks for directions. You begin explaining the route. Two workers carry a door between you.

On the other side, a different person is listening.

Would you notice?

In 1998, psychologists Daniel Simons and Daniel Levin studied real-world conversations with pedestrians. The person asking for directions was secretly replaced while a door passed between them. In their first experiment, only about half noticed the substitution. This is a result for those study conditions, not a universal rate.

Looking at an event is not the same as preserving a complete record of it. Sincere witnesses can overlook meaningful changes.

In a separate 1997 experiment, Rensink, O'Regan and Clark alternated modified scenes with brief blank intervals. Changes that would ordinarily be noticeable became difficult to find. Attention matters; the result does not prove every memory is false.

Three questions to keep: What could a witness observe? What may have happened outside their attention? Which conclusions go beyond the observation?

The same questions guide our investigations of surviving maps, photographs and testimony. In Issue 02, we'll apply them to Puységur's eighteenth-century witnesses.
