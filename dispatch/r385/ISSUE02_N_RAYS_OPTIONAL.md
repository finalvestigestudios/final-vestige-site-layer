# The Dispatch — optional editorial Issue 02, unsent
Subject: The prism was gone. They still saw the rays.
Proposed preheader: A 1904 experiment asks what happens when expectation becomes evidence.
Status: NOT_ADOPTED / HOLD_UNSENT. This is not an approved replacement for the six original issues.

In a darkened French laboratory in 1904, observers believed they could detect an extraordinary form of radiation. When the essential prism was removed from their apparatus, they still reported the expected spectrum.

René Blondlot had announced N-rays in 1903. Other laboratories struggled to reproduce his findings. American physicist Robert W. Wood visited and described his inspection in a letter published by Nature on September 29, 1904.

Wood reported removing a prism that supposedly separated the rays into a spectrum. The experimenters still described the anticipated pattern. That is not a proof that every witness lied. It is a warning that honest observers can be influenced by expectations, especially when the alleged signal is faint and subjective.

The sources themselves require care. Wood's published letter reports his experience; it is not a recording of every moment. The American Physical Society's historical review supplies broader context, including the failures of replication and the famous prism incident.

Try a simple test on the next surprising claim you encounter. Write three lines: what was directly observed; what explanation was proposed; what control would help distinguish it from a competing explanation? If the original observation cannot survive that control, the explanation deserves further scrutiny.

A similar question appeared in Benjamin Franklin's 1784 investigation of animal magnetism: if people reported a sensation, did that establish the invisible force allegedly producing it? The evidence can acknowledge an experience without certifying its proposed cause.
