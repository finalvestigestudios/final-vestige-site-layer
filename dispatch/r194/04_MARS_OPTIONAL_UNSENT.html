The canals that weren’t on Mars

A mapped network in 1877 became a civilization. The missing step was proof.

In 1877, Giovanni Schiaparelli began mapping long, dark markings he observed on Mars. His Italian word was canali: channels. In English, canals also suggested something constructed. The translation made a captivating story possible, but it could not prove the existence of builders.

Percival Lowell investigated the idea and became its most prominent advocate. His 1906 book Mars and Its Canals made a complex case for a planet-spanning system. The book is evidence of what Lowell argued; it is not independent evidence that the canals existed.

The distinction matters because an observation and its interpretation can become fused. A drawing may preserve exactly what its maker thought was present while offering no independent confirmation of why the lines appeared.

Better observations later overturned the apparent straight-line network. NASA and the European Space Agency describe the canals as an optical illusion. Mars really does preserve evidence of ancient water; it does not follow that these nineteenth-century maps documented artificial irrigation.

Here is a useful test for an ordinary day. Write down what you directly observed. Beneath it, write the explanation that came to mind. Finally, ask what independent finding might prove your explanation wrong. Those three sentences are not interchangeable.

At Final Vestige, that boundary between record and explanation is the whole point. It applies to historical maps, witness accounts, and what our eyes leave out.
