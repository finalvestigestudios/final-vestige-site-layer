Subject: The hatch and the missing spark
Preheader: What NASA's Apollo 1 investigation could establish—and what it could not.

On January 27, 1967, three astronauts were inside Apollo 1 for a ground test at Cape Kennedy. The rocket was not fueled. Yet the cabin caught fire, and the crew could not escape.

A short explanation would blame a spark. The records demand a longer one.

NASA's investigation found that an electrical arc was the most probable ignition source, but the exact ignition point could not be positively identified. The distinction matters: a likely explanation is not a recovered piece of proof.

The other conditions were not mysterious. The sealed capsule held pressurized pure oxygen. Flammable materials were present. The innermost hatch opened inward, and NASA's later historical account says opening the three-part hatch required at least 90 seconds in ideal conditions. Pressure and fire made that impossible. A NASA history records the last crew transmission ending about 17 seconds after the first fire indication.

Those times describe different things. Seventeen seconds is the approximate communication interval, not a proven duration of the entire fire. Ninety seconds is an ideal-case hatch-opening estimate, not a stopwatch measurement of the actual rescue attempt. Conflating them turns evidence into a dramatic but inaccurate headline.

Here is one method to borrow when examining any catastrophe: separate the probable trigger, the conditions that let damage spread, and the barriers that prevented escape. Then ask which of those the investigators actually demonstrated. A single cause rarely describes an entire system failure.
