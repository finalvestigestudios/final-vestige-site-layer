## Optional Stage A: Evidence before improvement (editorial, after VERIFIED welcome delivery)
**Subject:** The notebook is more useful than the promise

**Preheader:** A five-minute way to separate what happened from what you expected.

Someone places a diary next to an old photograph. The photograph shows a moment. The diary tells us what its owner believed it meant. An investigator needs both, but shouldn't mistake one for the other.

Try that distinction on a routine you want to build. At the end of a day, write three lines: *I intended to...*, *I actually did...*, *What interrupted me...*. Do this even on days when the experiment fails. A record of missed days tells you about feasibility; it doesn't prove any method succeeded or failed in a clinical sense.

This is the principle behind the Seven Day Focus Fieldbook. It asks for observations before conclusions, makes space for a modest starting routine and keeps a thirty-day log if you want to continue. The 52-page PDF is free. You never need a paid audio program or to subscribe again to use it.

## Optional Stage B: Trace the claim (editorial, ONLY if the reader has explicitly engaged Stage A)
**Subject:** A witness can be sincere and still be wrong

**Preheader:** What an eighteenth-century file can teach us before we accept a modern claim.

The old accounts of Puységur have a quality that makes them unusually valuable: people tried to write down astonishing experiences before the vocabulary we use for hypnosis today had settled.

A record of what somebody experienced is not interchangeable with proof of the explanation offered for it. The same distinction applies when an audio product promises to change a mental state or to produce the equivalent of a much longer practice.

Before you pay for any recording, write the seller's promise in one column and the independently established evidence in another. Ask what outcomes were measured, against what comparison and for whom. If those questions cannot be answered, the claim is still a claim. It isn't automatically false; it isn't demonstrated, either.

We practice this discipline with historical evidence because it protects curiosity without asking readers to surrender judgment.
