Evidence 01 / 06DOCUMENTED CONTEXT

Three men. One ground test.

2 source-checked claims

This was a ground test, not a launch. The rocket was unfueled; the spacecraft was not free of danger.

Evidence limitation

An unfueled launch vehicle did not mean a safe cabin.

Review the underlying finding

Gus Grissom, Ed White and Roger Chaffee prepared for the Plugs-Out Test at Launch Complex 34. The test had not been classified as hazardous because only tests involving fueled vehicles were, and the launch vehicle was unfueled. The spacecraft still held a 100 percent oxygen atmosphere, combustible materials and an innermost hatch that took 90 seconds to open under ideal conditions.

Evidence 02 / 06PRIMARY REVIEW BOARD RECORD

An atmosphere built for a different risk.

3 source-checked claims

One hundred percent oxygen at elevated ground-test pressure greatly increased the fire hazard inside Command Module 012.

Evidence limitation

Oxygen accelerated combustion; it was not itself the ignition spark.

Review the underlying finding

The 1967 Review Board documented a 100 percent oxygen cabin atmosphere at 16.7 pounds per square inch absolute during the ground test, and determined that the test conditions were extremely hazardous. It also determined that this atmosphere presents severe fire hazards if the amount and location of combustibles in the Command Module are not restricted and controlled.

Evidence 03 / 06INSTRUMENT CHRONOLOGY

About fifteen seconds changed the system.

5 source-checked claims

The surviving instrument record describes an extraordinarily rapid pressure-and-fire sequence.

Evidence limitation

The timeline reports observed markers. The intervals between them are not interpolated sensor measurements.

Review the underlying finding

The first fire transmission began at 23:31:04.7 GMT. About six seconds later the cabin-pressure sensor showed a clear rise; by about 23:31:16 its range was exceeded. The pressure vessel ruptured, at a pressure of at least 29 psia, at about 23:31:19 GMT. The Board describes the first stage of the fire as ending approximately 15 seconds after the verbal report. Counting from the 23:31:04.7 report to the 23:31:19.4 rupture mark gives 14.7 seconds. These are approximate marks from the Board account, not a reconstructed second-by-second animation.

Evidence 04 / 06HARDWARE AND DESIGN FINDING

The hatch opened inward.

4 source-checked claims

The innermost hatch opened inward, and the Board found that, with the cabin pressurized, it could not be opened before rupture.

Evidence limitation

The hatch was one of several interacting hazards, not a standalone explanation of the fire.

Review the underlying finding

NASA's history of the accident says the innermost of the command module's hatches opened inward and took 90 seconds to open under ideal conditions. The Review Board found that, due to internal pressure, the inner hatch could not be opened before the pressure vessel ruptured. Emergency procedures assigned the hatch to the Senior Pilot, Ed White. The Board found his harness buckle unopened after the fire, which indicated that he had begun the standard hatch-opening procedure.

Claim crosswalk (4)

  • verified Ed White, as Senior Pilot, was responsible for opening the hatch in an emergency.Board report, Part V: "Emergency procedures called for the Senior Pilot, occupying the center couch, to unlatch and remove the hatch". NASA crew biographies: "Ed White was selected as Senior Pilot for the first Apollo flight." NASA source (opens in a new tab) ↗

Evidence 05 / 06EVIDENCE BOUNDARY

The spark was never conclusively identified.

3 source-checked claims

The Board determined that the most probable initiator was an electrical arc, and also found that no single ignition source was conclusively identified.

Evidence limitation

Probable is not proven. This experience deliberately keeps the uncertainty visible.

Review the underlying finding

The Apollo 204 Review Board determined that the most probable initiator of the fire was an electrical arc in the sector between the -Y and +Z spacecraft axes. It also found that no single ignition source was conclusively identified. The Board documented the extremely hazardous test conditions and the severe fire hazard of combustibles in that atmosphere, and determined that the crew was never capable of effecting emergency egress.

Evidence 06 / 06ENGINEERING RESPONSE

Inward became outward.

2 source-checked claims

NASA replaced the inward-opening hatch with a unified hatch that opened outward, from inside or outside, in three seconds.

Evidence limitation

The improvements were part of a broad redesign; Apollo 1 did not create every safety innovation from nothing.

Review the underlying finding

NASA's history of the accident describes a new unified, outward-opening hatch that could be opened from inside or outside the spacecraft in three seconds. NASA's history of the flammability tests records 102 deliberate ignitions of Command Module Boilerplate 1224 between December 1967 and February 1968, in three series of tests. Our reading: these tests show NASA examining how fire would actually behave rather than assuming it could always be prevented.

Exhibit text photographed at Kennedy Space Center gives 5 seconds for the Block II hatch; this page uses NASA's article. NASA source (opens in a new tab) ↗
  • verified 102 deliberate ignitions of Boilerplate 1224 between December 1967 and February 1968.NASA History (2018): "three series of tests comprising 102 deliberate ignitions between December 1967 and February 1968". NASA source (opens in a new tab) ↗
