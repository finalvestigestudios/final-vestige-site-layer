Subject: The coast that outlived the certainty
Preheader: A 1513 chart offers a better question than an Antarctic mystery.

The map survives. The claim built around it needs a separate examination.

In 1513, the Ottoman navigator Piri Reis assembled a chart of the Atlantic world. Its surviving coastlines show a world of older nautical knowledge and newly circulating information from Atlantic voyages. Some shapes resist a clean fit against a modern globe.

A map isn't a photograph taken from above. It combines measurements, sketches, accounts, older charts, and decisions about what belongs where.

That distinction makes the fragment fascinating enough without giving it secret powers. The claim that a southern line proves advance knowledge of Antarctica does not follow merely from an ambiguous coastline.

Cartographic historian Max Justo Guedes examined the sources and Portuguese nautical knowledge available to Piri Reis in the early sixteenth century. That research gives us a more useful method: identify the object, establish the maker's possible sources, then ask what the evidence actually supports.

The enduring story is how geographical knowledge traveled between mariners and cultures.
