**Subject:** The ghost who was still alive

**Preheader:** In a nineteenth-century portrait, the apparition had a living identity.

In the early 1860s, a photographer appeared to promise something a camera should not be able to provide: a portrait of a living sitter, accompanied by the dead.

William H. Mumler's photographs carried pale shapes beside the living. The images held meaning for some bereaved families. But an emotionally powerful image and an established explanation for its appearance are two different things.

The Metropolitan Museum of Art catalogs one example, Three Spirits with Photograph on Table, as an albumen silver print from a glass negative, broadly dated 1862–75. The record establishes an object, a creator and a photographic process. It does not verify that the faint shapes were spirits.

The Met also reports that a figure presented as an apparition in two pictures was identified as a local resident who was still alive. Mumler later faced fraud and larceny charges in New York in 1869. Accusations themselves are not verdicts, and the precise process behind each surviving photograph cannot be inferred from an image alone.

The White House Historical Association documents another Mumler portrait of Mary Todd Lincoln, with a figure resembling Abraham Lincoln imposed over her image. The response of a grieving viewer can be deeply genuine even when a photograph's supernatural explanation is not.

Here's a useful way to read any striking record: first establish exactly what survives, then ask which independent test would distinguish rival explanations. Do not confuse the witness's certainty with the photographer's method.

Franklin's 1784 inquiry into animal magnetism asked a comparable question about belief, experience and control. Its investigation makes a useful counterpoint to the camera that seemed to testify.
