# THE DISPATCH — OPTIONAL ISSUE 02, UNSENT

**Subject:** The blindfold changed the evidence

**Proposed preheader (NOT Jetpack verified):** In 1784, investigators changed what a witness could see. The reports changed, too.

**Status:** REVIEW ONLY, NOT AN APPROVED REPLACEMENT of PR #175 Issue 02. Editorial slot, no affiliate promotion.

---

In Paris in 1784, investigators were asked to examine a remarkable claim: that an invisible fluid, called animal magnetism, could act on the human body.

They could have argued about the force in the abstract. Instead, they changed what a participant knew.

The commissioners' report describes a woman who said she felt warmth during demonstrations conducted by M. Jumelin. When blindfolded, her reported sensations no longer followed where the operator directed the supposed magnetic influence. In another trial, she believed a procedure was taking place while the operator was absent. She still reported sensations. The document reports both observations; its authors interpreted them as evidence of the power of expectation, rather than the proposed magnetic fluid.

The sensations were reported. The cause had not been demonstrated. Removing a visual cue did not disprove every imaginable claim about mind and body. It tested whether the reported effects tracked the particular cause being proposed.

The investigation was commissioned in 1784. The English translation we can inspect was printed in London in 1785. That is why we distinguish the experiment's year from the document's edition date.

The report also has limits. Its procedures and language are those of the eighteenth century, not a modern, preregistered clinical trial. And this historical test cannot settle whether any contemporary audio, hypnosis or meditation product improves attention.

Here is a useful three-question test whenever a story sounds convincing: (1) What exactly did a witness report? (2) What changed when an expected cue was removed? (3) Which explanation survived that change — and which remains untested?

The value of the report isn't that its authors found a satisfying villain. It's that they created a condition in which an explanation could fail.
