# The Beat Your Ears Invent
### What binaural-beat experiments measure—and why hearing a rhythm isn't proof that a recording can command the brain
**Final Vestige Studios | Mind & Perception | Original investigation | UNPUBLISHED | 8 November 2026**
**Editorial separation:** This investigation contains no affiliate recommendation. It is not a treatment guide.

Imagine a scientist placing headphones over your ears. In the left channel, a pure tone hums at 400 hertz. In the right, another tone sounds at 410. Neither speaker is producing a ten-hertz vibration. Yet under suitable listening conditions, you may hear a slow wavering, as though a third rhythm has emerged between the two. The ten-hertz difference is real arithmetic. The apparent beat is real perception. What happens next—inside the brain, in your attention, in your day—requires an entirely different set of evidence.

This is the strange promise and stubborn limit of binaural beats: the mind constructs an experience from signals that are individually less interesting than their combination. Once the sensation is noticed, a leap becomes tempting. If you can hear the invented rhythm, perhaps the whole brain is now oscillating to it. And if the brain follows the sound, perhaps concentration, calm, memory, or sleep can be selected like a preset.

That chain sounds tidy. Research does not establish it as a general rule.

## The sound between two channels

A binaural beat usually starts with two pure tones at slightly different frequencies, delivered separately to the ears. The listener may perceive a beat corresponding to their difference. That percept is not the same as a physical ten-hertz amplitude modulation mixed into a single loudspeaker signal; an ordinary monaural beat is made by mixing tones acoustically, and the mechanisms and test conditions differ. The effect is especially interesting precisely because the ear-to-ear combination depends on auditory processing.

A demonstration isn't a diagnosis, however. Some listeners scarcely perceive a beat under some conditions. Volume, tone frequency, the difference between channels, headphones, hearing, masking sound, and listening attention matter. A person who experiences the pulse has established that their auditory system integrated the tones in a particular way. They have not established an EEG response or a beneficial state of consciousness.

The distinction parallels the McGurk effect, in which seeing one mouth movement can alter what speech sound a person reports hearing. A perceptual illusion can expose machinery that ordinary experience hides. It cannot, on its own, certify an unrelated outcome. Final Vestige's unpublished McGurk investigation and its published change-blindness work make the same methodological point from different angles: there is a difference between what a person experienced and what an experiment measured.

## The claim inside the claim

Commercial audio language often bundles three propositions. First: certain sound patterns can be perceived. Second: exposure may alter aspects of electrical activity measurable with EEG. Third: the same recording will reliably improve a purchaser's focus, meditation, creativity, or health.

Even a positive answer to the first proposition would not automatically settle the second. Even a measured EEG effect would not settle the third. Electroencephalography records electrical activity from many sources through scalp electrodes. A change in spectral power or phase locking is not identical to achieving the sustained attention demanded by work, nor is it a clinical outcome. And the effect of one laboratory recording cannot simply be assigned to a differently produced commercial file.

In 2023, Ruth Maria Ingendoh, Ella S. Posny, and Angela Heine reviewed experimental studies of binaural beats and electrical brain activity. Fourteen studies met their criteria. Five reported results consistent with entrainment, eight reported contrary findings, and one returned mixed findings. Study designs, beat frequencies, durations, and EEG analyses varied enough to limit comparisons. Their conclusion was not that binaural beats are imaginary. It was that research could not yet settle a consistent, generalized entrainment effect.

This is one of those moments when a negative-sounding conclusion deserves to be read carefully. The review did not find that every participant in every experiment showed no measurable change. It found that a common mechanism invoked to explain large promises had inconsistent evidentiary support. That is a narrower conclusion—and a more useful one.

**Source:** Ingendoh, Posny & Heine, PLOS ONE, 2023, https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0286023

## A more favorable experiment doesn't erase the uncertainty

A 2025 study by Anastasiia Melnichuk, Robert K. Cooper Jr., and Larry W. Hawk Jr. makes a good test of whether an evidence-first investigation can accommodate a positive finding. Eighty undergraduate participants were randomized across sixteen combinations of beat frequency, carrier tone, onset timing, and masking noise. Each completed two visits comparing beat stimulation and control audio; EEG was collected.

Under one combination—gamma-frequency beats with a lower carrier tone and background white noise—the researchers reported better general attention performance, while not finding that beats prevented the decline in vigilance across time. They also reported EEG entrainment that varied with listening parameters.

That is worth taking seriously. It is also not a validation of every brainwave-audio product. The tested intervention depended on multiple laboratory-controlled parameters. The sample was drawn from undergraduates. The outcome concerned specific attention measures, and the researchers themselves distinguish overall performance from vigilance decrement. Without matching the recording, listening conditions, population, outcome and follow-up, there is no basis for claiming identical benefits for a retail download.

Importantly, a later positive experiment does not make the earlier systematic review retrospectively false. One was a synthesis of earlier, heterogeneous work; the other was a focused experiment testing parameters. Evidence changes as new experiments arrive. The correct editorial response is to update the picture without converting uncertainty into certainty.

**Source:** Melnichuk et al., Scientific Reports, 2025, https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-88517-z

## What about feeling calmer?

Attention is not the only outcome researchers study. Some people listen to repetitive audio because it feels pleasant or provides a cue to stop working. The fact that a routine is pleasant does not require a special EEG explanation. Conversely, a pleasant subjective response should not be dismissed because an entrainment mechanism remains unsettled.

A 2024 systematic review by Chee and colleagues examined 31 studies of music, noise, white noise and binaural beats, considering physiological arousal and cognition. The findings on cardiac, electrodermal and pupillary measures in relation to cognitive performance were mixed or inadequate for strong conclusions. A question about whether a listener likes a sound is different from whether physiological measures shift predictably and differently from matched control sound.

There is also clinical literature in settings such as surgery or dental care. Some reviews report improvements in anxiety measurements in particular circumstances; others highlight biases, heterogeneity and uncertain pain effects. These studies are not about solving everyday procrastination with a consumer MP3. They involve different populations, often brief exposure, and outcomes affected by many aspects of the environment and the comparison condition. It would be poor evidence practice to lift a promising result from a procedure room and print it above a 'get focused instantly' button.

**Source:** Chee et al., International Journal of Psychophysiology, 2024, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijpsycho.2024.112328 ; see also published reviews at https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40989974/ and https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41176178/ for narrower clinical contexts.

## A listening demonstration without the sales pitch

A useful experiment requires no purchase. Choose a safe, stationary place; do not run attention exercises while driving or operating machinery. Set aside one short, ordinary task—reading two pages, organizing one note, or beginning a paragraph. First try it for twelve minutes in quiet, with distractions removed. On another comparable occasion, try it with comfortable, ordinary instrumental music or neutral ambience at low volume. Record the task, the time, interruptions, and what actually got finished.

This is not a controlled clinical experiment. Order effects, fatigue, novelty, task difficulty, and personal expectation can all change the result. It does help expose a more practical question than 'Did the frequency transform my brain?': *Which routine lets me begin the work I intended to do?*

Avoid using artificially loud sounds, and stop if listening causes discomfort. Do not infer medical benefit, an EEG state, or generalizable effectiveness from your own small comparison. ## The history of an obedient mind

This modern story belongs in a historical archive for a reason. Long before anyone could record an MP3 or measure EEG power, European investigators debated whether a person's sensations and responses were being produced by an external influence, by expectation, by instruction, or by some combination.

In 1784, the Franklin commission challenged explanations offered for animal magnetism. A separate historical thread leads to the Marquis de Puységur and accounts of 'magnetic somnambulism.' These records are important because they document beliefs, experiments, testimony, suggestion and unequal relationships between operator and subject. They do not constitute clinical validation for modern recorded hypnosis or brainwave products. Final Vestige's *Puységur’s Animal Magnetism Files* preserves that archival distinction: https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/.

In both centuries, the mistake to watch for is the same: treating a compelling experience as if it answered every question about its cause. Someone hears a rhythm nobody played. Someone follows a suggestion and reports a striking sensation. The witnesses need not be lying. The experience may be genuine. But it is the beginning of an inquiry, not the last page.

A statement that a retail recording 'reprograms the brain,' delivers an hour's meditation in twelve minutes, or produces perfect concentration requires evidence appropriate to that much stronger claim. ## What would make the claim stronger?

A convincing product-specific evaluation would identify the actual commercial audio file, describe its sound characteristics, randomize enough people to compare it against credible alternatives, define the intended outcome in advance, account for expectation and baseline differences, and report results whether or not they favored the product. For claims about sustained productivity or health, researchers would need appropriately measured outcomes and follow-up—not only the listener's immediate impression or an unblinded EEG plot.

Such a trial might find an effect. It might find only preference. It might find no difference. The point of independent verification is not to prejudge which answer will appear.

There is a small irony here. A product category built around the mind's power over experience is especially vulnerable to mistaken certainty about the meaning of experience. Our ears can make a beat out of two tones. That is remarkable enough without borrowing an outcome no experiment established.
