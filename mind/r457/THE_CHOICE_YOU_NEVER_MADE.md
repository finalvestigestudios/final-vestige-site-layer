# The Choice You Never Made — And the Reason You Gave for It
**Final Vestige Studios | Mind & Perception Archive | Original investigation | 17 November 2026**  
**Status:** UNPUBLISHED EDITORIAL DRAFT. No clinical or commercial product testing is represented.

> A person points to one of two photographs. A researcher hands back the other photograph. The person explains the choice. But it was never their choice.

## A very ordinary decision, followed by an extraordinary substitution

Consider the moment after choosing between two pictures. You think your choice is the simplest fact in the room. You saw two faces, compared them, pointed to one, and watched the experimenter put the selected card into your hands. A few seconds later you are asked to say why that face appealed to you. It is a familiar kind of question. You may mention an expression, the shape of a mouth, or something that is difficult to name. You can usually supply a reason.

In the 2005 experiment by Petter Johansson, Lars Hall, Sverker Sikström and Andreas Olsson, the selected photograph was sometimes **not** the one handed back. Using a concealed switching technique, the researchers substituted the unchosen face and then requested an explanation. Many participants did not catch the discrepancy. Some proceeded to justify the substituted face. That is the basic result the authors named *choice blindness*. It is both a striking example of an error-monitoring failure and a warning against treating a fluent explanation as a recording of what happened.

The distinction matters. The paper does not demonstrate that nobody knows what they want, that all memories are false, or that an operator can make people believe anything. It demonstrates something narrower and experimentally more interesting: under a deliberately deceptive choice-and-feedback procedure, an outcome can be accepted as the outcome of one's own choice even when it was swapped. The participant's words are real evidence of the explanation they gave **after** the exchange. They are not reliable proof that the exchange matched the earlier decision.

The primary report appeared in *Science*, 7 October 2005, 310(5745):116–119, DOI 10.1126/science.1111709. Read the research abstract and bibliographic record at https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16210542/ . The authors' method used paired photographs of faces and a sleight-of-hand manipulation; the research record describes the discrepancy and the explanations but not a supernatural mechanism.

## Three separate events that can be confused

There are three distinct records: the person's original intention or preference; the action of selecting a face; and the face later shown as the selected outcome. In an ordinary interaction these normally align, so a listener hears the explanation and assumes they do. The experimental substitution broke that alignment.

To reconstruct the event, we should ask separate questions rather than collapse them into "What did the participant believe?" Was the original face recognized? Was the switch consciously noticed at the moment? Was the participant reluctant to challenge the experimenter? Did the person remember which option was originally preferred? Did the explanation merely fit the face now in view? Each question requires different data. A non-detection rate by itself cannot identify all the psychological mechanisms.

The temptation to leap to a sweeping conclusion is strong because the scene is cinematic. A card is swapped; the person tells a coherent story; we picture a mind that has rewritten its history. Yet the observed behavior is compatible with several processes: limited attention to the handoff, uncertainty about attractiveness, failure to compare a fleeting choice with a current outcome, post-choice rationalization, and social expectations surrounding an experiment. The original authors' interpretation is important, but it should be evaluated alongside subsequent tests and alternative explanations.

A useful source-reading rule follows: when an article claims that the study proved people "invent reasons for everything," return to the exact task. Face preference is not a contract, a medical decision, or a life-defining belief. The result supports skepticism about the introspective explanation under specified circumstances, not blanket distrust of every reason anyone gives.

## The marketplace: jam, tea and a sample that was quietly replaced

In 2010, researchers moved the logic away from photographs. Lars Hall and colleagues set up a tasting stall at a supermarket. Shoppers sampled paired varieties of jam or tea, chose a favorite, then received what they believed to be the chosen item and explained why it had won. On selected trials the researchers quietly switched the tasting containers. In the published study, **no more than one-third of manipulated trials were detected**. Even noticeably different flavor or scent pairs escaped detection in a substantial portion of trials.

The investigators' own descriptions include cinnamon-apple versus bitter grapefruit jam, and mango versus Pernod scented tea. Such pairings make the phenomenon vivid, but they also create a methodological discipline: the percentages are about manipulated trials in a supermarket tasting protocol, **not** the percentage of consumers who are always fooled by marketing. The researchers tested whether shoppers spotted the substituted taste or smell at that moment, not whether a paid product later satisfied those shoppers.

Primary record: Hall, Johansson, Tärning, Sikström and Deutgen, "Magic at the marketplace: Choice blindness for the taste of jam and the smell of tea," *Cognition* 117(1) (2010), 54–61. DOI 10.1016/j.cognition.2010.06.010. Read the indexed abstract at https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20637455/ and the authors' university publication summary at https://www.lunduniversity.lu.se/publication/52cc857a-0c24-48f4-b81f-a0407a2ebfe9 .

A physical tasting task also has important boundaries. A participant may be surprised, distracted, or unsure how confidently to identify a flavor. The act of explaining a sample can invite a locally plausible narrative. It does not follow that a participant would fail to recognize a fraudulent financial charge or mistake one subscription for another. To make that claim one would need experiments addressing those contexts.

## The crucial qualification: failure to notice is not identical to forgetting

One explanation of choice blindness is that the original preference simply fades from memory. A 2014 experiment put that idea to a direct test rather than assuming it. Participants rated 20 female faces for sympathy on a ten-point scale. On three selected trials, investigators showed altered ratings, three points from those originally supplied, and invited explanation.

Across those manipulated trials, about **41%** went undetected. Yet after being told about the manipulation, participants accepted the altered rating as their remembered original on only **3%** of trials. The researchers concluded that impaired memory alone did not fully explain the blindness. Those are separate outcome measures under a new protocol. Do not compare the percentages as if they came from the same denominator or were interchangeable estimates of one universal effect.

The study, "Memory impairment is not sufficient for choice blindness to occur," is freely available at https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4033137/ . It involved 88 participants. Its usefulness is not that it destroys the original result: instead, it forces the claim to become precise. A participant can fail to detect a manipulated outcome without permanently replacing the earlier choice in memory. Awareness, recall, explanation and endorsement must be kept separate.

That refinement makes the research more interesting. In everyday life we often ask someone to describe what they chose, and take their answer to stand for a stable earlier preference. The experiment suggests that the opportunity to challenge the displayed outcome may be quite different from the ability to remember an earlier choice when prompted later.

## When choices are consequential, resist easy analogies

Another extension examined financial decisions. Owen McLaughlin and Jason Somerville's pension-choice experiment reported that no more than **37.2%** of manipulated trials were detected over its tested conditions. But it recruited 100 participants for an experimental decision context, and the publication itself describes the manipulation, predictors and reasoning reports; it cannot establish how frequently people would unknowingly accept a substituted pension election on a real regulated platform.

Bibliographic caution: the Cambridge platform labels its online listing 2023, but the journal article is **2013**, *Judgment and Decision Making*, volume 8(5), pages 577–588. Source: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/judgment-and-decision-making/article/choice-blindness-in-financial-decision-making/74173A560D9294AE9A8D51FAF3A70FA6 . For Final Vestige, that date distinction matters: a current web-page timestamp is not the date an experiment happened.

Later work also explored whether briefly endorsing a substituted preference can influence subsequent choices and ratings. A 2013 paper, published online in October of that year, reported more frequent subsequent choices of previously rejected faces under its procedure. That suggests the experimental response is not always an empty verbal performance, but neither proves that every manipulated answer permanently changes a preference. The task still concerns a limited set of face choices, and the mechanism remains a research question. Source: https://doi.org/10.1002/bdm.1807 .

A responsible account should resist three shortcuts: treating missed substitutions as all-purpose suggestibility, treating a self-explanation as a lie, or treating an experimentally observed shift in later preference as a clinical or financial intervention. The strength of the evidence lies in what was measured, not in all the things a headline can make the scene seem to imply.

## An eighteenth-century archive asks the same kind of evidentiary question

Our published [Puységur's Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) preserve an eighteenth-century argument that sought credibility through testimony, witness statements, signed documents, predictions and notarial records. Those records can establish that particular people said or signed particular things. They cannot, merely by existing, establish the magnetic fluid or extraordinary causal mechanism the historical operators proposed.

The bridge to choice blindness is **methodological, not diagnostic**. We do not know that a named person in Puységur's files experienced choice blindness. We do know that a historical narrative, like a modern decision experiment, must distinguish the original event from a later account of it. The event, the written record, the witnesses' interpretations and the theory used to explain them may be four different things.

There is a second, ethical similarity. In Puységur's case files, several subjects were ill or vulnerable, and his notes openly identify operator pressure and the possibility of compliant false answers. In the modern choice-blindness experiments, deliberate substitutions helped researchers examine people's explanations, and debriefing matters. Neither scenario gives a present-day writer license to demonstrate the effect by tricking readers into an irreversible decision. A page about susceptibility to suggestion should be unusually careful not to exploit it.

Our published investigation of [Mesmerism and Franklin's 1784 commission](https://finalvestige.com/2026/11/02/mesmerism/) makes a complementary point: vivid experiences and sincerely reported effects need not validate the proposed physical cause. The commissioners tested the magnetic-fluid hypothesis, not whether everybody was pretending. That same divide between testimony and causal inference is what choice blindness teaches at laboratory scale.

## Try a twelve-minute choice audit without deceiving anyone

This is an original, free reflection exercise, **not** an experimental replication, susceptibility test or medical intervention. It does not require a partner to lie or switch anything.

**Minute 0–2 — Make a real, low-stakes choice.** Pick between two freely available, neutral options, such as two short public-domain passages or two ways of organizing a research note. Write down which one you chose **before** explaining it. Add the date and the exact alternatives.

**Minute 2–4 — Write one reason.** Limit yourself to a sentence. Record how certain you are on a 0–5 scale, but do not treat the number as a validated psychological measure.

**Minute 4–6 — Separate evidence from explanation.** Which part of the reason is observable? Which part is a judgment about yourself, such as "I always prefer simpler things"? Mark the difference. A subjective preference can be real without becoming a general law.

**Minute 6–8 — Check the record.** Reread the alternatives and your recorded choice. Has your reason changed? If so, record the change rather than rewriting the earlier note. Do not conceal the initial entry.

**Minute 8–10 — Look for a source.** If your explanation relies on an empirical assertion, seek a primary study or independent source. If it relies on personal taste, label it personal taste. Evidence about a population cannot determine your individual preference on demand.

**Minute 10–12 — Close the loop.** Write: "What I chose / What I observed / What I infer / What remains unknown." Keep all four lines. You now have a small decision record that resists the temptation to turn a later explanation into the only surviving account.

If the decision is medical, financial or legally important, do not substitute this exercise for qualified assistance. The point is to preserve the record of a harmless choice, not to diagnose a condition or prove a mental trick.

## What this does — and does not — tell us about meditation audio

Choice blindness is relevant to a consumer reviewing a persuasive sales page for an ordinary reason: it suggests that our stories about decisions can sometimes be less secure than we assume. That is a reason to **write down a purchase criterion before** reading testimonials or promotional claims. It is not evidence that meditation audio makes the brain more discerning or that hypnosis can remove the effect.

Inspire3 describes Zen12 as a twelve-minute-session meditation audio program with twelve levels and four styles for each level. Its ordinary catalog and a separate promotional sales page present different checkout contexts. These are **seller descriptions** and do not supply a controlled efficacy trial of the actual package. [The existing free Seven Day Focus Fieldbook](https://finalvestige.com/focus/) contains practical tools and questions for assessing audio promises without paying for a product.

If you are assessing an audio purchase, make your criteria explicit: file format, course length, whether guidance or ambient sound suits you, accessibility and sensory comfort, precise final checkout amount, refund restrictions, and what you can do for free first. Prefer a comparison that can survive being read after the excitement of a promotional clip has passed. There is no reason to infer increased focus or a brainwave "upgrade" from a choice-blindness experiment.

## What the evidence supports

**Supported:** in specific experiments, deliberately substituted choice outcomes were often not detected at once; people sometimes explained outcomes they had not actually chosen; subsequent work shows the result in face, tasting and some rating tasks.

**Qualified:** the frequency and meaning depend on task, manipulation, detection definition, participant population and timing. Failure to detect a substitution, accepting its description at that moment, remembering the original later, and changing a future preference are different measures.

**Not supported:** everyone is easily controlled; people are universally unable to know their reasons; any mental-audio seller can "reprogram" choices; the Puységur archive proves such an effect occurred to named historical subjects; or any Inspire3 product prevents choice blindness, produces wealth, treats disease or guarantees concentration.

The unnerving detail in the opening story is not that a stranger planted a thought. It is that an explanation can sound most finished precisely when the event it purports to explain still needs checking. The remedy is not to distrust every memory. It is to keep the source, the observation and the interpretation on separate lines.
