# The Tree That Wasn't Magnetized

*How an apricot tree in Benjamin Franklin's garden became a test of the difference between feeling something and knowing why.*

**An investigation into 1784, expectation and historical evidence.**

The boy had been told that a tree was waiting for him. Its branches looked like the others in the orchard. What distinguished it, according to the physician who had prepared it, could not be photographed or weighed: an invisible force called animal magnetism.

There was one condition. The boy would not be told which tree it was.

In 1784, at Benjamin Franklin's house in Passy, near Paris, commissioners investigating animal magnetism arranged a test involving a boy brought by physician Charles d'Eslon (spelled Deslon in the English translation). D'Eslon directed his supposed magnetic influence toward an apricot tree while the boy waited elsewhere. The boy, his eyes covered, was then taken not to that tree but to four others. By the fourth, commissioners recorded that he had fallen into a convulsive episode. He had not been presented with the tree designated as magnetized.

It is a memorable scene, and therefore a dangerous one to recount carelessly. The boy's distress was sufficiently serious that he was carried away and attended to. That does not make the purported magnetic fluid real. Conversely, one episode in one orchard cannot establish that every unusual human experience has the same cause.

The commissioners' question was narrower and more durable: **does the outcome follow the alleged treatment, or does it follow the person's expectation of it?** Their report offers a way of asking the question. It does not furnish an all-purpose answer to everything later called hypnosis. [1][2]

## Paris had already seen the spectacle

Franz Anton Mesmer's account of animal magnetism proposed a universal influence that could be directed through the body and supposedly relieve illness. Treatments involved touch, gestures, rods and public gatherings. Reports described sensations, altered behavior and sometimes dramatic physical episodes called crises.

The royal inquiry was not merely an abstract discussion of ideas. The French king appointed physicians in March 1784 to examine animal magnetism as practised by Charles d'Eslon. Members of the Academy of Sciences joined them. Among the commissioners were Franklin, Antoine-Laurent Lavoisier, Jean-Sylvain Bailly and Joseph-Ignace Guillotin. One appointed physician died early in the inquiry and was replaced. These details are set out in the commission's own report, not inferred from the familiar image of Franklin single-handedly exposing Mesmer. [1]

That distinction matters. The commissioners examined d'Eslon's practice. They did not systematically examine every later form of hypnosis. The shorthand phrase 'Franklin's experiment' can obscure the collaborative investigation and its actual scope.

The commissioners tried the methods themselves. Their private observations contrasted with the public sessions they described. That difference prompted controlled comparisons designed to separate a supposed physical agent from the sight of the practitioner, anticipation of a result and cues supplied through questioning.

For a claimant, an invisible cause poses a special difficulty. It must do more than explain any sensation after the fact. It should make a difference that can be found when expectation and the alleged treatment are separated.

## What the orchard report actually says

The orchard episode has an almost theatrical composition. One apricot tree was selected and treated. The subject was kept apart. D'Eslon requested to remain present at a distance, maintaining gestures toward the treated tree, so the commissioners took precautions intended to prevent signaling. The boy's eyes were covered. He was asked to embrace each of four other trees for approximately two minutes.

The report's narrative describes mounting sensations at the first three trees: perspiration, coughing, headache and stupor. At the fourth, still not the one d'Eslon had treated, the boy fell into what the commissioners called a crisis. The report explicitly notes that the fourth tree had **not** received the operation. [1, pp. 66–69 in the 1785 English printing]

The recorded sequence challenges one straightforward prediction: that this subject would reliably recognize a designated treated tree by its special influence under the test conditions. A defender might reply that other trees were naturally magnetic, that the operator's presence spread the influence or that exposure accumulated as he moved. The report records d'Eslon offering a version of the first two objections.

But if any tree can produce the effect regardless of treatment, the experiment can no longer distinguish the treated tree from its neighbors. A theory that can be adjusted to predict every possible outcome has surrendered its test.

Other comparisons in the report varied whether participants knew that treatment was supposedly occurring. One woman was made to expect treatment when none was performed. In another instance an operation was concealed. The commissioners interpreted the broader pattern as support for expectation and imagination rather than a demonstrated physical fluid. There is no reason to recreate the distressing procedures to appreciate the question they asked. [1, pp. 70–84]

## An extraordinary source is not a modern trial

Calling the orchard episode a controlled comparison is reasonable if control means that the proposed causal factor was withheld or concealed. Calling it a modern randomized, double-blind clinical trial would be inaccurate.

We do not have preregistration, a documented random allocation scheme, standardized outcome measurement, a large statistical sample or an operator fully blinded to treatment. D'Eslon remained nearby. Order effects, repeated questioning, prior beliefs and social circumstances may have influenced the boy. The subject was a child and the reported crisis raises serious ethical questions by present-day standards.

The surviving narrative is the commissioners' account of their own investigation. Historians can read it, compare editions, ask who controlled the narrative and examine other contemporary accounts. They cannot go back to measure the child's expectations or recover a complete recording.

These limitations do not rehabilitate a supposed magnetic fluid. They put the historical record in its proper frame. The episode can challenge an attribution without supplying a complete physiological explanation of each behavior.

The most valuable part of the story is not that famous men arrived with a correct answer. They asked what observation could make the proposed explanation wrong.

## What 'imagination' meant—and what it did not prove

The English translation repeatedly uses the word imagination. Today that word can carry an accusation: the patient imagined the symptom, so the patient lied. This does not follow.

The commissioners wrote about bodily reactions that they treated as observable phenomena. Their challenge concerned the claimed source. A person can sincerely experience an effect, and a practitioner can observe it, while both draw an unsupported conclusion about the mechanism.

Contemporary suggestion research examines how expectancy, interpretation, motivation and individual differences shape responses. In an experimental comparison of hypnotic and nonhypnotic suggestions, researchers reported support for the roles of imaginative suggestibility and response expectancies in the groups they tested. That work is not a retroactive diagnosis of the boy in the orchard. It helps explain why expectation is a legitimate scientific variable, not another word for fraud. [4]

The eighteenth-century commission also discussed touch, imitation and social setting. Its broadest statements about imagination should be read against the narrower observations on which they were based. Evidence does not gain strength when a researcher exaggerates its reach.

## Another story was beginning in 1784

The same year is central to Final Vestige's published [Puységur Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/).

Armand-Marie-Jacques de Chastenet, Marquis de Puységur, worked in the magnetic tradition but described what became known as magnetic somnambulism. He shifted attention from Mesmer's dramatic crises toward a seemingly quiet and responsive state. Later historians treated those reports as an important part of the prehistory of hypnotism.

Adam Crabtree's 2019 study reconstructs Puységur's 1784 publications and the political and social setting of the change. It describes a turn toward psychological explanation, not independent proof that a universal magnetic fluid existed. [3]

It is tempting to collapse the two events into one verdict. The commission examined d'Eslon's claimed agent and procedures. Puységur's recorded experiences and proposed explanations developed partly in parallel. A negative test of the magnetic fluid was not a controlled replication of every claim in Puységur's memoirs. Nor do meticulously cataloged historical records establish extraordinary diagnostic powers.

Both cases, however, ask the same durable questions: What did a witness report? What did a practitioner propose as the cause? Which observation could distinguish that explanation from a competing one?

## A modern reason to care

A contemporary audio program might claim that special tones automatically move the brain into a deep state, or that a short track supplies an hour's worth of meditation benefits. The right response is not to mock the listener. A recording might be pleasant, provide a predictable pause or help establish a routine.

Enjoyment, expectation, reported relaxation, measured brain activity and lasting health improvements are different outcomes. Evidence about one does not automatically validate the others. Research about meditation broadly is not a product-specific trial of any one commercial audio.

This is why a historical investigation and a purchasing guide should remain separate. This article asks what happened at Passy and how the commissioners reasoned. It does **not** endorse or refute a modern meditation product.

The useful inheritance from the eighteenth century is a habit of comparison: identify what the mechanism predicts, identify a credible alternative and ask how the observation changes when the subject cannot tell which condition is present.

## Try the distinction yourself, safely

No blindfolds, physical provocations, or tests on another person are necessary. Take a piece of paper and draw two columns: **What I observed** and **What I think caused it**.

Think of an ordinary moment when expectation changed an interpretation: a phone vibration that turned out not to be a notification, a name misheard in background conversation, or a sound that seemed more soothing after an enthusiastic description. Write only what you are comfortable recording.

In the first column, describe the sensory or behavioral event as plainly as possible. In the second, list at least two explanations. Ask what safe, ordinary observation might distinguish them. If the event was a suspected phone vibration, *I checked my pocket* is an observation; *the phone vibrated* is a claim that could be compared with the notification log.

The exercise does not establish a scientific theory of phantom vibrations. It trains a distinction. Experience can be sincere even when the first explanation turns out to be wrong.

## What remains open

The 1784 commissioners reported a severe reaction during exposure to a tree that had not received the designated magnetic treatment. Their broader inquiry found no need to invoke the alleged fluid for the observed effects. Their report is strong historical evidence for their methods, testimony and interpretation. [1][2]

It does not show that all hypnosis is deception. It does not test every magnetic practitioner. It does not supply a complete modern account of expectation. It does not substantiate or rule out a commercial meditation product launched centuries later.

The finest ending is quieter than a miracle or a debunking. In Franklin's garden, investigators attempted to make an invisible explanation answer to a visible difference between a treated tree and an untreated one.

The experience survived the comparison. The explanation did not.
