# The Choice You Never Made
*Why the mind can explain a decision that somebody else made for it*

Two jars sit side by side at a supermarket tasting table. One holds a jam with a soft, familiar sweetness. The other tastes sharper. You take a little of each. You point to the one you prefer. The person conducting the tasting asks you to explain what makes your selection better. But there has been a substitution: the spoon in front of you contains the jam you **didn't** choose. Can you tell?

That isn't the setup for a reality show. In a 2010 field experiment, Lars Hall, Petter Johansson and colleagues created a tasting station where shoppers sampled pairs of jams and teas. They covertly changed which sample participants received when asked to taste their "chosen" option again. Across the manipulated trials, **no more than one-third were detected**. The investigators also tested combinations whose tastes or smells differed markedly; the degree of detection depended on the conditions and was not zero. [1]

What makes the finding arresting is not simply that somebody missed a switch. It is what happened afterward: people could offer sensible explanations for their apparent preference. Ordinary language, ordinarily our instrument for explaining our own motives, became a way to give the substituted outcome a plausible history.

The researchers call the phenomenon **choice blindness**. It reveals a narrow but consequential gap between making a choice, seeing the reported result of that choice, and having access to the exact reasons behind it.

## First, choose a face

The work began before the jam table. In 2005, Johansson, Hall, Sverker Sikström and Andreas Olsson reported an experiment in *Science* involving pairs of photographs of faces. Participants picked the face they found more attractive. Using a covert manipulation, the experimenters sometimes presented the **unchosen** picture as if it were the participant's selection. Some participants failed to recognize the mismatch; when asked why they had chosen that face, they supplied reasons. [2]

The details matter. The conclusion is **not** that every person was fooled, or that every judgment is arbitrary. A successful manipulation requires a particular environment: a task, a break between action and apparent result, an opportunity to substitute the outcome, and some social plausibility. It is the studied failure of mismatch detection under those conditions that is established—not a general theorem that humans never know what they want.

Imagine the sequence as three separate records.

**Record one: intention.** A particular image attracts you. You decide for it.

**Record two: outcome.** The researcher hands you an image. It is not the image you indicated.

**Record three: explanation.** You find yourself giving a reason for the image in your hand. The explanation may be thoughtful, coherent, and fluent.

We tend to assume the third record is a direct printout of the first. The experiment gives a reason to doubt that assumption in some circumstances.

The mistake is to turn a fascinating specific result into an unqualified claim about the self. Introspection can be informative; memory can be useful; people can also detect substitutions. The point is that confidence in an explanation is not necessarily evidence that the explanation accurately reconstructs the choice that preceded it.

## The supermarket was an important change of scene

Laboratory demonstrations can be dismissed as card tricks. The jam-and-tea work moved the same question into a routine consumer setting, where participants had actual sensory material to compare. The experimenters were not merely asking participants to endorse sentences on a screen. People could taste or smell the items. Then the experimenters swapped what they presented as the earlier selection. [1]

This makes the result an especially sharp observation about how choice and justification can become disconnected. If you had chosen the sweeter jam, then received the sourer one without recognizing it, you might suddenly discover an apparent personal preference for its tartness. The language of preference can be generated in response to the object at hand rather than simply retrieved from an untouched internal record.

Again, the study does not establish that the changed choice would persist as a lifelong preference. It does not show that a salesperson can reliably overwrite people's preferences, or that an advertisement can force someone to buy. It does show that, in a defined experimental context, a surprisingly high share of altered outcomes went unreported.

The distinction between an experiment and a marketing prophecy is central. Claims like "science proves customers can be made to believe anything" are not supported by these studies.

## An answer can change its clothing, too

In 2012, Hall, Johansson and Thomas Strandberg extended choice blindness into a more sensitive domain: moral attitudes. Participants completed a survey. Through a paper-based sleight of hand, some responses were changed to express the opposite of what had been written. A majority of the alterations were undetected; **69 percent of participants failed to detect at least one of two changes**, and some participants reasoned in support of positions at odds with their original answers. [3]

The result warrants care. It does not mean moral beliefs are empty or that no one holds a stable principle. Nor does it give anyone license to alter or misrepresent a person's stated beliefs. Rather, it demonstrates how the *presentation of one's own position* can influence the argument one constructs about it.

An ethical study may reveal a vulnerability without authorizing its exploitation. The fact that some people rationalize a substituted answer is a reason to make records and consent more reliable, not an invitation to disguise what other people said.

A 2013 study went further, testing altered responses on political questions during an election setting. The authors reported that only a minority of manipulated answers were detected and participants often accepted altered survey summaries. [4] As with the earlier experiments, this does not establish durable voting changes or offer a general-purpose recipe for political persuasion. It tests reactions inside a particular intervention.

Taken together, these studies offer a careful conclusion: the sense that "I have always meant this" can sometimes be built using information presented **after** a response is made.

## What choice blindness is not

It is not change blindness, though the concepts are neighbors. Change blindness concerns failures to notice changes in a visual scene. Choice blindness concerns failures to notice a mismatch between a selected option and the option subsequently presented as selected. Both invite attention to what we compare, but one is not simply a synonym for the other. Final Vestige's [Change Blindness investigation](https://finalvestige.com/2026/11/02/change-blindness/) describes the related visual-perception problem.

It is also not hypnosis. In the choice-blindness experiments above, covert alteration of an outcome—not induction into a hypnotic state—was central to the design. It would be misleading to cite those findings as proof that hypnotic audio can implant preferences, or as validation of any paid self-improvement product.

And it is not a test of whether you are smart. Substitution detection varies with task structure, conspicuousness, memory, individual priorities and context. The studies investigate patterns in the conditions they created. Individual performance on a casual demonstration is not a diagnosis of gullibility.

## An exercise without deception

There is a way to explore the gap between preference and explanation without secretly changing another person's choices.

Take a sheet of paper and write down a small decision before you act: perhaps which of two covers you prefer for a notebook, which of two routes you would take to a familiar place, or which of two short essays interests you more. Write **one sentence of explanation before choosing**.

Now make the choice. Put the original sheet away. After a day, write down why you remember choosing that option. Compare the two explanations.

You may find the reasons unchanged. You may find you've forgotten one reason and added another. Either result is worthwhile. This is **not** a replication of the research, because it involves no controlled substitution, no random assignment and no measurement of choice blindness. It is an ordinary way to inspect the difference between a record made at decision time and a retrospective story.

The most interesting question is not whether you can catch yourself being wrong. It is which parts of your explanation were already there, which arose later, and which cannot be known from the exercise.

## The historian's dilemma: a beautiful explanation is not a record

Choice blindness belongs naturally in a studio devoted to historical evidence.

A nineteenth-century witness may tell a vivid story about a séance, a magnetic treatment or a moment of revelation. Historians need to distinguish what the witness reported at the time, what was recorded by an observer, and what was reconstructed years later. An internally persuasive story does not automatically establish an external mechanism.

That tension runs through [Puységur's Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/). The records of mesmerism and suggestion are not worthless because eyewitnesses might err. They are valuable *because* they are records that can be examined, contextualized and compared. A witness's sincerity, the timing of a report, and the adequacy of independent corroboration are separate questions.

Choice blindness reminds us of something subtle: the mind does not always lie when it is mistaken. A convincing explanation may be a good-faith attempt to account for an outcome that has already been misidentified.

That is why careful documentation matters outside laboratories too. We can preserve original responses, distinguish contemporaneous evidence from later recollection, and resist substituting a polished narrative for a traceable observation.

## A purchasing lesson that isn't an advertisement

There is one practical implication worth keeping even when no product is on offer. When you consider a costly or consequential purchase, record what you actually want **before** reading a sales page. List the expected features, the evidence you would need, the maximum amount you would willingly pay, and your reason for considering the purchase.

After viewing the pitch, compare the sales-page claims with that original record. Did the offer meet your requirements—or did your explanation of what you "always wanted" shift to match what was presented?

That is a decision hygiene exercise, not an experimentally validated shield against manipulation. It merely protects a contemporaneous note of your preferences from being overwritten by a later story.

It also sets an editorial standard for us: where a seller makes a dramatic claim about the mind, we must not transform an engaging demonstration into proof for that claim.

## The moment of uncertainty

The most memorable result of the original face experiment was that participants could describe reasons for a face they had not selected. There is something almost comic about it, until the comedy turns familiar. Everyone has eventually found themselves explaining a choice they remember less clearly than the explanation itself.

The research offers no reason to treat every explanation with suspicion. It offers something more useful: a reason to distinguish **confidence** from **provenance**.

What happened? What did I choose? When did I make that choice? What evidence exists apart from the smoothness of my account? Those are questions worth asking wherever claims must be tested—at a supermarket table, in an archival record, and in the quiet moment before someone asks you to buy.
