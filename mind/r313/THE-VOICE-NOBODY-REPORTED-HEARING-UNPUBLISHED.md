# The Gorilla Voice Some Listeners Missed

**Final Vestige Studios · Mind & Perception Archive**  
**Editorial state: unpublished, original source-review draft · November 12, 2026**

*For nineteen seconds, a voice moved through a laboratory conversation announcing something absurd. Some listeners missed it. The most interesting question is not why they failed to hear, but what the experiment actually measured.*

## An occupied room

You hear two conversations at once. One is yours to follow. The other is present, audible, but not the conversation you have chosen. Now a third voice moves through the scene and repeatedly announces that it is a gorilla.

It is an almost ridiculous intrusion. It is also the kind of interruption people expect they could never miss. In 2012, psychologists Polly Dalton and Nick Fraenkel turned that expectation into an experiment. Their paper, *Gorillas we have missed: sustained inattentional deafness for dynamic events*, published in *Cognition*, described a conspicuous auditory event embedded in a lifelike, spatially arranged sound scene. The intruder was audible for **nineteen seconds**. Even so, some listeners failed to report it. [1]

The experiment was not about an inaudible sound. Nor was it evidence that listeners had damaged hearing, that the brain switched off the auditory system, or that every distracted person would miss the same event. Its central finding was that the presence of a sound and its arrival in conscious report can come apart.

The word *report* matters. It is the difference between an experimental result and a slogan.

## The voice crosses attention

The researchers' original indexed abstract establishes the nineteen-second event and its striking failure to reach some listeners' awareness. A later published article about auditory surprise in music describes the setup more closely: two concurrent conversations, women speaking in one stream and men in another. An unexpected male voice moved through the auditory scene, repeatedly announcing that it was a gorilla. Participants were assigned to follow either the female or male conversation. [1, 2]

That later account reports that around **70 percent** of listeners attending the female conversation failed to notice the gorilla voice, compared with around **10 percent** who attended the male conversation. This is a **secondary published account** of the 2012 result: the freely visible original PubMed abstract does not itself provide those numerical rates. We did not independently audit participant-level data, and we should not present these percentages as a general population estimate. [2]

The difference between the two streams is revealing. The intruder resembled the male voices more closely than the female ones. When a voice falls nearer the attended category, it may be more likely to enter awareness. But even that is a hypothesis grounded in a particular design, not an instruction manual for human hearing. The task, sound field, language, attention direction and post-event question all influence what can responsibly be inferred.

Consider how easily the result could be distorted online. One edit might say that 70 percent of people are deaf to obvious sounds. Another might claim that hearing improves if you listen to a particular meditation track. Neither conclusion was tested. The original question was narrower and more valuable: can a long, salient auditory event be missed when a listener attends a competing task?

The answer, under these experimental conditions, was yes.

## The second experiment asks a different question

A single startling intrusion creates a problem for measuring detection. When someone says they did not notice a sound, was the signal weakly perceived? Was it masked? Did they set a conservative threshold for saying yes? Did they notice and forget? These are different mechanisms.

In 2015, Dana Raveh and Nilli Lavie published *Load-induced inattentional deafness* in *Attention, Perception, & Psychophysics*. Their open-access article reports **four experiments** examining whether a demanding visual task can reduce sensitivity to an auditory tone. Unlike the moving conversational gorilla, this task involved visual letter searches of varying difficulty alongside brief sounds presented on some trials. [3]

In the first experiment, a participant looked for an X or N among six characters. The easy display used smaller Os as distractors. The harder display used several more similar letters. A brief tone sometimes occurred against background noise while participants worked. Other experiments changed the order of responses, the probability of tone presentation and other features intended to probe whether changes reflected genuine detection sensitivity rather than a mere willingness to respond.

The authors concluded that higher visual perceptual load consistently reduced auditory detection sensitivity in their tasks. Their paper reports an average decrease of 26 percent in auditory detection accuracy across the four experiments, alongside a reduction in their sensitivity measure. Those are measurements from specified laboratory paradigms. They do not predict a 26 percent decrease in an individual's day-to-day hearing, and they cannot tell you whether a particular car alarm will go unheard. [3]

The deeper point is cross-modal: directing attention to vision can make a difference to what gets registered in hearing. Eyes and ears do not operate as separate all-access recording machines whenever a person concentrates.

A major strength of the 2015 work is that it does not merely ask after one surprising event whether it was noticed. Repeated tone-present and tone-absent trials allow the researchers to examine sensitivity, including false alarms and the threshold for reporting a tone. That is an improvement in measurement, not a proof of every proposed mechanism.

## Hearing, noticing, remembering

Ordinary language compresses several questions into the phrase, "I never heard that." The acoustic signal may be present and detectable under full attention while the listener fails to identify or later report it. Sometimes masking and noise matter. Sometimes attention direction matters. Sometimes an experimental question itself changes what people are willing to report.

Studies of surprise therefore deserve a skeptical reading. What exactly was played? Were participants told to attend one stream? Did they know a sound might appear? Did the researchers measure detection across many trials or ask one question afterward? Were samples and denominators clearly reported?

A 2014 experiment on inattentional deafness in music provides another useful case. Participants were occupied with a task involving timpani beats while an unexpected electric-guitar solo entered a musical excerpt. The published abstract reports two analyzed study samples of 115 and 47 people and observes inattentional deafness even among musicians. [4] This is an interesting comparison, not a replication of the same auditory scene or evidence that musical expertise never matters.

The studies differ in stimulus, task, response and experimental purpose. Their common lesson is about limits on selective awareness; they are not interchangeable data points for claiming one universal percentage of missed sounds.

## A historical witness is also a selective recorder

Our published [Puységur's Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) preserve another kind of evidence problem. In the late eighteenth century, Puységur recorded testimony, apparent cures, failures, predictions, refusal and warnings about operator pressure as he built a case for animal magnetism. The Final Vestige archive contains 153 source rows and sixteen case dossiers, including material that undercuts the claims its historical author wanted to advance. [5]

This is **not** the same phenomenon as inattentional deafness. The Puységur record does not scientifically anticipate auditory attention research. Instead, it raises a comparable research-method question: who controlled the situation in which a report was produced, which observations were preserved, and which alternative explanations were left open?

An operator who expects magnetic power, a listener instructed to follow one conversation, and a researcher measuring sound detection occupy different roles. But their reports all depend on instructions, questions and documentation. Evidence can be preserved without treating a witness's interpretation as proof of its mechanism.

That distinction protects us from two equally easy errors. One is to dismiss historical testimony because its explanation sounds strange. The other is to embrace the explanation simply because it was written down. A source trail can be genuine while the cause it proposes remains unestablished.

## What to try without pretending to replicate an experiment

In a quiet, safe place, write down the ordinary sounds you notice for one minute. Next, spend a minute reading a challenging passage while the environment remains unchanged. When you finish, list the ambient sounds you remember hearing during the reading minute.

Compare the lists without scoring yourself. You may notice a difference or none at all. There is no deception, no planted surprise, no standardized sound level, no independent ground truth and no control condition. **This is not a replication of the Dalton/Fraenkel or Raveh/Lavie studies**, and it cannot measure auditory health or attention capacity. Do not perform the exercise while driving, crossing traffic, monitoring a child, operating machinery or relying on any safety alert.

## What this has to do with meditation audio—and what it does not

Some sellers use attention science to argue for audio programs. The legitimate initial buyer question is usually more modest: do you want a structured short listening routine, and what files would you receive?

Inspire3's Zen12 sales pages describe twelve levels with four listening styles per level and a complete-course checkout listing forty-eight MP3 files. Hypnosis Live's separate Total Focus page describes a thirty-three-minute MP3 session. Brainwave Shots' Focus Shot page describes fifteen- and thirty-minute versions. These are **merchant-described package features**. They are not proof of improved attention, treatment of anxiety or ADHD, restored memory, or any clinical effect. [6–8]

The science of binaural beats offers no simple shortcut. A 2023 systematic review of fourteen eligible studies on binaural-beat entrainment and brain oscillations found five supportive findings, eight contradictory findings and one mixed outcome. The authors emphasized heterogeneity and inconsistent evidence. A different 2022 review/meta-analysis of fifteen studies reported a positive pooled cognitive effect while also documenting mixed and conflicting findings. Neither synthesis tested these particular Inspire3 products. [9, 10]

It would be equally misleading to claim that all audio methods have been disproven. The research questions, stimuli and outcomes are varied. The evidence-supported conclusion for this buyer guide is that a given product's claimed benefits need **its own** rigorous substantiation before we repeat them.

## The record that remains

The auditory gorilla is memorable because it makes an almost childish boast in a place where people expect to hear everything worth hearing. Yet the strongest version of this story is not the boast. It is the measurement: a nineteen-second intruder, attention assigned to a competing conversation, different recognition rates for different listening streams, and later experiments asking whether load changes detection sensitivity itself.

When a witness says nothing unusual happened, the answer may tell us about what reached their attention, not everything present in the room. An investigator's job is to preserve that uncertainty rather than cover it with a convenient explanation.

What is missing from the report may be as important as what remains.
