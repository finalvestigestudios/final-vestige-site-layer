Before you buy twelve minutes of quiet

An unexpected voice crossed a laboratory sound scene for nineteen seconds, yet some listeners never reported it. That is evidence about selective attention, not that a commercial audio track fixes your focus. If a short listening routine appeals to you, compare what Zen12 actually sells with a free twelve-minute alternative, then decide whether the format—not an unsupported promise—is worth paying for.

This is a source-based product-format guide, not a personal testimonial, a diagnosis, or an assurance of any cognitive or medical benefit.

What the exact seller actually describes

The Zen12 complete-course checkout lists twelve levels and forty-eight MP3 recordings, together with supplementary guides and support descriptions. Individual sessions are marketed as twelve-minute listening periods. These are the merchant's package descriptions.

Zen12 also claims a short session can equal the benefits of a substantially longer meditation practice. That is a seller efficacy claim, not independently established for Zen12 by the research reviewed here. A 2023 systematic review of fourteen binaural-beat entrainment experiments reported five supporting studies, eight contradictory studies and one mixed result. Another synthesis of fifteen studies found a positive pooled effect for some cognitive outcomes but mixed results between studies. Neither tested the specific audio files Zen12 sells. Do not treat the general science or an auditory-gorilla experiment as verified Zen12 efficacy.

Neither seller's cognitive-benefit language is independent proof of effectiveness, and the packages should not be mixed up.

The experiment behind the question

Dalton and Fraenkel's 2012 indexed original paper describes a nineteen-second auditory intruder that listeners could miss. Raveh and Lavie's open-access 2015 experiments separate perceptual-load effects on hearing from mere willingness to report. A 2023 binaural-beat systematic review found inconsistent entrainment results; a 2022 cognitive meta-analysis also notes conflicting studies. None tested Zen12 or verified its advertised outcomes.
