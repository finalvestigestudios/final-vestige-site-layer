# The Pendulum That Moved Without a Ghost
*Final Vestige Studios · Mind & Perception · source-controlled manuscript · November 18, 2026 · NOT PUBLISHED*

**Dek:** A weighted string can seem to answer a question without its holder deciding to move it. The movement is real. The supposed message is another matter. From the nineteenth-century laboratory to modern experiments in hypnotic agency, this is the history of a mistake that feels persuasive from the inside.

## The answer hanging by a thread

Imagine a small weight hanging from a length of thread. Someone holds the other end, lets the weight settle, and concentrates on a direction. Before long it may trace a tiny ellipse. The holder looks at their hand. It does not seem to be moving. Yet something has moved the weight.

That gap—between an action and the feeling of authoring it—makes the pendulum such a powerful prop. It has appeared in demonstrations of unconscious movement, claims about invisible forces, and attempts to extract knowledge from a person who says they do not know an answer. A pendulum is not proof of spirits, concealed intelligence or therapeutic power. It is a revealing example of how attention, expectation and bodily feedback can interact.

The crucial distinction is between three observations: the pendulum moved; the person experienced the motion as unchosen; and the motion encoded something objectively true. The first two may be reportable without the third following at all.

In Final Vestige's historical work, this distinction has an uncomfortable counterpart. A witness can sincerely describe an extraordinary experience, and a conscientious investigator can preserve the testimony in exquisite detail, without either person having identified the underlying mechanism.

## What the old experiment actually tested

In 1976, Randolph Easton and Ronald Shor published *An Experimental Analysis of the Chevreul Pendulum Illusion*. They studied sixty college students and used time-exposure photography to quantify the movement of hand-suspended pendulums during imaginative concentration. Their summary reports that motion varied with divided attention, the muscles involved in suspending the pendulum, and external oscillating audiovisual stimuli. Visual stimulus was more influential than auditory stimulus in their comparison.

That design matters because it asks a measurable question rather than an occult one: under what circumstances does the effect grow or diminish? The method does not require a claim that a subject was lying about feeling still. Nor does it require granting the pendulum independent agency. Small muscular changes can be amplified into a visible swing, while the subjective sense of deciding to move may remain weak or absent.

Their 1977 follow-up investigated augmented and delayed feedback. In two experiments, they showed that feedback conditions changed pendulum behavior. Delayed visual feedback disrupted performance while delayed auditory feedback did not have the same effect in their tasks. The authors treated the pendulum effect as a sensorimotor behavior open to investigation, not a supernatural instrument.

These studies do not mean every pendulum movement has the same cause or that no other physical influence could ever exist. They do show why a bare demonstration is weak evidence: a person, the surrounding stimuli and the feedback loop are part of the apparatus. Unless those are controlled, the movement alone cannot establish where an answer came from.

## The test that did not give the pendulum an advantage

A more recent experiment asks an especially tempting question. Could an ideomotor method reveal correct answers that a person's verbal report cannot access?

Gyorgy Moga and Zoltan Dienes published *Expressing unconscious general knowledge using Chevreul's pendulum* in 2022. They attempted to adapt a procedure previously associated with Ouija-board research. Their critical comparison concerned general-knowledge questions that participants had rated as guesses. A special advantage for the pendulum would have been important: it might indicate that this kind of action accesses information unavailable to ordinary reporting.

That is not what the study found. In the reported experiment, ideomotor answers and later verbal answers were similarly accurate, at about chance level on the selected guessed questions. The authors explicitly cautioned against assuming that an ideomotor action will reveal more knowledge than a verbal answer in a given situation, including a hypnotherapy setting.

This is the counterexample that a compelling story often leaves out. A pendulum can move without feeling voluntary. The experience can be striking. But the movement need not carry hidden knowledge. A psychological explanation of *why a response occurs* is not independent evidence that the *content of the response is correct*.

The paper does not rule out every form of unconscious information processing. It rules out the stronger inference from the effect to privileged knowledge in the conditions tested.

**Source:** Moga & Dienes (2022), *American Journal of Clinical Hypnosis*, 64(4), 306–315, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35184700/

## “It happened” and “I made it happen” are different questions

Consider two observations again. A participant raises an arm, and an observer records the movement. The participant reports that the arm moved by itself. Both can be accurately reported. Neither settles exactly how the participant's sense of agency was produced.

A 2004 study by Patrick Haggard and colleagues compared voluntary, passive and hypnotically suggested finger movements. The time judgments accompanying a movement suggested as involuntary resembled those associated with passive action more than those accompanying ordinary voluntary movement. That result does not imply outside control. It shows that suggestion can change measurable features associated with the experience of control.

In a 2012 study, Paul Cunningham and Philip Ramos found a mismatch between outward performance on hypnotic test suggestions and participants' reported involuntariness: some who met behavioral criteria described acting voluntarily, while some who failed behavioral criteria described involuntary experience. A performed task and a particular inner feeling cannot safely be collapsed into the same measurement.

A 2013 exploratory fMRI study examined fifteen highly hypnotically suggestible people. Its association between reported loss of control and altered connectivity among movement-related brain systems offers a possible physiological account for aspects of these experiences. But its carefully selected sample and experimental setting do not authorize sweeping claims about everyone who hears a hypnosis recording.

More recently, a paper published May 18, 2026, in *Neuroscience of Consciousness* combined EEG, physiological measurements and first-person reports in hypnotic induction and an ideomotor challenge. It described differing movement strategies despite comparable reports of disruption to agency. It is a specialized experimental study, not a clinical trial of a commercial audio course. Its value lies in treating subjective reports and recorded behavior as related but nonidentical evidence.

## The eighteenth-century investigator who saved the contradiction

Long before these laboratory methods, Armand-Marie-Jacques de Chastenet, Marquis de Puységur, documented patients and witnesses involved in what he called magnetic somnambulism. His surviving volumes present predictions, recorded speech, signed statements and episodes of apparent rapport. They also preserve uncertainty, failed predictions, patient vulnerability and concerns about the operator's influence.

The purpose of linking that archive to pendulum research is not to retroactively diagnose a historical subject or explain every incident with one present-day term. We cannot replay Puységur's rooms under modern controls. Nor can a twenty-first-century study certify a fluid or force claimed two centuries earlier.

The archival bridge is a method. In the **Puységur Animal Magnetism Files**, Final Vestige keeps the failed prediction by Louis de Rieux alongside the documentation of the test. It examines Charlotte's protests and the problem of listening to what a vulnerable subject says under a powerful operator. The original writings contain both remarkable assertions and reasons not to trust every answer an operator wanted to receive.

A signed statement can improve the provenance of an observation. It does not, by itself, validate the suggested mechanism. A demonstrator may be sincere. A subject may be sincere. Both may be mistaken about causation. These possibilities are not accusations; they are ordinary requirements of evidence.

## A small experiment in how to keep yourself honest

You need no pendulum, audio app, hypnotic induction or signup for this exercise.

For twelve minutes, choose a strong claim from a video, a product page or a historic witness statement. Draw three columns on paper: **observed**, **experienced**, **inferred**. Put only things directly recorded in the first column; someone saying “the weight moved” belongs there if the recording exists. Put “the holder felt no deliberate motion” under reported experience, with a note about whose report it is. Put “a hidden force knew the answer” under inference.

Next, ask what control could separate two possible explanations. Would recording the hand help? Would masking the suggested direction help? Would the answer need a predetermined scoring rule? Would you want a larger sample and a comparison group? Do not write a dramatic conclusion: write the minimum observation that could disprove it.

Finally look for the failure the storyteller kept. If you cannot find one, note *missing contrary evidence* rather than quietly inventing a success rate. This is an exercise in source appraisal, not a diagnostic or therapeutic procedure.

## Where consumer meditation audio enters—and where it does not

A reader interested in suggestion may reasonably be interested in audio-guided relaxation. These are different questions. Some people prefer a structured recording to sitting in silence; some prefer a timer, a walk, or no audio at all. The fact that suggestion can influence experienced agency does not demonstrate that a named retail product will improve cognition, eliminate anxiety, produce a hypnotic response, or match a long meditation session.

Inspire3 describes its Zen12 product as short audio sessions offered in multiple styles and levels. That is a seller description of a product format. The seller's stronger claims about automatic brainwave entrainment and guaranteed benefits require independent product-specific substantiation. A 2023 systematic review of fourteen binaural-beat EEG studies found inconsistent results across heterogeneous experimental designs. It did not test Zen12, and the review must not be marketed as proof that Zen12 works or does not work.

For the reader considering any paid audio, a useful first step is a twelve-minute no-purchase baseline and a plain comparison of usability, evidence, accessibility, refund terms and cost at the *actual* checkout. ## What the swinging weight can—and cannot—tell us

The pendulum is a better question than an answer. Its movement can be measured. The holder's experience can be taken seriously. The supposed hidden message still needs a genuinely independent test. Confusing these three is how an ordinary feedback effect becomes an extraordinary claim.

That is also a useful rule for reading testimony from another century. Preserve the witness's words. Preserve the records and the failures. Never quietly turn a sincere report into a proven cause.
