# The Second Target Disappeared Between Two Letters
*Final Vestige Studios · Mind & Perception · Original unpublished investigation · November 11, 2026 · Editorial review required*

**Deck:** A person watches a rapid series of letters. The first important symbol appears and is correctly identified. A second symbol follows, yet may never enter the report. The attentional blink is an experimentally measured gap between presentation and awareness—not evidence that a purchased recording can make the gap disappear.

## The letter on the screen

Imagine a laboratory monitor that doesn't show a sentence. The whole display is a single square, and characters flash at its center. An R becomes a T; a number interrupts the letters; then another character takes its place. You have been told to identify one specific target and then watch for another. You are sitting still. You are looking directly at the spot where each symbol appears.

You can report the first target. Yet in a particular interval afterward, a second target may go unreported. What makes that finding unsettling is not that your eyes moved away. The sequence was designed to keep the action in one place. The question is what your attention had to do with the first target before the second could receive enough processing to be reported.

The story is not a generalized parable that attention is weak. It is a controlled laboratory result about competing targets, distractors, response requirements and timing. Those details matter because a name as memorable as **attentional blink** makes a precise finding sound like a law of human consciousness.

## The 1992 experiment

Jane Raymond, Kimron Shapiro and Karen Arnell published "Temporary suppression of visual processing in an RSVP task: An attentional blink?" in August 1992. Their participants watched rapid serial visual presentation, or RSVP, in which visual items occupy the same screen position in quick succession. They were to identify a partially specified target letter and then detect a fully specified probe letter.

The paper's indexed abstract reports accurate identification of the first target, but poor detection of probes in a roughly **270-millisecond window beginning 180 milliseconds after that target**. Immediately following targets and later probes were detected more successfully. A brief blank interval after the target could eliminate the deficit, as could removing the requirement to identify the initial target. [1]

This is the first boundary around the claim. The experiment did not show that vision itself shuts down for a fixed portion of every second. Its observation concerned whether participants *detected and later reported a probe* in a defined visual task. The initial target had to be processed, and the materials appearing between target and probe mattered.

The blank interval is especially revealing. It suggests that visual items entering while recognition of the first target is still being completed can interfere with the later probe. Raymond and colleagues discussed recognition-stage interference. Other explanations would develop, but none can ignore what the original controls actually compared.

Because the full article's trial tables were not independently examined for this manuscript, the interval and design statements here are limited to its publicly accessible PubMed abstract. This is not an invented replication or a claim to have tested people at Final Vestige.

## The exception that changed the theory

A straightforward metaphor says attention needs a recovery period after catching the first target. In that account, the earliest second target should be hardest to see. But the laboratory literature often finds the opposite for two consecutive targets: **lag-1 sparing**. A second target appearing immediately after the first may be reported better than another appearing after one or more intervening distractors.

This is not a small technical footnote. It challenges the comforting idea that a one-directional fuel gauge—attention full, then empty, then full—explains the entire phenomenon.

In 2005 Bernhard Hommel and Elkan Akyürek examined the benefit and cost of putting two targets so close together. One experiment required reporting both their identities and the order in which they appeared. Relatively good reporting of the second target at lag 1 could come alongside poorer performance on the first. Another experiment recorded order confusions when the targets were hard to distinguish in relative strength. Their proposed interpretation involved competition and sometimes integration into a single attentional episode: getting two identities into the report can come at the expense of remembering which came first. [2]

A later study by Christian Olivers and colleagues, published in 2007 after appearing online in 2005, went beyond just two targets. When additional targets were presented without intervening distractors, performance could improve remarkably. In some conditions an item following an induced blink could recover if another target preceded it. The authors argued against a simple, purely limited-capacity explanation and highlighted how attentional control can be applied too aggressively. [3]

Neither study establishes that memory has unlimited capacity. Together they indicate that selection strategy, masks and event organization change what is reported. They also tell a creator how *not* to make a dishonest visual: an ordinary animated sequence of symbols is not automatically a faithful reconstruction of a published experiment.

To build a real demonstration, one would need controlled stimulus duration, known refresh behavior, specified target classes, defined distractors, repeated trials, counterbalancing and aggregation across viewers. A social clip has different constraints. Its responsible claim is that this is a phenomenon scientists have demonstrated under laboratory conditions, not that every viewer must fail on command.

## Reporting an event isn't preserving an event

There is a difference between light reaching the retina, a feature being processed, the observer deciding that something matters, the identity entering memory, and a verbal report after the screen clears. A missed probe answer does not identify a single point in that chain without further evidence.

That is why the distinction between two target identities and their temporal order in the Hommel–Akyürek experiments is intriguing. A record can carry genuine details while losing relationships between them. It is also why one should not simply call the result "blindness" in the ordinary medical sense. The participants were not diagnosed with visual impairment; they performed a demanding, temporally structured identification task.

Compare this with Final Vestige's published [Change Blindness investigation](https://finalvestige.com/2026/11/02/change-blindness/). The latter concerns failures to notice changes between views. The attentional blink concerns targets in a rapid stream. Both can involve failures of report, but they differ in procedure and should not be treated as interchangeable synonyms.

Our [Puységur Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) present a more distant historical parallel. Puységur assembled named patients, notebooks, statements, predictions and witnesses as evidence for animal magnetism. The modern archive preserves failed predictions, refusals, source wording and the question of whose account is believed. Those historical documents have evidentiary value about what people recorded, not proof of a magnetic fluid. Likewise, a contemporary lab protocol can support a behavioral observation without proving a favored grand theory of consciousness.

This is where the comparison belongs: **between ways of making evidence**, not between eighteenth-century magnetic cures and twenty-first-century cognitive science. Making the sources sound more similar than they are would repeat the very error the archive is supposed to teach readers to catch.

## A meditation study that marketing can easily distort

The scientific literature does connect attentional blink with some forms of mental training. Heleen Slagter and colleagues reported in a 2007 PLoS Biology study that **three months of intensive mental training** were followed by a smaller attentional blink and reduced P3b electrical response to the first target. The participants showing larger changes in that response generally also showed greater improvement in reporting the second target. [4]

The study's accessible figure descriptions specify 15–19 items in a trial, a letter stream containing two target numbers, individual letters displayed for 50 milliseconds with 34-millisecond intervening blanks, and short versus long target separations of 336 and 672 milliseconds. The paper investigated a particular training context and physiological/performance relationship. It did not measure every ordinary use of the word "focus."

Here is the critical commercial boundary: an intensive training study **did not test a purchased Zen12 MP3, a Hypnosis Live recording, or a Brainwave Shots product**. There is no valid evidentiary substitution in which "three months of intensive mental training" is silently renamed "a daily twelve-minute commercial audio session." The intervention, dose, participant population, comparator and outcome all change.

Another relevant research avenue is measurement itself. A U.S. National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health summary describes a 2020 study that developed brain-decoding measures to distinguish breath attention from wandering or self-referential processing in 16 adults. That preliminary research demonstrated a way to study attentional states; it did not validate any specific consumer recording. [5]

The fact that meditation has been studied is important. It is not a permission slip for an unrelated seller to promise that a customer will cure their distractibility, improve clinical symptoms, or obtain an hour of meditation's benefits from a shorter audio recording.

## A demonstration you can perform without claiming laboratory precision

Take a printed page containing both words and numbers. On a first pass, mark each digit. On a second pass, mark every word containing a chosen letter. Write down what task each instruction made you prioritize and whether you overlooked any items on the first attempt.

This is a useful reflection on how a search rule changes attention. **It is not an attentional-blink test.** It cannot reproduce millisecond RSVP timing, prove a clinical condition, or estimate your personal attentional resources. If a video creator asks you to find targets in a fast montage, enjoy it as an illustration rather than an assessment.

For a second, more practical reflection, write down the one task you have been putting off. Set a twelve-minute timer. Choose a first step small enough to complete or meaningfully begin within that interval. Record what you did. Try a no-audio session and, if you already use an ordinary listening routine, a session with it. Compare your own observations without inventing medical conclusions.

## What stayed on the screen

It is tempting to end with a trick: the brain is blind for a moment, and a special sound or method will restore it. But the second target did not vanish from the display. A participant failed to report it under a specific set of instructions. When the targets were closer together, some second targets became easier to report; when intervening items changed, the result changed with them.

The experiment is more interesting when the controls are kept in the frame. What survives is a precise, reproducible question about how the mind orders its unfinished work. What does *not* follow is an invitation to turn a real research finding into an unverified consumer cure.

**The second symbol remained on the screen. The unanswered question is what was required for it to survive into the report.**
