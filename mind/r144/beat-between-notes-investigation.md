# The Beat Between Two Notes: What Sound Can—and Cannot—Tell Us About the Brain
**Final Vestige Studios · Mind & Perception Archive · Original investigation · UNPUBLISHED REVIEW DRAFT · November 5, 2026**

*An audio illusion is real. The promise attached to it may not be.*

Imagine two steady tones. One reaches your left ear, the other your right. Their pitches differ slightly. Under suitable listening conditions, a listener can experience a third rhythm, as though the sound itself were pulsing. No third tone has been added to either ear. The experience emerges from the way the auditory system deals with information arriving on its two sides.

Now imagine the next sentence in an advertisement: because the ears can produce an apparent beat, a recording must be able to reproduce the mental benefits of a much longer period of meditation.

The first proposition belongs to auditory perception. The second is a claim about outcomes. Between the two lies a chain of questions that cannot be answered simply by pointing at a waveform.

That distance—between observing an effect and asserting a benefit—is where this investigation begins.

## I. An experience that is not in either recording

A *binaural beat* is heard when different steady tones are delivered separately to the two ears. For example, if one ear receives a 220-hertz tone and the other a 230-hertz tone, the listener may perceive rhythmic fluctuation corresponding to the ten-hertz difference. That is an **illustration of the frequency relationship**, not an instruction to generate sounds or a claim that ten hertz reliably produces any particular psychological state.

This is not quite the same as a pulsing sound physically mixed into a single track. When acoustic waves interfere or a recording is deliberately amplitude-modulated, the signal itself contains a changing amplitude. In a classic binaural-beat arrangement, the relevant tones are kept separate and the percept arises centrally. The distinction matters when sellers describe every pulsing soundtrack as the same neurological intervention.

The phenomenon has fascinated researchers partly because it makes a deceptively simple demonstration of the brain's constructive role in hearing. We do not passively receive a perfectly finished version of the world. Different pieces of sensory information interact, and what we experience is a result of that processing.

The illusion also gives marketers a vivid image: sound slips past deliberate effort and causes something automatic to happen. But **automatic perception is not automatic therapeutic benefit**. Hearing a beat is not the same thing as proving that a listening session induces a stable, beneficial brain state—or that the state improves concentration, health, or personal performance.

The right research question is not whether the percept exists. It does. The right question is what reliably follows from exposure, under what conditions, for whom, and compared with which alternative.

## II. The EEG temptation

Electrical activity recorded at the scalp is often analyzed in frequency bands. Investigators discuss broad ranges conventionally labeled delta, theta, alpha, beta and gamma. These labels are useful analytic categories, but they are not on/off switches for emotions or abilities. One person in one room does not have a single sovereign frequency that explains every aspect of attention and feeling.

The *entrainment hypothesis* proposes that rhythmic stimulation can influence ongoing brain activity in a matching frequency range. That is a testable physiological proposition. It is not a blank check for a product to claim that its sound “copies” an expert meditator's brain or replaces a known number of minutes of practice.

For direct verification, researchers might measure brain activity while listeners hear a controlled stimulus, compare it with a credible control condition, describe exactly which EEG feature changes, and assess whether the finding replicates. Even a reproducible difference in an EEG measure would leave a second question: did the listener experience a meaningful improvement? A readout is not a validated outcome.

In 2023, Ruth Maria Ingendoh, Ella S. Posny and Angela Heine examined the core EEG evidence in a systematic review of fourteen studies. **Five studies were consistent with the entrainment hypothesis, eight reported contradictory results, and one had mixed results.** The authors emphasized wide methodological differences in stimulus presentation, design and EEG analysis. Their conclusion was not that binaural beats do nothing; it was that the available experiments were inconsistent and required better standardization. [Ingendoh, Posny & Heine (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0286023).

The count itself is instructive. If someone quotes “a study found brainwave entrainment,” that may be true of a particular study while being a poor summary of the larger literature. An honest account has to leave the contradictory studies in view.

And the methodological differences are not trivia. What frequencies were played? How loud were they? Were headphones used correctly? Did the control group hear ordinary tones or a comparable audio experience? Did the analyst choose EEG windows and regions in advance? How long was the exposure? Was the result a perception report, a change in electrical activity, or an actual behavioral outcome? Each answer changes what the study can establish.

## III. The positive results deserve a hearing, too

A skeptical investigation should not replace advertising certainty with the opposite kind of certainty.

A 2019 meta-analysis by Miguel Garcia-Argibay, Miguel A. Santed and José M. Reales combined twenty-two studies contributing thirty-five effect sizes across cognition, anxiety and pain-related outcomes. It reported a pooled Hedges' g of **0.45**, a statistically significant medium-sized effect in that analysis. The authors also reported variation associated with exposure timing and duration. [Garcia-Argibay and colleagues (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00426-018-1066-8).

That is meaningful research evidence. It is not proof that every marketed program works or that a single session transforms the mind. Pooling different outcomes and protocols can reveal an average association while leaving serious questions about which circumstances produce it. The estimate is about the studies selected and analyzed, not an automatic license for claims about a named commercial library.

Another systematic review and meta-analysis, by Sandhya Basu and Bidisha Banerjee, focused on memory and attention. Fifteen studies met their inclusion criteria. Across thirty-one effect sizes, they reported an overall Hedges' g of **0.40** but also described conflicting findings for particular frequencies, memory tasks and attention tasks. Their conclusion called for more robust study designs. [Basu & Banerjee (2023 print; online 2022)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00426-022-01706-7).

These results complicate a simplistic “pseudoscience” dismissal, just as the EEG review complicates a simplistic “proven” advertisement. There may be contexts in which certain types of auditory stimulation have useful effects. Which contexts, how reliable, how large, and whether effects extend beyond expectation and ordinary sound are the open questions.

It is especially important not to treat the pooled g figures as a personal prediction. The figures describe standardized differences across studies. They do not tell an individual reader the likelihood of feeling calmer next Tuesday, the amount of additional attention they will have, or a clinically meaningful result of one specific download.

## IV. A study that did not find the advertised kind of improvement

A separate 2023 experiment tested forty-hertz binaural beats with fifty-eight healthy adults. The investigators used the Attention Network Test to examine alerting, orienting and executive control, and compared the binaural condition with a control tone.

They reported **no statistically significant improvement in reaction time, error rate or the measured attention networks** and found no effect on the study's self-rated anxiety measure. [Leistiko, Madanat, Yeung & Stone (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12144-023-04681-3).

That negative result should be interpreted as carefully as a positive one. It is not a demonstration that every frequency, every recording and every listening circumstance fails. It is evidence against claiming that this particular experimental procedure reliably improved those specific outcomes in that sample.

The contrast is valuable. A pooled analysis may suggest average benefits across heterogeneous studies while a specific controlled experiment finds no benefit under its conditions. Science does not require us to choose one and conceal the other. It requires us to describe the conditions and the uncertainty.

A 2024 systematic review of thirty-one studies of music, ambient noise, white noise and binaural beats examined physiological indicators of arousal alongside cognitive performance. The authors found the evidence mixed or insufficient for firm conclusions about the relevant autonomic-arousal explanation; in many studies the physiological and cognitive questions were not assessed together. [Chee and colleagues (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijpsycho.2024.112328).

This is a useful warning for anyone staring at a dramatic EEG graphic. Even if a recording changes a physiological measurement, the important human question has not necessarily been answered.

## V. What about stress?

People often look for short audio sessions because their days are loud, fragmented and exhausting. That is a perfectly understandable buying motivation. It is also precisely where claims deserve a higher standard of care.

Jennifer Platt and Lucy Hammond reviewed twelve randomized controlled trials of binaural beats for stress management in nonclinical settings, publishing the review in 2024. Eight studies reported at least one outcome favoring the intervention over the comparison, while four reported no group effects. The authors flagged inconsistent methods, limitations around randomization and blinding, gaps in understanding moderators, and limited knowledge of adverse effects. They did not find a sufficiently strong basis for widespread recommendation or an established optimal frequency-and-duration protocol. [Platt & Hammond (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1080/18387357.2024.2374759).

This is a very different statement from “listening guarantees stress relief.” It allows some promising observations without pretending that a marketed format, a particular session length or a commercial library has already been validated.

Meditation more broadly has a separate evidence base. The US National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health discusses evidence for some approaches and also notes that unwanted experiences can occur and research quality varies. That body of work should not be silently transferred to a binaural-beat product, a hypnosis recording, or a promise of “one hour of benefits in twelve minutes.” [NCCIH](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/meditation-and-mindfulness-effectiveness-and-safety).

An advertisement can combine three true statements—meditation has been studied, binaural beats can be perceived, and electrical brain rhythms can be measured—and still reach an unsupported fourth conclusion that a specific soundtrack reproduces a clinical or contemplative outcome.

## VI. A demonstration with an honest result

Try this without buying or playing anything. Choose a quiet moment and sit comfortably. For twenty seconds, notice the sounds already present: a refrigerator, distant traffic, ventilation, birds, the slight movement of clothing. You may find that one sound becomes prominent when you turn attention toward it and recedes when you choose another.

Then ask what happened. Did the environment change, or did your attention select different parts of a scene that was already there?

There is no score and no “right” feeling. This is **a reflective observation exercise, not a controlled experiment, diagnostic assessment or validated technique for improving attention**. It demonstrates a question worth asking about hearing: how do perception and attention interact with the available sound?

If you want to explore the research itself, you can read the studies linked here. It is not a treatment and it does not require any merchant subscription.

A more technical listening demonstration would need to separate tones by ear, use appropriate hearing-safe levels and verify the audio output. Simply playing two sounds from a phone speaker would not establish a classical binaural-beat condition. This article does not embed audio, collect listening results, or ask readers to make medical decisions based on a subjective exercise.

## VII. How a buyer can interrogate a promise

Suppose a prospective buyer encounters the claim that a particular audio program makes meditation happen automatically. Seven questions separate a purchasing decision from a leap of faith.

**What exact thing was measured?** A detected beat, an EEG frequency component, a heart-rate measure, a questionnaire score, a timed attention task, and a diagnosed clinical outcome are not interchangeable.

**Was it the same product?** A small study of independently produced thirty-hertz tones is not a test of a commercial album containing music, speech, nature sounds and proprietary production choices.

**What was the comparison?** Is the result better than silence, a plausible neutral soundtrack, ordinary music, a sham binaural condition or a previously established practice? Each comparator answers a different question.

**How long and how often?** Results from repeated laboratory sessions should not be turned into promises about one twelve-minute recording.

**Which participants?** Results in healthy volunteers cannot automatically be extrapolated to people seeking treatment for anxiety, trauma, insomnia or other conditions.

**What would disconfirm the claim?** A plausible claim has measurable outcomes and room for failure. “Your unconscious mind is changing even when nothing seems different” is difficult to test and easy to use as an excuse for absent results.

**What is the commercial evidence?** Price, refund terms, checkout total, subscription consent, independently authenticated product contents, and the identity of a paid referrer are separate facts. A seller's advertised guarantee is not independent proof that a refund has occurred.

These questions are not a demand that a buyer become a neuroscientist. They are an invitation to refuse the false choice between believing every alluring story and rejecting the usefulness of listening altogether.

## VIII. The older story behind the new technology

Final Vestige's [Puységur Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) document a historical world in which investigators struggled to separate observed changes in behavior from explanations involving invisible forces, expectation and suggestion.

A modern binaural-beat soundtrack is not the same phenomenon as eighteenth-century magnetic sleep, and the history of one does not prove the mechanism of the other. Their common historical lesson is more modest: an experience can be genuine while an explanation for why it occurred remains disputed.

The fallacy has a familiar structure. Someone experiences something remarkable. An advocate names a mechanism. The mechanism acquires a promise of practical transformation. And eventually the promise becomes something that can be purchased.

Research deserves to interrupt that sequence. If a listening program feels pleasant, that might be enough reason for someone to enjoy it, provided they understand the cost and alternatives. Pleasure and convenience do not require a grand neurological story. But if a seller claims a reproducible therapeutic or cognitive transformation, it is fair to ask for evidence from the actual product, not just a demonstration that hearing is complicated.

## IX. The verdict that remains open

The binaural beat is not imaginary in the everyday sense: a real listener can experience an apparent rhythmic quality even when the physical input to each ear does not contain that rhythm. Some meta-analyses have found positive average effects across selected studies of psychological outcomes. Other experimental and systematic-review evidence reveals inconsistent EEG findings, null behavioral results and major limits of generalization.

What the public evidence reviewed here **does not establish** is that any particular retail audio program recreates the benefits of a much longer meditation session, changes a person's brain “on demand,” treats a health condition or produces guaranteed focus.

Those are specific claims about a named product and a meaningful outcome. They need their own adequately controlled tests.

Until then, the most interesting achievement may be the smallest one. Two tones, a slight difference in pitch, and a third rhythm that the listener experiences. It is an invitation to study how we perceive—not proof that the mind has been sold a shortcut.
