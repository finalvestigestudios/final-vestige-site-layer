# The Gorilla Was in the Scan: How Attention Can Hide the Obvious
**Final Vestige Studios | Mind & Perception Archive | Independent investigation — unpublished draft | 5 November 2026**

*One question connects a famous basketball video, a hospital reading room, and a historical archive of hypnosis: when we look carefully, what do we think looking guarantees?*

## The search was serious. The gorilla was ridiculous.

Twenty-four trained radiologists examined lung scans in an experiment reported in *Psychological Science* in 2013. Their ordinary, demanding assignment was to look for lung nodules. The researchers inserted an image of a gorilla into the last scan. It was not the sort of subtle abnormality the doctors were trained to find: the image occupied a volume approximately 48 times that of an average nodule in the study. Twenty of the twenty-four radiologists did not report it. Eye tracking showed that twelve of the twenty who missed it had looked directly at its location while it was visible.

This is arresting, but it is easy to misunderstand. The finding is **not** that radiologists are careless or that medical scans are routinely filled with missed creatures. The gorilla was a contrived, unexpected target; the doctors were asked to perform a legitimate specialized search. Their task made sense. That is precisely why the experiment matters. Expertise did not abolish the selectivity of attention, even when a person's gaze traveled through the relevant location.

Drew, Võ, and Wolfe described their experiment carefully. The radiologists were relatively good at spotting the task-relevant nodules. Their nodule-detection rate was about 55%; a comparison group of novices was much less successful at that instructed task. Conversely, the novices also largely failed to mention the unexpected gorilla. The researchers later showed that the inserted figure was recognizable when attention was specifically directed to it. A reader should resist turning the 83% figure from this small, artificial sample into a forecast of real-world diagnostic error.

Primary study: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3964612/ ; bibliographic record: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23863753/ .

The great detail is not the costume. It is the gap between *looking at* a location and *registering* an object as relevant to what one is doing.

## A basketball game rewrote what “obvious” means

Before the radiology study, Daniel Simons and Christopher Chabris published their 1999 experiments on sustained inattentional blindness. Participants watched dynamic ball-passing scenes and performed an attention-demanding counting task. An unexpected person or object could pass through the scene and go unreported. The probability of noticing varied with features of the unexpected object, similarity to attended objects, and task demands. The research did **not** establish one universal percentage of people who fail to notice a gorilla, independent of how the task is arranged.

It is tempting to summarize the result as “we see only what we expect.” But that phrase is too neat. The empirical question is how the observer's task, the unexpected stimulus, the visual scene, and the response questions jointly affect detection. A surprising object that resembles the attended category may be more likely to break through than an equally noticeable object that does not. The story is about selective processing under conditions, not a permanent hole in human vision.

Original study: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10694957/ ; DOI: https://doi.org/10.1068/p281059 .

One further wrinkle deserves attention. In 2010, Simons described a new demonstration for people who already knew the gorilla trick. Foreknowledge helped them spot a gorilla but did not make them generally better at detecting other unexpected events: a changing curtain and a departing player could still pass unnoticed. Recognizing the famous example is not the same as possessing a general-purpose shield against perceptual surprises.

2010 paper: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23397479/ ; DOI: https://doi.org/10.1068/i0386 .

The phrase *inattentional blindness* names a failure to notice something visible under the conditions of a competing task. It is distinct from literal damage to eyesight. It is also distinct from *change blindness*, in which an alteration between views goes unrecognized; from *attentional blink*, in which detecting one target temporarily compromises detection of another rapid target; and from the Thatcher illusion, in which turning a face upside down alters the perception of disturbing feature inversions. Those are neighboring questions, not interchangeable proofs of one theory.

## The observer and the record

Imagine a research assistant replaying a recording of a puzzling event. On a first pass, the assistant notes every spoken instruction. On a second, every gesture. On a third, the timing of objections and interruptions. Each pass may discover something the previous pass overlooked, precisely because each asks a different question. That does not mean one pass is intrinsically correct. It means the archive benefits from separating observation from inference, and from retaining the original record.

This is a useful metaphor, not a new experiment and certainly not a claim that careful replay defeats inattentional blindness. Nothing in the gorilla literature guarantees that a second inspection will reveal all missing information. In high-stakes work, properly designed checklists, independent reviewers and task-appropriate procedures must be evaluated in their own right.

The research also complicates a familiar intuition: “But their eyes were on it.” Eye position is evidence about where gaze landed; it is not a transcript of everything consciously perceived. The radiology authors found gaze at the gorilla's location among some participants who did not report it. Eye tracking neither reads thoughts nor proves that the observers secretly recognized the image and chose not to mention it. The evidence is narrower and more interesting: fixating a region need not entail reportable awareness of an unexpected item.

A research explanation must make room for competing details. In the radiologist study, the gorilla appeared near a nodule, and the authors considered whether finding the intended target could obscure awareness of another event. They did not establish every mechanism by which the gorilla was missed. They also cautioned against generalizing from a specialized lab task to the competence of real-world professionals. The experiment invites better design of attention-demanding tasks, not blanket distrust of experts.

## A very small exercise in keeping your own record

Here is a free three-minute exercise. It is **not** a validated inattentional-blindness experiment and does not measure your attention, intelligence, suggestibility or mental health.

Choose a familiar, safe desk scene with six or more harmless objects. Do not alter it while another person is watching, and do not use a road, workplace safety station or anything where missing a detail could hurt someone.

**First pass (one minute):** write only the labels of items that are meant for writing or reading. Record what you actually saw, not what you assume is usually there.

**Second pass (one minute):** inspect the same scene for sources of light, reflection and shadow. Do not “grade” yourself; make a second list.

**Third pass (one minute):** compare the lists. Which features did you intentionally ignore because of the task? Which did you record without looking closely? Where did you make an inference rather than an observation?

The useful output is a two-column note: **observed directly** versus **inferred from context**. The exercise helps practice describing evidence honestly. It does not demonstrate that you suffered inattentional blindness. Nobody has shown that repeating it makes commercial audio programs effective, eliminates errors or increases concentration in a standardized test.

## A patient, a witness, a magnetizer

Long before laboratories built ball-passing videos, observers tried to explain remarkable behaviors through suggestion. In the late eighteenth century, the Marquis de Puységur's reports of animal magnetism introduced cases in which attention, expectation, authority and interpretation were entangled with claims of unusual mental states.

The historical documents matter because they reveal what people believed they witnessed and how those accounts circulated. They do not certify magnetic fluids, supernatural perception or modern hypnosis-product outcomes. A contemporary reader should not force a nineteenth-century label onto a twentieth-century attentional task. The historical connection is epistemic: how do we separate a documented report, a participant's description, and a mechanism not yet demonstrated?

Our [Puységur archive](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) belongs to the history-of-evidence side of this story. The primary laboratory findings belong to experimental psychology. They can be read together without pretending they are the same kind of proof.

## What the gorilla cannot tell us about hypnosis, meditation, or recordings

Marketing for guided attention and hypnosis audio sometimes trades on a real anxiety: modern life is distracting and people would like relief. A scientifically interesting demonstration of selective attention can make that pitch emotionally persuasive. But **inattentional-blindness experiments do not test the commercial effectiveness of any Inspire3 product.**

## What the experiment finally leaves us with

The radiologists' job was to search for an expected medical feature. The basketball viewers' job was to count a particular class of events. In both settings, attentional priorities shaped what observers later reported. The experiments did not show that people saw nothing; they showed that even attentive people can fail to report something that seems unmistakable once it is pointed out.

That finding should make investigators more precise rather than more suspicious. Ask what someone was tasked to notice. Ask what evidence survives outside their recollection. Ask what your own interpretation adds. Design the next test around those questions instead of a slogan about the brain.

The gorilla was conspicuous. The task was real. The lesson is neither gullibility nor failure. It is a discipline of evidence: an observation is always made from somewhere, for some purpose, under some set of limits.
