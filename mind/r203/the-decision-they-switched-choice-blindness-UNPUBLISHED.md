## The answer that was not yours

Imagine two photographs on a table. You're asked a simple question: which face do you find more attractive? You point to one. The researcher turns the selected photograph toward you and asks why you picked it. You say something about the expression or the eyes.

There is a catch. Through a covert exchange, the face placed in front of you is not always the one you chose.

The remarkable part is not merely that some people fail to catch the switch. Some go on to explain the altered outcome, supplying reasons with the confidence of someone describing an actual choice. That is the puzzle Petter Johansson, Lars Hall, Sverker Sikström, and Andreas Olsson reported in *Science* in 2005. They called it **choice blindness**: failure to detect some mismatches between a selected option and the outcome subsequently presented as one's selection. [1]

This sounds like a parlor trick. Its value is more demanding. If we can be mistaken about whether an outcome belongs to our choice, what should we make of the reasons we offer afterward? And where does a demonstration end and an overconfident story about human behavior begin?

The questions matter to a research archive because reconstructing a decision after the fact resembles reconstructing a historical event. The record may include the person's explanation; that account can be sincere and still fail to establish what happened at the critical moment. Sincerity is not a substitute for independent evidence.

## A decision, a switch, and an explanation

The original experiment used pairs of photographs of female faces. Participants indicated which face they preferred. In some trials, the researchers used a card-manipulation technique so that the participant was shown the face they had **not** selected. They were asked to explain their decision. [1]

In this design, the experimenter knows the initial choice and can compare it against the outcome displayed. That external record is essential: the research does not simply ask people to remember their preferences later. It creates a known discrepancy between action and feedback, then measures whether they notice. Some mismatches were detected; others were not. Explanations sometimes referred to qualities of the unchosen face as though those qualities were the reasons for the original decision.

Avoid the tempting but unsupported shortcut: *nobody notices the difference*. The observed effect is not universal. Detection depends on the experimental setup, stimulus, discrepancy, timing, question, and participant. The paper's basic contribution is the possibility of undetected outcome substitution, not a constant blindness rate that can be applied to all buyers or all decisions.

The study also does **not** prove that a participant's preferences were secretly rewritten at a deep level. What is directly observed is a choice, a manipulated presentation, a detection or non-detection, and a verbal justification. Whether the justification reflects conversational cooperation, memory reconstruction, altered evaluation, or other processes calls for additional study. A person could fail to notice the substitution without having changed what they originally liked.

That boundary matters. There is a difference between *I can't tell that you substituted the object* and *I now genuinely prefer the substituted object*. Researchers use additional measures to investigate the second question, but it should never be smuggled in as a conclusion of every choice-blindness demonstration.

## A supermarket becomes the laboratory

In 2010, Hall, Johansson, Tärning, Sikström, and Deutgen moved the method into a more ordinary consumer setting. Visitors at a supermarket **tasted jam** or **smelled tea**, comparing pairs before selecting one. They were not tasting tea: the two tasks used different senses. After the selection, they were invited to sample their supposedly chosen product again and explain the choice. Without telling them, researchers sometimes switched the contents. [2]

The published abstract reports detection on **no more than one third of manipulated trials** overall. The study's detection measure combined recognition during the manipulation, later reporting, and reports of a sensory change; it was not simply a count of people who explicitly announced that a deceptive swap had happened. For some conspicuously different flavor or aroma comparisons, detection was higher, but the method still generated missed substitutions. [2] Those proportions are for *manipulated trials in this study*, not estimates of the fraction of people in daily life who buy the wrong thing or believe a misleading advertisement.

The setting strengthened ecological relevance: participants sampled real consumer goods rather than only photographs. But a tasting booth operated by researchers is not the same as paying for a product, comparing competing suppliers, reading refund rules, or deciding whether to make a recurring purchase. No merchant commission, future satisfaction, or long-term benefit was measured by this experiment.

There is a second reason to be precise. Smell and taste can be difficult to describe, and a person may need to reconstruct vocabulary to explain a choice even when no switch occurs. That does not negate the effect—the manipulated outcome is still real—but it cautions against treating every confident-sounding explanation as a stable psychological commitment.

This is how a good demonstration should work for readers: it shows you something surprising about the experimental situation, then identifies the boundary beyond which the evidence does not travel.

## When the questions become moral

A 2012 study by Hall, Johansson, and Strandberg used a self-transforming paper survey of moral opinions. By altering what participants appeared to have said, the researchers could ask whether participants noticed a reversal and how they justified the apparent response. The published account reports that **69% failed to detect at least one of two changes**. [3] Detection could mean recognizing and correcting an apparent mismatch between a statement and a recorded rating; it did not necessarily require participants to deduce that the questionnaire itself had been secretly altered.

That percentage should not become a headline claiming 69% of people have no moral convictions. The actual measure concerned detection of changes in a **particular experimental instrument**; the questions were framed and presented under controlled circumstances. A participant's explanation of a substituted answer is informative but does not establish that their moral principles have permanently reversed.

The shift from faces to preferences to moral survey answers reveals why the phenomenon attracts attention. It challenges a tidy model in which preference is always fixed first, choice expresses it, and explanation faithfully reports the original preference. But challenging a tidy model is not proof that choice is arbitrary, that beliefs are meaningless, or that anyone can be manipulated into any opinion.

In a **2013 publication**, an overlapping research team reported political-questionnaire fieldwork conducted during **Sweden's 2010 election**. They experimentally altered responses before inviting participants to discuss the results. That study examined detection, endorsement of a modified summary, and **immediately stated voting intentions**, not verified ballots or lasting opinion change, in a specific political context. [4] It is ethically relevant to persuasion research but not a field test demonstrating reliable voter conversion in real campaigns. There is no warrant for Final Vestige to promise marketers a measurable political swing from a laboratory technique.

## The strongest test is often a more awkward one

Choice blindness is not confined to light personal preferences. McLaughlin and Somerville investigated experimentally manipulated pension-portfolio choices, a decision category with serious financial associations. Their paper appears in *Judgment and Decision Making*, volume 8 (2013). The Cambridge site currently displays an online hosting date of 2023, which **must not** be confused with the original 2013 journal publication. [5]

In their hypothetical financial-choice task, **98** usable participant records remained after two withdrawals from an initial 100. Detection varied with how similar or dissimilar the substituted portfolios were and how detection was counted. Their abstract reports an **aggregate upper bound of 37.2% detected manipulated trials**. [5] The paper also reports lower aggregate estimates, around **28.5%**, under a different counting assumption. The protocol presented similar substitutions first and dissimilar ones later, stopping manipulation trials after detection. The stronger similarity comparison depended on an assumption about what earlier detectors would have detected in a later dissimilar trial they were not shown; the upper estimate for that dissimilar condition reached 50%. These are modeled or assumption-dependent bounds, **not an observed universal ceiling** and not a clean causal estimate of similarity effects.

This offers an instructive correction to a slick generalization. The researchers did not make real pension investments on behalf of participants. Their findings cannot be turned into a probability that actual savers will miss a changed investment allocation, nor can they support a recommendation for a financial product. A hypothetical portfolio decision, however carefully designed, has different incentives, consequences, and legal safeguards from a live transaction.

Even within the study, a missed substitution is not always a newly accepted preference. The researchers' content analysis found different kinds of response: some reports suggested changes of preference, while many were compatible with maintaining earlier preferences. Counting all undetected switches as persuasion successes would erase precisely the distinction the researchers were trying to examine.

An archive should preserve that uncertainty. One trial can show a surprising failure of detection while leaving multiple mechanisms open for future investigation.

## Choice blindness is not change blindness

Final Vestige's published [Change Blindness investigation](https://finalvestige.com/2026/11/02/change-blindness/) explores situations in which observers miss alterations to a visual scene. Choice blindness overlaps in spirit, but its object is different. A participant may perceive both photographs perfectly well and yet fail to register that the face now presented as the selected one was not the one originally chosen.

Change blindness asks whether a change in a scene is detected. Choice blindness links an **action**, its **reported outcome**, and a subsequent **explanation**. The slip is not simply missing a visual difference; it can be a mismatch between the agent's own recorded decision and what is attributed to that agent afterward.

The distinction is useful when a video says that your eyes are lying or your brain invents every decision. Neither slogan is the research result. The experimental intervention deliberately creates a mismatch, and the evidence tells us how people respond within that situation. It does not make ordinary perception universally unreliable.

The [Mesmerism investigation](https://finalvestige.com/2026/11/02/mesmerism/) and [Puységur's Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) tell a parallel historical story about interpretations of suggestion. Eighteenth-century accounts documented dramatic experiences and conflicting explanations. What later investigators needed was a way to distinguish expectation, intervention, testimony, and independently observable cause. The modern choice-blindness experiments do **not** validate animal magnetism or hypnotic efficacy. They offer a methodological analogy about the limits of introspection and the importance of comparing accounts against a preserved record.

## A free exercise: preserve the decision before hearing the story

No magic cards, hidden cameras, deceptive manipulation or partner are required. This exercise is **not an experiment that induces choice blindness**. It is a transparent decision-audit tool, meant to make future comparisons possible.

Before choosing between two ordinary, low-stakes options—two free audio samples, two publicly described books, or two possible weekend activities—write down the options in specific terms. Record the decision criterion before you choose: cost, duration, access, content, refund conditions, or one other feature that really matters to you. Then write what you chose, when, and why.

Next, inspect the actual option carefully. If the choice involves a paid product, look at the exact merchant page and any checkout terms only after deciding what information you need; do not submit payment to complete this exercise. Check whether the feature you thought you selected is still present in the actual offering. Distinguish an uncertain memory of the advertisement from the description in front of you.

Finally, write a short post-choice explanation **without editing the first entry**. Compare the two accounts. A difference is a reason to investigate, not proof of a psychological illusion. The explanations can change because you learned something new, because your priorities shifted, or because your initial notes were vague.

| Before the choice | After reviewing the actual offer |
| --- | --- |
| Exact two alternatives | Exact selected outcome shown |
| Criterion that mattered most | Does the result match that criterion? |
| Price, format and refund evidence **as observed** | What changed, or what was simply misunderstood? |
| Initial explanation and timestamp | Updated explanation, preserved separately |
| Evidence source | Independent confirmation or remaining unknown |

This humble paper trail is what the experimental switch itself removes from participants' view. Here, the record stays yours. It neither diagnoses cognitive weakness nor proves that journaling prevents choice blindness. It simply allows an honest comparison that retrospective explanation alone cannot supply.

## What an audio buyer can actually establish

The connection to meditation audio is not that choice-blindness research proves meditation audio works. It does not. The connection is **decision clarity before and after an offer is presented**.

Consider Zen12 Premium, a meditation-audio program listed by Inspire3. At the exact publicly visible seller page reviewed on November 7, 2026, the merchant described **twelve levels and forty-eight MP3 recordings** and advertised a one-year money-back guarantee. That is a seller-supplied inventory and policy description, not verified delivery or refund performance. [7]

The same merchant promotes an equivalence between twelve minutes of listening and an hour of meditation, alongside psychological and health benefits. [7] Neither the face-switching experiment nor the jam study nor general meditation literature establishes those outcome claims **for the branded Zen12 recordings**. The correct editorial response is not to repeat the equivalence as established science.

A serious buying question is more concrete: do you want a structured library of recordings, or are you hoping for a clinically guaranteed transformation? If you want structured playback and can afford it, the format may be worth comparing to alternatives, but the current evidence review cannot promise any particular effect. If you want a modest routine without expense, a twelve-minute timer and the existing [Seven Day Focus Fieldbook](https://finalvestige.com/focus/) are no-cost alternatives; that fieldbook is a planning resource, not clinically tested treatment.

An audio seller's demonstration can be engaging and still leave decisive questions unanswered. What files will actually be delivered? Is the advertised refund policy honored at checkout? Which benefits were tested on the **exact product**, with what comparator and outcomes? How do its promotional statements differ from the scientific papers linked on the page? Has the reviewer actually used it, or only inspected the documentation?

Final Vestige does **not** claim to have used the recordings. We have not verified our individual Inspire3 affiliate eligibility, terms, authorized earning link, or commissions. There is no affiliate purchasing link in this investigation. If a future independent commercial guide contains an authorized earning recommendation, it must disclose the relationship immediately beside that link and distinguish click measurement from confirmed paid commission. [8][9]

A financial incentive should sharpen disclosure obligations, not soften evidentiary standards. The study of choice blindness cannot tell us whether a merchant's promises are fulfilled, and it certainly does not validate an affiliate sales funnel.

## What the evidence does not say

We should refuse six popular extrapolations.

**It does not say preferences are imaginary.** The task deliberately swaps outcomes; that intervention is not ordinary choosing.

**It does not say everybody misses substitutions.** Detection occurs, sometimes often, and changes with conditions. The published percentages are study-specific.

**It does not say people believe every reason they give.** Verbal justifications are observable; their internal certainty and stability require separate measures.

**It does not equate an undetected outcome swap with a purchased product.** These experiments measured detection and explanations, not merchant sales or affiliate commissions.

**It does not establish therapeutic effects of hypnosis or meditation.** A psychology experiment involving preference judgments is not a clinical trial of audio recordings.

**It does not prove a paper decision ledger prevents mistakes.** Our free audit is an editorial technique for preserving your own baseline, not a treatment, test instrument or validated debiasing intervention.

## What would change our conclusion?

A well-designed replication could compare conditions with different stakes, explicit receipts, time delays, recognition opportunities, and independent outcome checks. It would separate detection from a durable shift in preference, distinguish participants who are confused from those who genuinely adopt the substitute, and make the conditions of its estimates transparent. For buyer-facing applications, actual consumer outcomes and separately verified merchant terms would be necessary; one cannot bridge those gaps with a viral clip.

That is the larger lesson for this archive. The mind's explanation of a choice can be insightful without being a complete transcript of the choice. Preserve the original decision when you can. Then inspect the evidence behind the story told afterward.
