01 — Observe

The attention cycle is not a failure report

Hasenkamp and colleagues identified four analytically distinct intervals around participants' self-reported moments of noticing wandering: wandering, awareness, shifting back, and sustained attention. Their fMRI study had only fourteen practitioners, so avoid turning its neural associations into a universal life claim.

In a 2013 randomized mindfulness-course study, participants showed some improvements in attention-related outcomes compared with a comparison course. A 2024 meta-analysis of 111 randomized trials found small-to-moderate average cognitive effects in certain domains, not all domains. These are training studies, not trials of this specific commercial recording.

02 — Try before paying

Three minutes. One ordinary return.

  1. Choose a safe, comfortable seat and a neutral attention anchor, such as the contact of your feet with the floor.
  2. For one minute, allow attention to rest there. When you notice a thought pulled you away, silently say “noticed” and return. You need not suppress the thought.
  3. For another minute, do the same and notice whether recognizing distraction feels different from preventing distraction.
  4. In the final minute, reflect: did the exercise show how many thoughts occurred, or only how many times you caught them?

Free, nonclinical reflection, not a diagnostic test or treatment. Stop if uncomfortable; do not practice while driving or using machinery.

Noticing the drift may be the most interesting thing that happened.
