# The Sound That Was Never Spoken
### How the brain repairs missing words—and why a convincing experience is not proof of its cause

*Final Vestige Studios · Mind & Perception · Independent investigation · Editorial draft, not published · November 3, 2026*

**Evidence finding:** In controlled listening experiments, people sometimes perceive a speech sound that has been physically replaced by noise. The effect is called *phonemic restoration*. It reveals the contribution of context to hearing—not a license to conclude that belief can repair every perception, that hypnosis controls the mind, or that an audio product produces a clinical benefit.

## The missing instant

Imagine a recording that does not quite say what you think it says.

A speaker finishes a sentence. In the middle, there is a cough. You could swear the speaker pronounced the interrupted word cleanly, and you might even point to the wrong place when asked where the cough occurred. The recording has not magically repaired itself. The sound is gone. Your hearing nevertheless reports a continuous word.

That was the problem Richard M. Warren placed before readers of *Science* on January 23, 1970. Warren's brief report, “Perceptual Restoration of Missing Speech Sounds,” described a remarkable but carefully bounded effect: replace a speech sound with a cough or tone and some listeners believe the absent sound remains audible. Replace it with silence and the interruption is easier to locate. A listener's percept is therefore not a transparent transcript of the physical waveform. [S01]

The difference matters. In the noisy version, the auditory system encounters an interruption it could plausibly attribute to something that covered an otherwise continuous word. In the silent version, the empty space announces an absence. This is a difference between **masking** and an **unmasked gap**, not evidence that the ear receives information through mysterious channels.

Consider what a listener brings to the recording: knowledge of words, grammar, common phrasing, the sentence's topic, and expectations about what the next syllable might be. Not all of those expectations are conscious. They are the accumulated shortcuts of recognizing speech quickly enough to have a conversation.

## What the experiment actually demonstrated

Warren's 1970 report is frequently summarized as “the brain fills in missing words.” That is catchy, but technically loose. A phoneme is a speech-sound category, not necessarily an entire word; researchers often replace a segment *within* a word. And hearing an intact word is not the same as correctly remembering a recording. The experimental question is narrower: under particular conditions, can the listener experience physically absent sound as though it were present?

Warren's observations addressed a peculiar combination: people reported hearing the missing speech sound; the interfering noise appeared to shift in time; and silence was less likely than masking noise to induce this experience. The abstract is clear about the contrast. It does **not** say that all people are equally susceptible or that the phenomenon occurs in all languages and acoustic settings. [S01]

Warren and Charles J. Obusek expanded the work in 1971. Their report examined the incidence of restoration and how people located the interruption, showing that both preceding and subsequent speech context could matter. What seemed subjectively obvious—“I heard that sound”—was now something researchers could vary by changing the acoustic stimulus. [S02]

A subtle lesson emerges. Testimony is valuable evidence of **experience**. It is not, without the recording and a sound analysis, decisive evidence of **what physically happened**. This distinction does not demean witnesses. It tells an investigator how to preserve both the observation and the alternative explanations.

## Is restoration guessing, or something deeper?

An ordinary guess is a decision after uncertainty: “I think that was the word.” Phonemic restoration can be phenomenologically different. The interrupted sound may feel present rather than absent-but-inferred.

That difference made the effect scientifically interesting and difficult to explain. If a listener simply chooses a plausible word on a questionnaire, that is not necessarily the same as *perceiving* a restored segment. If the listener hears noise as covering a continuous word, the perceptual system is doing some of the work before a deliberate decision.

Arthur G. Samuel's 1981 program made the question more precise. “Phonemic restoration: Insights from a new methodology” developed ways to separate sensitivity to actual changes from the habit of responding as though a sound was present. A related paper asked what happens when a restored sound does or does not have acoustic confirmation. Its abstract reports that results varied with the class of phoneme and the substituting sound. Fricatives were easier to restore than vowels under white-noise substitution, while the pattern reversed with pure-tone substitution; short silence changed performance with stop consonants. [S03][S04]

That is an important check against a simplistic “the mind hears whatever it expects” explanation. **The bottom-up acoustic details still matter.** Expectations are not omnipotent. A theory that ignores the actual noise cannot account for Samuel's documented variations.

Later investigators continued separating components that popular descriptions tend to fuse. Trout and Poser, examining auditory-only and audiovisual conditions in 1990, reported that phoneme type and physical cues affected measured restoration. The relationship between sound, vision and learned linguistic constraints could be tested rather than assumed. [S05]

A different challenge emerged in Bruno Repp's 1992 experiments: is the apparent restored sound a form of auditory induction that consumes part of the masking noise, or is it a more abstract reconstruction of a word? The publication explicitly framed those mechanisms as alternatives. A useful investigation must preserve this uncertainty. [S06]

## The listener's expectation is one of the variables

The tendency to regard a percept as a verdict is understandable. We depend on listening. Crossing a street, speaking with a family member, hearing instructions at work—these all require rapid recognition under imperfect conditions. The same processes that make listening robust can sometimes leave us confident about something not literally present in the recording.

But the phenomenon is more sophisticated than “everyone is fooled.” The missing segment's identity, the kind of masking sound, the availability of an intelligible word, and the surrounding phonemes all change what can be restored. In a 2022 preliminary clinical study, for example, researchers tested phonemic restoration with real words and pseudowords in very small groups of people with Alzheimer's disease or semantic dementia alongside controls. Results differed by stimulus type and patient group. With only five patients in one condition and four in another, that paper is not a clinical screening recommendation; it is a demonstration that the mechanisms and experimental context deserve care. [S07]

Even when a listener perceives an absent sound, the mechanism remains something to investigate. The subjective feeling is real. The proposed explanation must compete with other explanations.

## A free experiment in careful listening

You can do a useful **observation**, though not a scientific replication, without purchasing software.

Write two short sentences whose meanings depend on a different letter or sound in the middle of a word. Ask a friend to read each one naturally. Record them locally with permission, then create a second recording where a short noise replaces—not merely overlays—the targeted segment. Make a third version where the same segment becomes silence. Listen without looking at the words; note where you think the disruption occurred, what you think you heard, and how certain you feel. Reveal the edited waveform only afterward.

There are several reasons **not** to call your result a Warren-style experiment. The noise level may differ, your editing may leave acoustic clues, the sentences may not be matched, your familiarity with the script changes your expectations, and the sample size is tiny. A home observation offers something more modest: it forces you to distinguish a confident report from a documented acoustic event.

If someone in your group hears a clear word in the noise-spliced version, the interesting next question is not whether their mind is “weak.” It is what properties of the sentence and sound made that report possible. If nobody experiences restoration, that is also an observation; it does not overturn the controlled literature.

For an accessible alternative without audio editing, simply write down a sentence you think you heard in a crowded place and verify it later with the speaker—only when doing so is ordinary and consensual. This illustrates uncertainty in everyday comprehension; it is **not** proof that phonemic restoration specifically occurred.

## Where listening meets suggestion

The temptation is to make a great leap: if expectation can shape a sound, perhaps suggestion can write any experience into the brain. The leap is unwarranted.

Warren and Samuel conducted speech-perception experiments. Their findings do not test hypnotic induction, commercial meditation sessions, subliminal audio, treatment of anxiety, or improved productivity. A finding about one mechanism in hearing cannot serve as product-specific efficacy evidence for another.

There is, however, a useful historical comparison. In 1784–85, the Marquis de Puységur documented what he interpreted as magnetic somnambulism. His accounts preserved observations, testimony, operators' expectations and theories about unseen forces. Final Vestige's [Puységur's Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) makes a rigorous distinction between the **provenance of a document** and the **truth of its proposed mechanism**. A record can be authentic while its explanation remains unproven.

That same distinction is the ethical foundation of this investigation. Warren's laboratory stimuli permit acoustic manipulation, repetition and alternative models in ways an eighteenth-century case narrative could not. This is a methodological comparison, **not** evidence for animal magnetism and not a claim that Puységur documented phonemic restoration.

For readers drawn to visual perception, Final Vestige's published [Change Blindness investigation](https://finalvestige.com/2026/11/02/change-blindness/) examines another way a report can diverge from the physical scene. Change blindness and phonemic restoration are distinct research phenomena, so the comparison is conceptual, not diagnostic.

## What this does not establish

The research **does show** that speech perception sometimes integrates missing information from context, especially when noise plausibly masks the absent segment. It **does not show** that memories are generally false, people cannot trust their senses, or all auditory hallucinations have the same mechanism.

It does not license the popular claim that the brain is “rewired” by particular sounds, nor that brainwave recordings reproduce long meditation sessions. Those are separate, product-specific questions.

The ethical risk grows when a company turns a credible laboratory discovery into a shortcut for an unrelated promise. A reader could reasonably move from “some sounds are reconstructed” to “perhaps all internal states are easy to manufacture.” An evidence archive should interrupt that move, not monetize it.

The most useful conclusion is smaller and stranger. We can recognize a sentence even when part of it was never delivered. A robust sensory system is not simply a microphone. It is an interpreter—astonishingly useful, occasionally mistaken, and open to careful test.
