# The Sound Between Two Ears: What a Binaural Beat Can—and Cannot—Prove
*Final Vestige Studios | Mind & Perception Archive | Unpublished editorial manuscript | 18 November 2026*
*Editorial status: SOURCE REVIEW REQUIRED; not a merchant endorsement or product-performance test.*

## The sound no speaker actually played

Imagine one ear receiving a steady tone at 200 hertz and the other receiving one at 210. Neither signal contains a ten-hertz pulse. But under suitable listening conditions a listener may hear a slow fluctuation that seems to exist between the tones. The apparent ten-hertz rhythm is not hidden inside either recording. It arises from auditory processing when the two slightly different tones reach separate ears.

That is the remarkable—and limited—fact at the center of binaural-beat research. Something perceptually real can be constructed by the nervous system without a matching beat being present in either ear's physical signal. But a perceived rhythm is not automatically a therapeutic intervention, a measurable increase in attention, or a reliable switch that makes the brain enter a marketed state.

The distinction matters because a visitor looking for help beginning an overdue task may encounter products that promise “focus” through frequencies, meditation through audio, or an almost effortless change in mental performance. Good consumer research asks three questions in order: *What is being played? What does the listener actually perceive? What independent evidence links that exact recording to the advertised outcome?* These are three different questions with three different kinds of proof.

This investigation makes that separation visible. It also shows why both blanket dismissal and spectacular marketing certainty outrun the literature.

## The 1973 intervention was a question, not a cure

In October 1973, biophysicist Gerald Oster published *Auditory Beats in the Brain* in *Scientific American*. His subject was binaural hearing: how the brain handles distinct signals received at the left and right ears. The original article treated the beat as a phenomenon that could illuminate auditory mechanisms, not as evidence that a particular consumer track improves productivity or relieves disease.

Oster's discussion also points to a consequential distinction. In a **monaural beat**, two nearby frequencies are mixed together before reaching the listener, creating a physical fluctuation in the resulting acoustic signal. In a **binaural beat**, the separate frequencies must reach different ears for the percept to arise in the relevant way. Headphones are the familiar means of separating those inputs, although equipment and listening context matter.

That is a real mechanism distinction, not branding vocabulary. A commercially sold “brainwave” recording can contain pulsing volume, layered music, noise, ordinary rhythm, binaural tones, or combinations of techniques. The presence of the word *frequency* in its advertising does not identify which method the producer used.

A single frequency also is not a behavioral outcome. Calling a sound “theta” says little about how well a specific participant later remembers a paragraph or initiates an unpleasant task. The measured dependent variable is the bridge between an interesting stimulus and any practical promise.

**Primary source:** Gerald Oster, *Auditory Beats in the Brain*, *Scientific American* (1973), DOI [10.1038/scientificamerican1073-94](https://doi.org/10.1038/scientificamerican1073-94).

## Hearing a rhythm is not the same as changing every brainwave

The phrase “brainwave entrainment” is often used as if it describes a single uncontroversial process. It can instead cover at least four claims: that a rhythmic stimulus is detected, that measurable neural activity responds at or near its timing, that a broad functional brain state follows, and that a meaningful improvement in a real-world outcome results. The first two do not logically guarantee the last two.

Researchers have directly measured auditory responses. In a 2014 *Journal of Neurophysiology* study, Ross and colleagues recorded neuromagnetic cortical responses to binaural beats across different rates. They observed frequency-dependent responses and discussed what these might reveal about binaural hearing and sound-location processing. That finding is interesting evidence of neural processing. It is not a randomized test of a store-bought meditation recording against an adequately matched control on tomorrow's work output.

In 2020, a study in *eNeuro* asked an especially useful question: what happens if binaural stimulation is compared with a monaural control rather than silence alone? The researchers observed cortical responses to binaural stimulation but reported stronger entrainment under the monaural control; neither condition improved their mood measures. Their result does **not** show that no auditory intervention can ever affect mood. It shows that the extra value of the binaural arrangement cannot simply be assumed from its audible novelty.

This is what scientific controls are for. If a pleasant soundtrack helps a participant settle into a routine, the active ingredient may be the routine, the music, expectation, reduced distraction, or some mixture. A product-specific study needs controls suitable for identifying the contribution it actually advertises.

**Sources:** Ross et al. (2014), DOI [10.1152/jn.00224.2014](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.00224.2014); *Binaural Beats through the Auditory Pathway* (2020), [eNeuro original study](https://www.eneuro.org/content/7/2/ENEURO.0232-19.2020).

## An apparent contradiction in the research—and why it isn't one

If you search for meta-analyses, you can find positive-sounding conclusions. A 2019 meta-analysis in *Psychological Research* combined 22 studies and reported a medium overall effect, g = 0.45, across several studied domains including cognition, anxiety and pain perception. That result deserves mention. Excluding it simply because it complicates a critical article would be poor investigative practice.

But what is being pooled? Different outcomes, listening durations, comparison conditions, stimulus parameters and populations do not automatically become one proven consumer promise. A pooled average is not the effect estimate for every kind of listening, every user or every branded MP3. Neither does it tell us whether an individual track delivers a particular benefit over an ordinary soothing soundtrack.

Then, in May 2023, Ingendoh, Posny and Heine published a systematic review of EEG evidence in *PLOS ONE*. Fourteen studies met its inclusion criteria. Five supported the entrainment hypothesis, eight reported contradictory findings and one had mixed outcomes. The authors emphasized substantial differences in stimulus design, study methods and EEG analysis. The outcome here was narrower: **does the stimulation systematically entrain measured brain oscillations?** It was not a direct verdict on all subjective benefits of listening to music.

A 2024 review of 31 studies examining autonomic markers and cognitive performance in response to several types of auditory stimulation found evidence mixed or insufficient to connect proposed autonomic mechanisms reliably to cognitive outcomes. A separate 2024 review explored anxiety and depression with binaural beats, again highlighting protocol and study limitations. Those reviews do not create a universal verdict about every product either.

A February 2026 systematic review of interventions involving music, binaural beats **and audiovisual stimulation** in young adults screened 122 abstracts and included ten trials. It reported promising findings in some domains but highlighted small samples and heterogeneity. Its intervention categories should not be silently collapsed into “binaural beats are now proved to work” or “Focus Shot is clinically effective.” Mixing music therapy, audiovisual stimulation and binaural beats expands the scope of a review but also limits what can be attributed to any one commercial audio mechanism.

There is no logical inconsistency between “some pooled behavioral outcomes were positive” and “EEG entrainment outcomes were inconsistent.” They ask different questions with different methods. Neither substitutes for independent trials of the actual product under consideration.

## The product-specific problem: an audio name is not a stimulus specification

Consider the currently public seller page for [Brainwave Shots' Focus Shot](https://brainwaveshots.com/focus-shot). The merchant describes fifteen- and thirty-minute versions, background ambience with brown noise, additional “brainwave only” recordings, and a full thirty-minute protocol alternating between 12 and 18 hertz. These are **seller descriptions of the package and protocol**, not measurements performed by Final Vestige.

The same seller page says headphones are not required. That single detail should stop an uncritical writer from labeling the recording a verified binaural-beat treatment. A true binaural stimulus depends on separately delivered signals to each ear; if headphones are unnecessary for the product, its marketed mechanism might be another form of rhythmic sound, a mixture, or something else. Without a technical file analysis and vendor explanation, we do **not know** exactly which stimulus arrangement produces its claimed effect.

On the page the seller also attributes unusually powerful attention, motivation, long-lasting focus and safety to the audio. We have not located independent controlled clinical testing of **Focus Shot itself** that justifies republishing those outcomes as facts. A twenty-year-old laboratory observation, an EEG finding, and a general meta-analysis cannot fill that product-specific gap automatically.

Its displayed price, crossed-out comparison figure, percentage discount, customer-rating widget and refund statement are merchant-side observations from the day of review. They are not authenticated affiliate checkout terms, payable commissions, independent customer endorsements, or a promise that a discount remains available. A prospective buyer should examine the actual checkout, country/currency, delivery restrictions and full refund conditions directly before purchase.

Why report the uncertainty this plainly? Because the highest-intent reader deserves an answer to the real question: *Will the product solve the specific problem I'm paying for it to solve?* The honest answer is that its available format can be described, but its advertised practical effects have not been independently substantiated for this product.

## A twelve-minute comparison anyone can conduct without purchasing audio

The free [Seven Day Focus Fieldbook](https://finalvestige.com/focus/) provides a much better starting point for a person struggling to begin work than a slogan about sound frequencies. Its baseline routine does not require any purchase or special headset. The following exercise adapts the **decision method**, not the scientific credibility of any marketed product:

1. Select one task with a clear first action: open a document, list five references, or identify the next paragraph you need to read. Write down what “started” means **before** you begin.
2. Record the date, start time and current distractions. Set a twelve-minute timer. Do the task in ordinary quiet or with whatever familiar background sound you normally use.
3. When the timer ends, record only concrete outcomes: whether you started, how many relevant steps you finished, whether you abandoned the task, and what interrupted you. Avoid treating feeling “in the zone” as proof of neural entrainment.
4. On a different day, repeat a comparably sized task using another *free, rights-cleared, familiar* listening condition if you wish. Keep task difficulty, time of day and volume as similar as practicable. Do not generate loud high-frequency tones or use a test while driving.
5. Compare notes cautiously. Two sessions do not establish cause and effect. Practice, novelty, tiredness and expectations can all affect the result. Use your observations to decide whether a routine is pleasant and practical—not to make a medical diagnosis.

A genuinely rigorous trial would need randomization, blinding where possible, suitable matched controls, prespecified outcomes and enough participants to detect meaningful differences. Your notebook is not that trial. It *is* a way to stop substituting a sales promise for your own defined goal.

## Why Puységur belongs in this story

Final Vestige's [Puységur Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) examines the claims, testimony and source problems surrounding eighteenth-century magnetic somnambulism. The historical actors were not studying EEG, binaural beats or digital audio. Collapsing their beliefs into modern “brainwave technology” would be an anachronism.

There is nevertheless a useful continuity in the *research problem*. If a witness sincerely reports a striking subjective state, what precisely was observed? What was the setting? Who interpreted the experience? How could an independent investigator distinguish the perceived phenomenon from the explanation offered for it?

In the older records, a demonstration could seem extraordinary while its causal account remained disputed. With modern auditory experiments, the percept can be reproducible even if commercial claims about mental performance remain unproved. Neither history nor modern neuroscience licenses a leap from reported experience to universal efficacy.

This is the editorial boundary between Final Vestige's investigations and any separate buyer guide. A scientific finding can be interesting even when it sells nothing. Conversely, an audio product can be convenient to use without proving the most ambitious claims its marketing makes.

## The four questions that survive the advertising

Before spending money on a branded focus or meditation recording, ask:

**What will I actually receive?** Number and duration of files, delivery method, access requirements, offline availability, and whether a specific headset is needed should be documented from the exact current offer.

**What kind of sound is it?** Ask whether the recording uses binaural tones, monaural beats, amplitude modulation, ordinary music, noise, verbal instruction, or a combination. If the vendor does not explain the distinction, don't infer it from a “brainwaves” headline.

**What has been measured?** An EEG response, self-reported relaxation, task performance and a reduction in a diagnosed disorder are not synonyms. Demand evidence that corresponds to the advertised result, and ideally to the actual product.

**What happens if it doesn't help me?** Verify current terms, exclusions, refund procedure and support. A seller-page guarantee is not proof that a specific customer's refund will be granted.

The sound between two ears is a genuine and intriguing perceptual event. It reminds us that perception is not a recording device. It does not supply a purchase verdict on its own. The responsible conclusion is deliberately less cinematic than a miracle: define the claim, inspect the stimulus, read the method, and keep the result within the boundaries of the evidence.
