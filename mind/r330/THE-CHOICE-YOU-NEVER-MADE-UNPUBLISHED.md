# The Choice You Never Made—and the Reason You Gave for Making It
*Final Vestige Studios · Mind & Perception Archive · Unpublished investigation · 12 November 2026*

**A decision can be changed after you make it. Sometimes you will explain the substituted decision as if it were yours. That is not proof that people are puppets. It is a warning about how readily explanations can outrun evidence.**

## The moment the photograph changed hands

Imagine a research participant looking at two photographs of faces. The instruction is unremarkable: choose the more attractive of the pair. The participant points. Then the experimenter lifts the selected photograph and asks a second question: why did you choose this person?

There is a catch. During the handoff, a carefully engineered switch can put the *other* photograph in the participant's hands. What has changed is not the participant's preference, at least not yet, but the physical evidence offered back as its supposed expression. If the participant notices, the manipulation is exposed. If not, the participant may offer a fluent explanation of a choice that was never made.

This is the core of an experiment by Petter Johansson, Lars Hall, Sverker Sikström and Andreas Olsson, published in *Science* in October 2005. They named the failure to detect the mismatch **choice blindness**. Their abstract reports that some participants failed to notice conspicuous mismatches and nevertheless offered reasons for the outcome placed before them. The finding is narrower than a viral slogan such as “your mind makes up every decision.” It concerns particular choice tasks and a covert intervention that changed what people were shown. [1]

The distinction matters because there are at least three possible events here. A person can fail to detect the swap. A person can explain the substituted option. And a person's underlying preference can actually change. These outcomes cannot be treated as synonyms.

## The evidence and the sleight of hand

The original technique had the logic of a stage trick but the discipline of an experiment: the selection was observable, the outcome could be manipulated, and detection could be assessed. There is no need to assume a mysterious power over consciousness. The researchers created a conflict between a participant's just-made choice and an apparently authoritative record of it.

Humans are often good at noticing what they intend and what they do. Yet they are not equipped with an infallible transcript of the causal steps that produced each preference. When a person is invited to account for an outcome that appears already settled, they can construct a plausible explanation that fits that outcome.

This does **not** prove that the spoken explanation is a conscious lie. Nor does it allow us to say the individual secretly preferred the other face all along. The study design shows that explanation and action can become dissociated under a controlled manipulation. A further 2006 analysis investigated introspective reports in this paradigm, while the larger literature on metacognition asks how people assess the accuracy of their own thoughts and decisions. [2]

The experiment invites a precise question: when we tell a story about why we chose something, how much of that account comes from memory of the original decision, and how much comes from the evidence available *now*?

## Jam, tea, and the shop counter

In 2010, the same research group moved the question from face photographs to a supermarket. Shoppers tasted two jams or teas and picked the one they preferred. The experimenters then covertly exchanged samples and asked the shoppers to sample—and justify—the supposed winner. According to the paper's abstract, no more than a third of the manipulated trials were detected overall. Some strikingly different pairings were detected more often, though still not universally. [3]

A busy marketplace is not a pristine laboratory. But the task still relied on a deliberate switch by researchers, not spontaneous ignorance about one's own tastes. The result should not be inflated into “two thirds of every purchase can be changed without notice.” Those were manipulated trials in a particular experimental arrangement, with specific stimuli, procedures, participants and detection criteria.

The marketplace study also underscores why sensory comparison and a person's explanation are different records. A participant may truly experience the newly handed sample as pleasant or unpleasant; an explanation of why that was supposedly the original preference remains a separate claim.

## What happened when the questions became political

Researchers subsequently applied the paradigm to political questionnaires. A 2013 Swedish field experiment altered some survey answers and invited participants to reason about positions they appeared to have taken. The published abstract reports detection of no more than 22% of manipulated replies; it also reports 92% acceptance of a revised survey score and a substantial proportion willing to *consider* shifting coalition preference. [4]

That last phrase deserves special care. Willingness to consider a different coalition is **not** a verified change in an actual ballot. The study occurred in a particular election setting using a particular instrument. It provides evidence of vulnerability to an engineered survey mismatch, not license to claim that an advertiser can secretly change how an entire electorate votes.

A 2017 study in Argentina offers a useful counterweight. It implemented the choice-blindness paradigm on political statements in live and web contexts and found variations in confidence and agreement associated with manipulated answers. Crucially, unlike the Swedish result, the authors **did not observe changes in voting intentions**. They described lower confidence on manipulated replies even when participants had not explicitly reported noticing a switch. [5]

Here the research story becomes more interesting than a one-directional headline. Some effects of covert answer substitution can be substantial. Other outcomes do not travel reliably from one context to another. A lower confidence rating and a visible challenge to the manipulation may also be different kinds of detection. Any broad claim about “mind control” would erase those distinctions.

In 2018, researchers reported two additional experiments involving manipulated statements about political issues. Their first sample had 140 participants, and their second had 232. They observed lasting shifts in the attitude ratings they measured, with larger shifts among people who supplied arguments for the altered position. The paper describes a follow-up a week later. [6] It is evidence about a specific intervention and measurement, not an instruction manual for changing beliefs or a justification for deceptive persuasion.

## The hidden issue: whose account becomes the record?

What joins the face, jam and survey experiments is not that the participants were stupid or abnormally suggestible. It is the elevation of an apparently authentic outcome into a source of evidence about an earlier intention.

That is close to a question running through Final Vestige's historical archive. In the late eighteenth century, the Marquis de Puységur accumulated accounts of people who appeared to speak and act differently under what he described as magnetic somnambulism. His memoirs assembled testimony, predictions, procedural descriptions, attestations and failures to support a larger explanatory system. Final Vestige's [Puységur's Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) treats those records as historical evidence of what was *claimed*, and examines the distance between the record and the claimed cause. [7]

There is no legitimate leap from a modern choice-switch experiment to a retrospective diagnosis of Puységur's subjects, and still less to a demonstration that animal magnetism operated. These histories differ in their methods and assumptions. The connection is **methodological**: when a person supplies a plausible report in response to an authority or experimental setting, we should inspect the conditions in which the report was produced before accepting it as a transparent record of internal experience.

The important question is not whether human testimony is worthless. Much history could not be written if that were true. It is whether a story becomes more convincing simply because the narrator seems confident and the explanation arrives smoothly.

## An experiment you can do without tricking anyone

This is an observation exercise, not a replication of choice blindness. It requires no deceptive substitution.

Take a sheet of paper and choose between two low-stakes options: whether to start a small task with a five-minute outline or with five minutes of gathering sources. Before doing either, write down your selection and **one** reason. Spend two minutes doing the selected approach. Then write down any new explanation that occurred to you *afterward*.

Compare the two records. Did the later explanation contain details you could not have known beforehand? Did your preference change once you experienced the task? Is a new reason evidence that the earlier decision never happened? No: it may simply reflect ordinary learning. The exercise illustrates the value of preserving a timestamped record of intention, not the choice-blindness effect itself.

## Where self-hypnosis and meditation fit—and where they do not

It is tempting to market this research as a trick for “rewiring” beliefs. That would be an unsupported extension.

Choice-blindness experiments contain deliberate changes to decision feedback. They do not demonstrate that a meditation recording creates deeper attention, that a hypnosis MP3 alters a person's preferences, or that a brainwave soundtrack controls cognition. Research on suggestion and attention may inform questions about expectation, response and perceived agency; a specific commercial product requires its own relevant evidence.

For readers considering an audio-based routine, the practical questions are more modest: What is actually included? What does the seller promise? Are there independent controlled evaluations of this **specific** product and its claims? What are the current checkout terms? Is there an ordinary, free alternative for the same goal? Those belong in a separate buyer guide—not in the evidentiary conclusion of an investigation.

## The case remains open in the right way

The most startling part of the choice-blindness experiments is often described as the swap itself. The deeper story is what happens afterward. A participant gives an account that sounds like the explanation of an intentional act. The experimenter knows that the event being explained has changed.

We should not conclude that every reason is fictitious. We should conclude that **the ease of producing a reason is not proof of the history of the decision**. When the original answer matters, keep the original answer. When an institution supplies a record, check the chain of custody. When a commercial offer borrows the language of psychology, ask where its product-specific evidence begins.

That discipline is a better defense against manipulation than a promise that a particular audio file makes the mind impervious to it.
