# The Hand You Could See but Did Not Own

**Final Vestige · Mind & Perception · Original investigation · UNPUBLISHED.** Research verified against primary and peer-reviewed sources October 31, 2026. No commercial recommendation appears in this investigation.

Imagine an arm resting on a table. You can see the skin-colored fingers, the wrist and the sleeve, but this hand does not belong to you. It is a convincing imitation. Your own hand rests behind a small screen, outside your view. An experimenter now brushes the visible rubber hand and the concealed real one at matching moments. Your eyes register the brush touching an object. Your skin reports being touched. Under certain conditions the two reports begin to agree upon an impossible location.

Some people describe the rubber hand as though it were part of their body. They know intellectually that it is artificial. That knowledge need not prevent an unusual feeling of ownership. The experience is striking enough to attract theatrical demonstrations and breathless explanations. But the experimental story is far richer than the image of someone flinching when a fake hand is threatened. It asks what a scientific instrument can know about something as private and apparently straightforward as the feeling that a body part belongs to us.

The most important result is not that the hand was fake. It is that researchers could make the mind's everyday certainty about bodily position into a question whose conditions could be changed.

## A correspondence in Nature

Matthew Botvinick and Jonathan Cohen published their brief report, “Rubber hands 'feel' touch that eyes see,” in Nature on February 19, 1998. [1] Their setup made use of a conflict between vision, touch, and proprioception—the ability to judge the location of one's body without continuously looking at it.

A genuine hand lay hidden, while a rubber hand sat in view. In the famous synchronous condition, the brushes touched the concealed and visible hands at approximately the same moments and in corresponding places. The investigators contrasted this coordinated stimulation with conditions in which the timing did not match. Some participants described touch as apparently occurring on the visible artificial hand. Other measurements considered the perceived position of the concealed hand.

Timing was the useful experimental lever. A rubber hand did not have to become a magical object. The researchers could alter a relationship among ordinary sensory signals and ask whether the reported experience changed. That is the difference between showing something astonishing and designing a test that can distinguish possible explanations.

The brain is not a passive screen that receives a single perfect picture of the body. Vision sometimes fails to tell us where a hand is. Skin sensation supplies contact without necessarily giving us a picture. Body-position signals give another kind of evidence. A normally familiar situation joins those sources into a coherent estimate without demanding our attention. The laboratory deliberately pushes them apart.

It is tempting to report that the brain was fooled into thinking a rubber hand had become real. That phrase is memorable, but it compresses three questions into one: did touch seem to occur at the rubber hand; did the participant say the rubber hand felt like theirs; and did their estimate of the real hand's location move toward it? Each requires a different form of measurement and may have a different explanation.

## When measurements disagree

One experimental term is proprioceptive drift. Researchers sometimes ask a participant to indicate where the hidden real hand lies. If that estimate shifts toward the visible artificial hand, an apparent change has occurred. But what exactly has changed? The estimate, the experience of ownership, or both?

In 2011, Marieke Rohde, Massimiliano Di Luca and Marc Ernst published a detailed investigation in PLOS ONE. [2] They varied the timing of measurements and compared synchronous brushing, asynchronous brushing, and mere visual exposure. Their results complicated the popular assumption that positional drift is simply an objective measure of feeling that the rubber hand is one's own. In some conditions drift could be present without an equivalent ownership report. The authors argued that the two measurements need not reflect precisely the same mechanism.

Also in 2011, Henning Holle and colleagues reported an especially instructive dissociation. [3] A rubber hand positioned at a 180-degree rotation could yield increased estimated positional drift under synchronized stimulation even when participants did not report a corresponding feeling of owning the hand. A person could move their estimate while still rejecting the prop as part of their body.

This may sound like a dispute over terminology, but it is a dispute over what a result means. If a measurement changes, investigators should establish the process it captures rather than borrowing the most dramatic label available. It is possible to measure a real phenomenon and misunderstand what one measured.

That is not an argument against scientific measurement. It is an argument for carefully chosen controls and a willingness to report inconsistencies. Agreement between instruments can strengthen an interpretation. Disagreement can reveal that one umbrella term has been hiding several distinct experiences.

## The question of suggestion

The experiment makes an unusual experience likely enough that people may enter it with expectations. An experimenter is holding brushes above a rubber hand. A subject is asked to report what the arrangement feels like. Might some participants work out what the researcher hopes they will say?

In psychology these influences can be called demand characteristics. Such concerns are especially important when a claim depends on the participant describing a private experience. To consider them is not to accuse subjects of lying. Honest reporting can be influenced by framing, social context, interpretation, and the vocabulary an investigator provides.

There is a genuine ongoing debate about how important these factors are to the rubber-hand illusion. In 2022, Mel Slater and H. Henrik Ehrsson published a reanalysis addressing hypnotisability, expectation, and coordinated stimulation. [4] In their analysis the relative advantage for synchronous over asynchronous stimulation remained substantial across levels of measured hypnotisability. They reported a more modest contribution from hypnotisability and a small contribution from measured expectations in their models.

That paper does not prove that expectations never matter. Nor does it license the claim that all ownership reports transparently reveal a single neural process. Other researchers have continued to investigate demand-characteristic challenges in studies of embodiment and presence. A 2022 Scientific Reports article discusses why measuring these subjective states is methodologically difficult. [5]

One camp may emphasize the robustness of matched visual and tactile stimulation. Another may insist that researchers have not adequately insulated the procedure from what participants think is expected. Both raise questions that can be addressed through research designs, not by declaring the debate over.

When a science story tells you that one demonstration proves the mind can be programmed, ask what was actually manipulated, what was reported, and what a comparison group experienced.

## A much older archive of extraordinary experiences

There is a historical connection to Final Vestige's Puységur's Animal Magnetism Files, but it is a connection about evidence, not a shared mechanism. [6] In eighteenth-century accounts of magnetic somnambulism, practitioners and witnesses described altered consciousness, rapport, alleged cures, predictions, and obedience. Those reports were woven into claims for a physical or quasi-physical magnetic cause.

Puységur's archive is valuable partly because it preserves failures, limits, and the ways authority shaped the records. It shows a historical actor trying to transform testimony into evidence through names, witnesses, objects, written statements, and remembered events. The act of documenting a claim does not automatically make its proposed cause correct.

The rubber-hand illusion is not proof of mesmerism. It does not establish that hidden magnetic influence exists, that the earlier reported cures took place as claimed, or that a practitioner could direct a patient's thoughts. Indeed the 1998 study was not designed to examine those eighteenth-century propositions at all.

The useful connection is methodological. Someone may sincerely describe a real sensation while offering an explanation that remains unverified. A scientific explanation requires more than the sincerity of the description. It needs a design capable of distinguishing competing causes.

That insight also keeps modern perception research from becoming convenient advertising language. A discovery that coordinated sensation influences body ownership cannot, by analogy, establish the medical, psychological, or financial benefit of a sound recording. Experience and proposed mechanism must be examined separately.

## Why the body is a hard problem

Most of the time, the body seems too immediate to need explanation. We look down, find a hand at the end of an arm, and move it. We can also reach for something without keeping a continuous watch on the fingers. Ordinary action depends on several sources of information working well enough together.

The rubber-hand arrangement is powerful because it makes those sources disagree. The eyes report one hand in the available scene. The skin reports contact with another hand in a different place. The timing of events can make the visible hand a more persuasive candidate for some aspects of the experience. Changing that timing can weaken the agreement.

But there is no universal switch that makes any object into a body part. The appearance and orientation of the prop, the experiment's control condition, participants' expectations, and the measure chosen can all be relevant. A successful classroom demonstration does not prove that the same response will occur for everyone.

The term ownership is also easily misunderstood. Feeling a temporary illusion of bodily ownership does not erase a person's anatomical knowledge, transfer legal identity, or establish an enduring psychological change. A fleeting subjective result can still teach researchers something without supporting those larger claims.

Science becomes more interesting, not less, when the explanation preserves these boundaries. The demonstration is not a key to unlimited mind control. It is one unusually visible moment in the ordinary work of combining the senses.

## A free observation without a fake experiment

You do not need to buy an apparatus, ask someone to touch you, or risk startling anyone. Try a simple, nonexperimental reflection.

Rest one hand on a table. Look at the position of your index fingertip and notice how confidently you could point to it. Look away while keeping the hand still. Without moving, picture where the fingertip lies relative to the table's edge. Then look back. The exercise may produce no surprising result; that is perfectly ordinary.

This is not a replication of Botvinick and Cohen's experiment. It cannot diagnose suggestibility, measure proprioceptive drift, or tell whether you would experience the rubber-hand illusion. It invites the much smaller observation that seeing your hand and sensing its position are different sources of information that you usually do not need to separate.

If you later watch a rubber-hand demonstration, ask which hand was hidden, whether the touches were simultaneous, whether a mismatch control was used, and whether investigators collected subjective reports or positional estimates. If the video supplies none of those details, the claim may outrun the evidence.

## What the hand reveals

The artificial hand on the table is a prop. The participant's sensation need not be artificial. The boundary between them is the question scientists have tried to understand since the original brief report and in the decades of disagreement that followed.

The lesson is neither that all experiences are suspect nor that a vivid experience proves its explanation. The lesson is that even a feeling as intimate as bodily ownership can depend on how different sources of information arrive and are weighed.

One of the strangest things about the experiment is therefore also one of the simplest: the convincing part was never the rubber. It was the timing.
