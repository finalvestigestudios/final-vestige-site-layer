# The Gorilla in the Lung Scan: When Looking Is Not Seeing

**Final Vestige Studios · Mind & Perception Archive · Original unpublished investigation · November 17, 2026**

*Search intent:* invisible gorilla experiment; radiologists missed gorilla; inattentional blindness; looking versus noticing.

**The evidence question:** When trained observers inspect an image and fail to report something unusual, what have they actually missed—and what is the risk of turning that finding into a commercial claim?

## A gorilla where no gorilla belongs

Picture a workstation with a stack of grayscale chest CT slices. The assigned task is precise: locate small lung nodules. The reader scrolls through anatomical cross sections, marks targets and moves on. Inside one stack, researchers have placed a tiny illustrated gorilla—tiny relative to a person, but approximately **48 times larger than the average nodule** in the task. It is emphatically not a medical finding.

In 2013, Trafton Drew, Melissa L.-H. Võ and Jeremy M. Wolfe tested 24 radiologists on a familiar lung-nodule search. Twenty did not report the gorilla. The astonishing fact is not that a single radiologist missed an odd picture. It is that **20 of 24**, roughly 83%, missed it *under this laboratory task*, even though it was much larger than what they were searching for. [Drew et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797613479386); [open author manuscript](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3964612/).

The headline is irresistible: *Experts stared at a gorilla and couldn't see it*. It is also too imprecise. The researchers used eye tracking. Of the 20 radiologists who missed the gorilla, **12 actually fixated its location while it was visible**. In that subgroup, the mean dwell time was 547 milliseconds. Fixation means the eyes were directed to a location; it does not mean the observer consciously recognized or encoded the gorilla as an object. Eight other radiologists who missed it were not reported as fixating it. The more accurate story is stranger, and more useful: directing one's eyes at an image region is not sufficient for reporting an unexpected object.

We should also resist the inverse error. One oddity in a controlled CT task cannot tell us how often real clinical diagnoses are missed, what causes each diagnostic mistake, or whether a brief meditation recording prevents errors. Those would require very different evidence.

## How the study was constructed

Experiment 1 included the 24 radiologists. They reviewed five chest CT cases, and the gorilla appeared in the last case. They searched for nodules, not cartoons. The last-case placement mattered: participants were not meant to anticipate an absurd interruption. The task was genuinely demanding. Overall nodule detection in the expert group was about 55% in the experiment; this number describes the experimental task, not general professional competence or real-world diagnostic accuracy.

After the final case, the investigators escalated their questions from whether anything looked different or unusual to asking directly whether participants saw a gorilla. Twenty of the 24 did not report the object. Radiologists who missed it spent an average of 5.8 seconds viewing the five image slices containing it. All 24 could recognize the gorilla when it was explicitly shown after the task. That last fact weakens a tempting alternative explanation: perhaps it was simply too faint to be seen.

Experiment 2 was revealing because it did not make the experts look uniquely incapable. An untrained comparison group performed much worse at finding nodules—about 12%, versus 55% for the radiologists—and none of the **25 naïve observers** reported the gorilla. Expertise seems to have helped with the assigned visual-search task and may have helped somewhat with detecting the surprise. Yet expertise plainly did not abolish the effect.

Experiment 3 asked a different question: was the gorilla inherently invisible in the CT image? When observers explicitly looked for the gorilla, they performed near ceiling, approximately 88% correct, even with brief presentation. That result supports the explanation that the earlier failures depended substantially on **task set**, not merely pixel visibility. It is not a proof that any one person consciously saw the object but failed to remember it; the design does not directly read private experience.

Source: [Drew, Võ & Wolfe, *Psychological Science* 24 (2013), 1848–1853](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3964612/), especially Experiments 1–3 and Results. The open full text is an author manuscript; the publisher [record](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797613479386) identifies the version of record.

## Where the experiment came from

Fourteen years earlier, Daniel Simons and Christopher Chabris published “Gorillas in our midst.” Participants watched a dynamic scene and monitored one part of the action while an unexpected actor appeared. That family of experiments is a lesson in **inattentional blindness**: an unexpected object may go unreported when attention is allocated to a different task. The original paper emphasized task difficulty and similarity between the unexpected object and attended objects, rather than a simple rule that everyone misses a gorilla half the time. [Simons & Chabris, 1999](https://doi.org/10.1068/p281059).

This should not be confused with **change blindness**. In the latter, something about a scene changes, often across a visual interruption, without the person detecting the difference. Final Vestige's existing [change-blindness investigation](https://finalvestige.com/2026/11/02/change-blindness/) examines a flicker task and an on-campus switch of conversation partners. The gorilla-in-CT study asks whether people report an object that is there while they are searching for something else, not whether they compare two versions of a scene.

These studies undermine a folk theory of vision: that if our eyes point at a feature, its significance will be filed automatically in awareness. A better model is selective. Attention helps determine which features become reportable. That does not mean conscious experience is absent at every unattended location, nor that a photograph can tell us precisely how attention works in every person or setting.

## The 2024 correction to the viral story

In March 2024, Connor Hults and colleagues—including Simons—published an open review of inattentional blindness in medicine. They surveyed work spanning radiology, surgery, nursing and augmented reality. Their conclusion is more cautious than many popular retellings. Missed objects and abnormalities are real and important, but **many studies cannot separate inattentional blindness cleanly from other failures of attention or task performance**. [Hults et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1186/s41235-024-00537-x).

Why such caution? Consider a surgical simulation containing repeated unexpected signals. The first truly unexpected event may induce a different mindset: from then on, the observer is looking for surprises. Multiple trials of “unexpected” stimuli no longer measure the same thing as an unannounced first encounter. Or consider a medical task in which finding every abnormality is a normal duty. Missing a second clinically meaningful finding may be a failure of search, divided attention, memory, or expectations. Labeling all such misses “inattentional blindness” prematurely decides the mechanism.

The review also notes that the evidence about **expertise** remains limited by small study samples and task differences. The 2013 radiologists had specialized knowledge; the novices had less. But interpreting the difference as a universal percentage improvement caused by expertise would exceed what those cases can establish. The 2024 authors specifically call for research designs that distinguish an unanticipated object from an expected incidental finding and report instructions and noticing measures transparently.

There is a practical editorial lesson here. An extraordinary result can be real and still require a smaller, more exact claim than its headline. The stronger the surprise, the greater the obligation to preserve the experimental conditions.

## How an evidence trail turns into an authority claim

The [Puységur Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) form an unlikely but productive companion to the gorilla study. In the late eighteenth century, Puységur built elaborate documentary machinery around magnetic somnambulism: named witnesses, notarial deposits, patient declarations, predictions written before their outcomes, failures that remained in the record, and warnings about what an operator might induce by questioning a dependent subject.

No one should pretend the 2013 CT experiment proves or disproves animal magnetism. These are different times, procedures and claims. The connection is methodological: **a well-documented observation is not the same as a demonstrated mechanism**. A notary can document that testimony was recorded; notarial authority does not establish the hidden force that purportedly produced it. An eye tracker can document gaze location; it cannot, on its own, report the observer's conscious experience. Both cases become misleading when a genuine evidentiary instrument is asked to prove more than it measures.

The most troubling passage of Puységur's archive concerns consent and operator pressure. Some subjects asked to be left alone; some appeared to answer under strong expectations; later notes warned about abuse of power. That history is a reason to treat suggestibility and guided attention with humility, not to promise easy control of the mind.

A retail audio program may offer a convenient way to make room for reflection. It should not borrow the prestige of unrelated historical accounts or a striking perception experiment to certify an outcome that its own product has not independently demonstrated.

## A free twelve-minute field observation, without deception

This is **not** a recreation of the CT study. It needs no surprise gorilla, patient images, third-party subjects, copyrighted laboratory footage or claims of diagnosis.

**Minutes 0–2: Choose the target.** Pick an ordinary object-rich, safe location—a desk, a shelf, an illustrated book page you own. Privately write one narrow search question, such as “Which items are blue?” Avoid driving, roadways or anything where narrowing attention could create danger.

**Minutes 2–5: Record a first pass.** Examine the scene for your chosen target for up to three minutes. List your observations, but do not force yourself to guess unnoticed objects. The aim is to see how a task changes your description, not to engineer failure.

**Minutes 5–7: Change the question.** Without rearranging any objects, ask “What occupies the far left?” or “What textures are present?” Look again and record what you had not previously written down. These are now *new noticing opportunities*, not evidence that an unseen phenomenon occurred earlier.

**Minutes 7–10: Compare descriptions.** Put your two lists side by side. Separate a new observation from a memory gap and from a possible oversight. If you recorded a photograph yourself, you may use it to verify what was there, but do not present the exercise as a scientific test of inattentional blindness.

**Minutes 10–12: Decide what follows.** Write one useful work habit: perhaps pause after completing a narrow search to ask a broader question, or invite a second independent review where accuracy matters. Do not claim that this habit is clinically proven to prevent medical errors. It is an inexpensive procedural idea, not a therapeutic intervention.

## What we know, and what we do not

**Supported by the studies:** an unanticipated illustrated gorilla was missed by 20 of 24 radiologists performing a nodule search in a lab; 12 of those 20 had eye fixations at its location; the task and expectations mattered. Earlier dynamic-scene experiments had established a related inattentional-blindness phenomenon, and a later medical review urges sharper claims about mechanisms.

**Not supported:** the claim that radiologists ordinarily miss 83% of medically important findings; that eye fixation proves full conscious recognition; that all professionals are equally vulnerable; that an audio product fixes the effect; or that the Puységur historical accounts establish the modern neuroscience of suggestion.

The lasting fact is less sensational than the headline and more troubling: perception depends partly on what the observer has been asked to find. The crucial editorial habit is to ask which question an experiment actually answered—and which question an advertisement would like us to imagine it answered.
