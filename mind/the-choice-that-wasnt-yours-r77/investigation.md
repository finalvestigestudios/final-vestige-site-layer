# The Choice That Wasn't Yours
### The strange experiment in which people explained a decision they had never made

*Final Vestige Studios | Mind & Perception Archive | Investigation draft | 2 November 2026 | UNPUBLISHED*

Imagine two portrait photographs on a tabletop. You look from one to the other and choose the face you find more appealing. The researcher gives you the photograph and asks why you preferred it. You begin talking: the expression seemed warmer; perhaps the eyes were more inviting.

Only the photograph in your hand isn't the one you chose.

This is not a fictional scene built around a magician's revelation. It is the central problem posed by a 2005 experiment from Petter Johansson, Lars Hall, Sverker Sikström and Andreas Olsson, published in *Science*. The researchers used a sleight-of-hand procedure to create a discrepancy between a participant's selected face and the picture later presented as their selection. On some trials, people did not detect the swap. They nevertheless supplied reasons for preferring the face now in front of them. [1]

The experiment does **not** prove people lack preferences. Nor does it establish that human decisions are generally manufactured by strangers, that memory can always be rewritten, or that suggestive audio can alter anyone's beliefs. It exposes a narrower, more unsettling possibility: **our account of why we made a choice can sometimes be constructed after the outcome is placed before us.**

## A decision made in public, a reason supplied in private

The face-selection task depended on a specific sequence. First came a real choice. Participants compared pairs of photographs and indicated which they preferred. Next, on certain trials, the researchers covertly substituted the unchosen image for the chosen one. Finally, the participant was invited to explain the preference.

The crucial scientific comparison was not whether the reasons sounded persuasive to a reader. It was whether the participant detected the discrepancy and how they behaved after it occurred. The researchers reported failures to notice conspicuous mismatches and recorded participants' explanations for the substituted outcomes. They called the phenomenon **choice blindness**. [1]

It is tempting to hear this as the old saying that people are easily fooled. That description misses the point. The mistake takes place at a seam between intention and later explanation. A person can genuinely remember participating in a choice, genuinely hear themselves offering a reason, and still be mistaken about the identity of the object they originally selected. The resulting explanation may be fluent and sincere. Sincerity, however, is evidence about what the speaker believes—not a guarantee that the speaker has access to the process that produced the belief.

The experiment also raises an uncomfortable methodological question. When the investigator controls the encounter, the objects, and the timing of the reveal, the participant's ability to catch the manipulation is partly a property of the **procedure**. A different image pair, different speed, different warning, or different opportunity to compare could alter detection. We should never turn a result from one carefully engineered task into a universal diagnosis of ordinary decision-making.

## The confession nobody had to coerce

In 2006, Hall and colleagues continued examining the introspective explanations produced in the choice-blindness paradigm. They were interested in the language of self-explanation: what can somebody tell us about a decision when the outcome shown to them is not the one they chose? Their analysis places the experiment beside an older psychological problem, famously explored by Richard Nisbett and Timothy Wilson: the limits of direct verbal access to mental processes. [2]

The subtlety matters. Someone asked to explain a decision is not necessarily lying; often they are responding to a social request with the material available to consciousness. We make sense of things. Faced with an apparent result, we naturally search for a coherent account of how we got there.

That interpretive labor is normally useful. Without it, we would struggle to narrate our histories, learn from decisions, or talk about preferences. The danger arrives when the explanation's confidence is mistaken for independent evidence that the underlying event happened exactly as told.

In a detective story, this is the moment when the witness's detail seems too good to doubt. In psychology, the same detail becomes something to investigate. The experimenter asks: can we verify the earlier choice? Can we measure detection independently of the story? Can the person correct the record when given another opportunity?

## When the paper survey changed itself

The research did not remain confined to photographs. In 2012, Hall, Johansson and Thomas Strandberg published a study of a paper questionnaire on moral opinions. In a controlled procedure, some statements were altered so that respondents were subsequently confronted with the opposite of their earlier answers. The investigators then asked them to discuss those altered positions.

**Sixty-nine percent of participants failed to detect at least one of two reversals** in the conditions described in the published report. Participants sometimes supplied coherent arguments in favor of positions that contradicted what they had initially endorsed. [3]

The number is striking, but the interpretation requires restraint. It does not mean 69 percent of people's values are fake, and it is not a forecast of how a random person would behave in a different setting. It refers to particular manipulations in a particular experiment, with an operational definition of detection.

A third study, published in 2013, adapted choice-blindness methods to political attitudes during a Swedish election. The authors reported that participants often accepted a manipulated summary of their previous survey responses. This is evidence that some expressed positions are sensitive to how a prior answer is represented. It is **not** a warrant for psychological manipulation, nor evidence that a specific modern campaign uses the same mechanism. [4]

There is a deeper civic lesson here. Polls, questionnaires and interviews are not transparent windows into a person. They are interactions between a person, the wording of a question, the recording method, the timing, and the version of their answer that is later presented back to them. Reliable inquiry demands that each part of this chain remain inspectable.

## Do we change the choice, or merely the account of it?

This is where careless retellings go wrong. Three propositions are often collapsed into one.

First: someone can fail to notice that a presented outcome differs from the earlier choice. This is what the experimental manipulations test directly.

Second: someone can produce a reason for the outcome they believe they chose. The recorded explanations speak to this.

Third: someone's fundamental preferences or beliefs have been permanently transformed by the manipulation. That requires further evidence and follow-up. A fluent explanation does not by itself demonstrate durable belief change.

The distinction between **detection**, **rationalization**, and **lasting change** prevents a fascinating experiment from turning into a grand promise about personal transformation.

The same caution should govern any conversation about hypnosis, self-hypnosis, meditation or suggestion. A participant reporting an experience may be truthful. A seller offering an explanation for how that experience occurred may still be wrong. The mechanism must be tested separately, and a finding involving photographs or paper surveys cannot serve as proof that an audio product produces therapeutic, cognitive or financial outcomes.

## An eighteenth-century echo, without an invented connection

Final Vestige's published *Puységur's Animal Magnetism Files* reconstructs a much older dispute over suggestion, therapeutic authority, personal testimony and what historical records can establish. The original cases belong to their own century, their own culture and their own evidentiary conditions. They are not early experiments in choice blindness, and nothing in the modern research validates animal magnetism.

The useful bridge is methodological. Puységur's world left behind assertions, observations, reports and later interpretations. In the choice-blindness laboratory, the decisive improvement is that the researcher can independently preserve the participant's original selection. The visible record does not have to depend on the participant's later account.

That is the difference between **having a story** and **having an audit trail**.

A research archive should make that difference obvious. Original document: what actually says what? Contemporary observer: who recorded it, and under what pressures? Interpretation: what was inferred? Replication: what can be repeated? Commercial claim: who benefits if a reader accepts the conclusion?

These categories are not rhetorical decorations. They are how investigators protect a story from the ambitions of its teller—including their own.

## A safe field test: keep the first answer

Choice blindness is difficult to reproduce ethically at home because the original studies depended on controlled deception and consent procedures. Final Vestige does not recommend secretly reversing another person's answer, testing a partner's loyalties, or using a trick to extract a declaration of belief.

There is, however, a useful nondeceptive practice that demonstrates the importance of a contemporaneous record.

Choose an ordinary, low-stakes question: Which of two book covers would you rather read? Which of two landscapes feels more inviting? Write down your answer and a one-sentence reason **before** looking at anyone else's opinion. Take a break. Later, read the first note and add a second note about whether your reasoning has changed.

You might get the same answer. You might realize the reason was incomplete. Neither result is a diagnosis. The exercise simply keeps an earlier record available instead of asking your later memory to stand in for it.

For a stronger comparison, preserve the original photograph or prompt too. No need for a stranger to perform a trick. No need to promise a revelation. The value lies in seeing the paper trail.

## What remains uncertain

The original experiments reveal real vulnerabilities in awareness and retrospective explanation under certain conditions. They do not provide a single number for everybody's susceptibility, nor a clinical test of suggestibility. Laboratory task details, expectations, experimental demands, prior familiarity, and participants' opportunities to verify outcomes all matter.

The 2012 and 2013 extensions show that the concept travels to more consequential kinds of questions, but they also introduce the complexities of values, language, social identity and survey setting. They do not settle how stable every moral or political conviction is outside those settings.

The research is most powerful when it refuses to tell us more than it measured. That restraint is not a weakness. It is what makes the findings worth taking seriously.

And so we return to the photograph. A person chose one face, received another, and began to explain. The surprising part is not merely that a trick could work. It is that the explanation could sound as though the gap had never existed.

The mind can tell a persuasive story about a choice. Before we trust the story, we should ask whether we still have the choice.
