# The Gorilla Was Right There: How Expert Eyes Missed an Unexpected Figure
**Final Vestige Studios | Mind & Perception | Original evidence investigation | 7 November 2026 | UNPUBLISHED**

*In a controlled study, twenty of twenty-four radiologists did not report a gorilla inserted into a lung scan. Twelve of those twenty had looked at its location. What, exactly, did that mean?*

## A figure inside a stack of images

There is a special kind of concentration that comes with searching medical images. A radiologist interpreting a lung CT moves through successive cross-sections, following anatomical patterns and looking for small abnormalities. The act of looking is deliberate, not casual. The stakes of real clinical work can be serious, although the experiment that concerns us took place in a research setting, not a real diagnostic decision.

In 2013, Trafton Drew, Melissa Võ, and Jeremy Wolfe published a startling laboratory result. They had twenty-four radiologists search five lung CT scans for nodules. Into the last scan they inserted a white-outlined gorilla. The gorilla was far larger than the nodules they were looking for. And twenty of the twenty-four radiologists did not report noticing it. The researchers called the phenomenon *inattentional blindness*: failure to notice an unexpected object while attention is occupied with another task.

The word “blindness” is tempting, and potentially misleading. These people were not visually impaired. The figure was physically presented in the images. They were not asked to search for it. They were asked to search for nodules. The difference between those two assignments turns out to be the important part of the story.

The [2013 primary paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3964612/) supplies the experimental details. It is also a reminder of a central Final Vestige principle: the more cinematic the claim, the more carefully we should inspect what the evidence actually measured.

## The experiment behind the headline

A standard CT scan is a series of images, usually explored by scrolling. The final experimental scan in Drew and colleagues' study contained **239 slices**. The gorilla appeared across **five** of them, its opacity rising and then falling to avoid a sudden, attention-grabbing onset. It was positioned near a nodule. The approximate volume of the figure was **48 times** the mean nodule volume in the images.

Participants were told to identify and click on nodules. Nine radiologists were tested in Boston and fifteen at a professional meeting in Louisville. An eye-tracking apparatus recorded where they looked. The participants could move through the scans freely within the research protocol.

After the final scan, investigators asked whether anything had seemed different, whether anything unusual appeared, and eventually whether the participant had seen a gorilla. **Twenty of twenty-four** did not report it. This is approximately **83% of this small experimental sample**. It is not a population estimate of medical error, nor a claim that eight in ten radiologists miss surprising abnormalities in practice.

On average, radiologists who missed the gorilla spent **5.8 seconds** viewing the slices in which it appeared. Across the search they encountered those slices more than once. That is a long time compared with a fleeting television frame, but the task involved moving through complicated medical images in search of something else.

Then came the eye-tracking result: **twelve of the twenty who missed the gorilla fixated its location** when it was present. Those twelve spent an average of **547 milliseconds** looking there. Eye position is not identical to attention, recognition, or conscious report. But it provides unusual evidence that the critical location was available to their sight without producing an acknowledged sighting of the figure.

That separation is the heart of the investigation. A ray of light reaching the eye does not automatically become a meaningful item in awareness.

## The familiar gorilla came first

The medical-image study was a deliberate successor to a better-known experiment. In 1999, Daniel Simons and Christopher Chabris published [*Gorillas in Our Midst*](https://doi.org/10.1068/p281059), which used a dynamic video with groups of people passing basketballs. Viewers performed an attention-demanding monitoring task while an unexpected person in a gorilla costume appeared.

The viral retelling says simply that people missed an enormous gorilla. The paper itself is more useful than that. It asks which unexpected objects are noticed, how similarity to attended objects matters, and how the difficulty of the assigned task changes awareness. Unexpectedness is not a magic property possessed by a costume. What observers are trying to do determines which features are likely to receive attention.

There is another caution. Someone who knows the gorilla will appear is no longer in the original surprise condition. Showing a familiar viral clip again and again may create a memorable lesson, but subsequent viewings are not independent reproductions of the first experiment.

The 2013 team wanted to know whether trained visual experts, performing work in their own domain, would be immune. If years of experience teach an observer where to look and what to notice, perhaps expertise would catch the unexpected.

Expertise helped with the assigned task. It did not eliminate the surprise effect.

## The study did not show that specialists are poor observers

The radiologists detected roughly **55%** of the nodules in the experimental images. That figure must not be projected onto ordinary clinical practice: the task, images, time limits, and experimental environment were specific. It tells us that the assigned search was demanding even for professionals.

A second experiment brought in novice observers. They received brief training in detecting nodules, then searched similar CT images. The novice participants did not report the gorilla in the surprise trial; their nodule-detection performance was much lower, about **12%**. The authors considered this compatible with some expert advantage, but the comparison cannot cleanly isolate the cause. Familiarity, ability, cognitive load, and search strategy all differed.

Perhaps more surprising, the researchers did not simply assume that the gorilla was visible. They created a separate control experiment. Twelve novice observers watched the CT case as a movie and were explicitly told where a gorilla might appear and to judge its presence. Under those directed-attention conditions, performance was approximately **88% correct** despite short exposures.

That control matters because the obvious objection is reasonable: perhaps the figure was too indistinct to identify when embedded in a scan. The control suggests it could be recognized under a task that made the figure relevant. It does not guarantee that every participant under every display condition would see it.

The investigators also explored “satisfaction of search,” a phenomenon in which finding one target can affect searching for others. The inserted gorilla lay near a nodule. If discovering the nodule alone explained the miss, one might expect observers who missed that nodule to notice the gorilla more often. The paper reports that no participant who missed that local nodule detected the gorilla. This observation weakens a simple version of the explanation; it does not settle every question about search behavior.

## Why a gaze record is not a mind-reading machine

It sounds spectacular to say that doctors stared directly at a gorilla without seeing it. But every word in that sentence has methodological weight.

An eye tracker records where the eyes are directed. It cannot by itself determine what information a person recognizes, consciously experiences, later remembers, or considers worth reporting. The task questionnaire records answers, not a complete map of subjective experience. In between those measures, attention, perception, memory, and response decisions interact.

A fixation lasting half a second does not certify recognition. Nor is an unreported object proof that zero processing occurred. The more defensible claim is narrower: people can orient their gaze toward an unexpected object without subsequently reporting awareness of it during an attention-demanding task.

The question sequence after the trial—first general difference, then anything unusual, then a gorilla—was designed to probe noticing without relying on a single spontaneous comment. That improves interpretation, but it does not provide a perfect separation between not perceiving and not recalling. Scientific language is most valuable where it leaves a little uncertainty visible.

It is also worth separating this phenomenon from **change blindness**. Change blindness concerns a difference between two moments going unnoticed, often when a flicker, camera cut, or interruption masks the transition. Inattentional blindness concerns an unexpected object that goes unreported while another task occupies attention. The experiences can resemble each other, but their experimental manipulations differ. Final Vestige's [published Change Blindness investigation](https://finalvestige.com/2026/11/02/change-blindness/) provides a useful companion.

## Does being an expert make the effect worse?

No general conclusion of that sort follows from this study. Expertise may make task-relevant signals more salient; it can also narrow the observer's expectations. The paper reports the striking vulnerability of experts under one search task. It does not conclude that training makes people more blind, that expertise is futile, or that less-experienced readers would find the scan safer to interpret.

The novice group performed substantially worse on the primary nodule task, complicating a direct comparison. The authors explicitly interpreted their result as evidence that professional experience does not confer immunity, not as proof that radiologists are defective at their profession.

This distinction is not etiquette. It is the difference between a defensible finding and a harmful extrapolation. In a safety-critical field, accurate descriptions of research populations and tasks matter. A laboratory silhouette in a CT image is not a real lesion. This paper should not be treated as a diagnostic benchmark or as advice to bypass established medical procedures.

The more useful implication is about **search design**. When a task requires detecting a narrow class of targets, observers may be less prepared to register an item that does not fit those expectations. Whether a different checklist, second reader, algorithmic alert, or training method would improve safety is a separate intervention question. This study alone does not validate any such remedy.

## The older question: whose interpretation is evidence?

Final Vestige's [Puységur Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) concern eighteenth-century accounts of trance-like states, suggestion, and dramatic claims about perception. They are important historical source materials partly because they record what people said happened and how observers understood those events. The documents do not, by themselves, demonstrate that a supposed magnetic fluid existed.

There is a limited analogy between those records and the gorilla experiment: sincerity and explanation are separate. A radiologist may sincerely report seeing nothing unexpected while the eye tracker records a fixation at the critical location. A historical witness may sincerely report a trance while later explanations of its cause remain uncertain.

The analogy should go no further. A CT search experiment does not test mesmerism, Puységur's methods, hypnotic responsiveness, or the truth of any historical doctrine. The [published Mesmerism investigation](https://finalvestige.com/2026/11/02/mesmerism/) is a better route into the historical evidence. What connects the subjects is an investigative habit: preserve what was observed, distinguish what was inferred, and examine what a proposed mechanism would need to establish.

That habit is particularly important when today's advertiser borrows yesterday's scientific vocabulary.

## A free observation practice, with no false promises

Here is an original, optional exercise. It is **not a validated inattentional-blindness test**, does not reproduce either gorilla experiment, and must not be used to judge medical or professional competence.

Ask a willing friend to arrange ten everyday objects on a table. Spend thirty seconds looking at them while you concentrate on counting objects that have a circular edge. Then turn away and write down everything you can recall, whether circular or not. Compare the result with the original arrangement.

You may find that the features needed for the counting task are easier to recall than unrelated details. Or you may remember nearly everything. Both are possible. Since there was no genuine unexpected event, and because this exercise contains a memory component, the result tells you nothing reliable about how you would have performed in the gorilla study.

Its value is the question it leaves behind: **What was I attending to, and what did my task make irrelevant?** Before reviewing an important record, write down your search question. Afterward, consciously consider whether a second, different question might reveal something else. This is a general reflection exercise, not a proven clinical or safety intervention.

## The lesson is not that your eyes cannot be trusted

The photograph of a gorilla inside a scan is memorable because it violates our intuition. We think that a conspicuous object should be conspicuous to everyone, all the time. But the experiment shows that *conspicuousness is partly a relationship between the object and the job an observer has been asked to perform*.

The radiologists were engaged in a difficult, familiar task. Most did not report an inserted figure that could be identified when attention was redirected. Some had looked at the location without reporting it. Those are extraordinary observations, presented with ordinary scientific limitations: a modest sample, artificial stimulus, specific laboratory procedure, and observational measures that do not fully reveal conscious experience.

This is not a reason to distrust expertise or to purchase a promised mental upgrade. It is a reason to distrust the easy assumption that looking and knowing are the same.

**When an explanation seems obvious, first ask: what was the observer actually looking for?**
