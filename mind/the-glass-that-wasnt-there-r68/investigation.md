# The Glass That Wasn't There
## How a question can leave a trace inside a memory

**Final Vestige Studios — Mind & Perception Archive | Investigation draft | November 2, 2026**  
**Editorial status:** UNPUBLISHED. Independent research. No commercial recommendation.

There was no broken glass in the film. That is the detail worth carrying into the next room.

In 1974, psychologists Elizabeth Loftus and John Palmer reported an experiment that now sits at the center of the science of eyewitness memory. Participants watched films of automobile accidents. Later, someone asked about the vehicles' speed. The question was not quite the same for everyone. For some, the cars had *hit* each other; for others, they had *smashed* into one another. A verb had slipped into the space between an event and the account of it.

In the second experiment, participants returned to the question of broken glass. The film had shown none. Yet those who had heard the more forceful verb were more likely to report that they had seen glass. The researchers did not demonstrate that every witness was deceived, or that language can manufacture any memory at will. They showed something narrower, more unsettling, and more useful: information introduced **after** an event can affect how people subsequently report that event. [1]

A camera records the light that passes through its lens. Memory is different. It is built, retained, retrieved and interpreted inside a system that continues to receive information after the camera would have stopped rolling.

### The experiment, without the legend

The first Loftus–Palmer experiment tested how verb choice affected speed estimates after film clips. The later glass question belonged to a second experiment. Keeping these experiments distinct matters. The famous story is often compressed into a single demonstration, as if everybody heard one sentence and thereafter saw shards scattering across the road.

That is not what the article establishes. It reports differences **between experimental groups** under specified viewing and questioning conditions. To assess a result, we need to know who saw what, when the question was asked, which response was measured, and what comparison group existed. A difference in estimated speed is not the same outcome as a later yes/no report of glass. And a report on a questionnaire is not direct access to the detailed subjective contents of a remembered scene.

This separation between what a person reports and the mechanism that generated the report would become a central scientific dispute. Did later information alter the memory representation itself? Did witnesses retain competing details but use the recent suggestion while answering? Were they uncertain about which source supplied the detail? Several processes might contribute, and one experimental result does not settle them all.

### The stop sign that could be rewritten

Four years later, Loftus, David Miller and Helen Burns published a larger set of experiments. Across five studies and a pilot, **1,242 participants** viewed slides of an automobile–pedestrian accident. They received information consistent with, unrelated to, or misleading about what they had seen. On later recognition tests, misleading post-event information reduced accuracy. The effects varied with procedure and timing. [2]

The number 1,242 is the **total across the reported studies**, not the size of a single experimental cell and not a claim that 1,242 witnesses all formed false memories. The study's importance lies in a reproducible procedure: show an event, change one piece of information later, and then compare subsequent judgments.

A useful way to read both papers is as evidence for the vulnerability of the **information chain**. The original perceptual event is only one input. The question, the intervening narrative, other people and the retrieval context may become inputs too.

That does not mean the original event has necessarily disappeared. Psychologists have debated whether some effects arise from alteration of stored information, interference, source confusion, or response strategies. The reasonable conclusion for an evidence archive is not that memory is always false. It is that the later report is not automatically a clean copy of the first observation.

### Two witnesses, two films

In 2003, Fiona Gabbert, Amina Memon and Kevin Allan reported a study that moved the problem from a questioner's wording to an ordinary social exchange. Two people believed they were discussing the same filmed incident. In fact, each had seen a version containing some information the other had not. Some pairs discussed the event before giving separate recollections; control participants did not discuss it.

According to the published abstract, **71% of the witnesses in the discussion condition** mistakenly recalled at least one item acquired from the other witness. [3] That is a condition-specific result, not a claim that 71% of all conversations contaminate memory.

The study illustrates a particularly difficult provenance problem. If a detail is heard during a conversation about a witnessed event, it may later feel as though it came from the event itself. The speaker need not be malicious; the shared narrative need not be entirely wrong. An inaccurate detail can travel inside an otherwise sincere conversation.

For Final Vestige, this resembles a familiar archival problem. A newspaper repeats a survivor. A later historian quotes the newspaper. A documentary quotes the historian. Four citations may appear to support the claim, but all four may trace to one uncorroborated first account. Repetition is not independent confirmation.

### A false story and the feeling of remembering

The risk extends beyond laboratory accidents. In 2024, Schincariol and colleagues published a meta-analysis of experiments studying exposure to fabricated news. Their synthesis included **13 articles**. Across the included research, approximately **40% of participants reported at least one false memory**, and about **60% reported at least one false belief** after exposure. However, the average percentage of presented false events an individual remembered or believed was much lower, about **22%**. [4]

These are different denominators and different outcomes. “At least one” among several stories is not the same as the probability of believing any particular story. Believing an event happened is not the same as reporting a memory of experiencing or seeing it. Those results cannot be generalized uncritically to all news, all populations or every misleading headline.

The meta-analysis also shows why these studies belong in the evidence section, not in a sensational video promising to reveal that nothing you remember is real. The psychological effect is conditional. People vary. The content, plausibility and experimental procedure vary. Averages describe study samples, not a universal law of mental weakness.

### The 1784 archive: a different kind of witness

Our existing [Puységur's Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) reproduces and analyzes claims from the history of animal magnetism. Its record matters because Puységur and his contemporaries described striking behavior and attempted to interpret what they had encountered. Some reports include unsuccessful cases and later retellings. None of this makes the proposed magnetic mechanism an established fact.

The Loftus studies are not tests of eighteenth-century magnetism. They do offer a methodological warning relevant to **any** archive of unusual testimony: the original description, a later interpretation and subsequent retellings are three different kinds of evidence. A witness's sincerity cannot alone identify the mechanism of an event. Nor does uncertainty justify declaring every witness mistaken.

The discipline is to preserve the provenance of the claim. We should ask what the witness could directly observe, how soon they recorded it, which questions were asked, whether other witnesses shared information, and whether later authors copied a common source.

### A free demonstration: the question before the answer

Here is an exercise that does not require a recording, a stranger, or a product.

Write a neutral description of a small, everyday event you can verify: a person put a blue notebook beside a cup, then moved it to a shelf. Set it aside. Give two volunteers the **same written description**, but later ask each a different question. For one, ask, “Where was the notebook moved?” For the other, ask, “Where was the heavy notebook moved?” The original description never said the notebook was heavy.

Before you try it, write down what you predict. Then ask them to distinguish *what the original sentence stated* from *what the question suggested*. Compare their written answers, but do not mislead them about a consequential real event or pretend you are conducting a valid psychological experiment.

This exercise demonstrates how to **audit a leading question**. It does not establish a false-memory rate, induce a lasting memory, measure someone's susceptibility, or reproduce the Loftus experiments. That would require controlled materials, consent, counterbalancing and many more observations.

You can also do it alone on paper: underline every adjective that appears for the first time in the *question*, not the original evidence. Mark those words as **post-event information**. The habit is worth more than an improvised score.

### What the research does not license

A claim that **all** memory is unreliable is as unscientific as a claim that memory is a perfect recording. Experimental misinformation effects do not mean an account of abuse, a witness statement, or a childhood recollection is false merely because it was recalled later. Accuracy must be assessed through the particulars and independently available evidence, not inferred from the existence of a psychological phenomenon.

Nor do these studies establish that commercial audio, suggestion, hypnosis or meditation can insert or erase any desired experience. They were not trials of Zen12, Hypnosis Live or any other product. General findings about memory and suggestion cannot honestly be converted into a named product's efficacy claim.

The practical lesson is a modest, durable procedure: record early, separate observation from interpretation, preserve original notes, identify the first source for every detail, and avoid exchanging accounts before independent recollections when circumstances permit. These safeguards cannot guarantee accuracy. They can make it easier to see where a later idea entered the story.

### The glass, again

The image of shattered glass is difficult to resist. The experiment's point was that a witness might report seeing glass precisely where none appeared. The scientific achievement was not the creation of a ghostly image. It was the ability to trace a measurable difference back to a carefully varied question.

That is the part worth remembering: sometimes the object that changed was not the scene. It was the sentence spoken afterward.
