The Word That Wouldn't Let the Color Through

A printed word tried to answer a question about its ink. The difference between the two answers transformed the study of attention.

Historical experiment and source-led interpretation · Not a diagnostic test

What the words do

When GREEN is printed in blue ink, reading and ink-naming ask for different responses. You may know perfectly well which response the experimenter wants and still feel the word interfere.

In 1935, J. Ridley Stroop measured two distinct conflicts: reading color words printed in incongruent inks, and naming the inks when the printed words pointed elsewhere. The second task generated substantially more interference than the first. That asymmetry is the key, not a moral contest over willpower.

What the original study reported

Reading 100 conflicting color words took about 2.3 seconds longer (5.6%; not considered reliable). Naming 100 ink colors when the words conflicted took about 47.0 seconds longer (74.3%) than its control. These are results from the historical experiment, not universal benchmarks.

Read the original DOI and published abstract.

The explanation grew up

The shorthand says reading is automatic. Yet Colin MacLeod's 1991 survey of approximately four hundred studies argued that simple speed-of-reading and all-or-nothing automaticity accounts could not accommodate the entire body of evidence. Task rules, learned responses and parallel processing complicate the story.

The psychological finding is robust enough to investigate; the brain slogan is not a substitute for the experiments.

A two-minute paper observation

Make a line of color words in black ink and a separate line whose ink colors disagree with the words. Read the first line, then name the ink rather than the words in the second. Notice hesitation or correction without scoring yourself. Have a partner randomize order if you wish.

This is not a controlled replication. Reading history, color discrimination, materials and timing all matter. If colors are inaccessible, a conflicting word-and-arrow direction task can illustrate the broader idea of response conflict without replicating the original Stroop procedure.

Record what you observed and what you inferred as separate statements. It is more useful than pretending that a homemade experiment produces a diagnosis.

The witness and the experiment

Our published Puységur archive separates written testimony and historical provenance from evidence for the operator's proposed magnetic mechanism. The Stroop experiment teaches a distinct but sympathetic discipline: distinguish an observed interference effect from whatever convenient explanation arrives first.

A signed account can document a claim without proving its mechanism. A colored word can reveal response competition without revealing your clinical health. Different material; the same refusal to substitute the story for the record.

Why this does not prove a meditation product works

Modern reviews of mindfulness-based interventions report mixed executive-function outcomes. A 2021 review of 29 group-based mindfulness studies did not find the overall executive-function effect robust. Another 2021 meta-analysis of 25 controlled studies found a small executive-function effect, with nonsignificant effects in several other cognitive domains. Neither tested Zen12, Hypnosis Live or Brainwave Shots.

Evidence ledger and limits

Observed: conflicting words interfere with an ink-naming task in controlled conditions. Not established here: a clinical diagnosis, a universal brain-training protocol or benefits from any proprietary recording.
