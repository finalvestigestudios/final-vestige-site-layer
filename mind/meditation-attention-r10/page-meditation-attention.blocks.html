The Second Number Was There All Along

An intensive meditation retreat changed performance on an attention test. Here is what the research showed, what it left unresolved, and why a commercial audio file cannot inherit its results.

On a computer screen, a procession of letters arrives too quickly to linger over. Amid them, a number appears. Then another. Both are plain enough when seen alone. Yet ask a person to identify the first and the second becomes strangely difficult to report. There is no dark screen, no eyelid in the way, no missing pixel. The second number was there all along.

The trick is timing. Catch one meaningful thing in a crowded sequence and the mind may briefly become worse at catching the next. The experimental name is the attentional blink, a term that makes the lapse sound like a tiny shutter closing. It is not a literal eye blink. It is a limitation in how we select, recognize, and report rapidly arriving information. Raymond, Shapiro and Arnell, 1992 tested that limitation by presenting targets in a rapid stream. Their result remains a useful reminder: clear visual input is not the same as successful conscious report.

A dozen years after the original finding, scientists began asking a more surprising question. Suppose the shutter is not fixed. Could prolonged training in how a person attends to experience alter the way two successive targets compete for recognition?

The answer found in a 2007 experiment was intriguing. It was also far more specific than the kind of claim now attached to commercial meditation audio. To understand the difference is to understand an entire fault line in the modern attention business: evidence that training changes performance is not evidence that an audio product produces the same effect.

I. Two numbers, one narrow window

In the laboratory, researchers use rapid serial visual presentation, often abbreviated RSVP. Letters or images appear at the same location, one after another. The participant is asked to identify or detect two designated targets, T1 and T2. When T2 follows T1 in a vulnerable interval, detection or reporting of T2 suffers. In the 1992 work, an especially poor detection interval began about 180 milliseconds after the first target and lasted roughly 270 milliseconds under the reported conditions. Those values describe that experiment—not a universal biological countdown.

Why should a number disappear from a report without disappearing from a screen? The honest answer is that attention is not one simple mechanism. Candidate explanations have included competition for a limited processing stage, how the first target is consolidated into memory, and the way distractions interrupt recognition. Later experiments modified the explanation rather than treating the first account as a final verdict. Shapiro, Raymond and Arnell, 1994 examined how the task definition affects the blink.

The important distinction is between the laboratory observation and the metaphor. A participant's failure to name T2 under controlled conditions does not mean the brain was literally switched off. Nor does it prove the existence of a reserve of hidden consciousness waiting to be unlocked by a sound file. It shows that the demands of one recognition task can impair performance on another presented close in time.

The phenomenon also matters because it exposes something ordinarily invisible about ourselves. We experience seeing as if the world arrives already assembled. Here, small changes in the order and spacing of visible items can change what we truthfully say we saw.

II. The retreat and the experiment

In 2007, Heleen Slagter and colleagues published Mental Training Affects Distribution of Limited Brain Resources in PLOS Biology. Their design followed 17 practitioners before and after a three-month intensive Vipassana retreat, during which they reportedly meditated 10–12 hours each day. A comparison group of 23 meditation-interested novices received a one-hour class and was asked to practice twenty minutes daily during a week before each testing session. This was a longitudinal comparison with sharply different training intensity, not a randomized test of a short commercial program. Read the original study and methods.

Both groups performed a rapid sequence task: letters flickered by, interrupted by two target numbers. The second number arrived either 336 milliseconds or 672 milliseconds after the first. The shorter gap fell inside the experimental blink interval; the longer gap provided a comparison. Participants did not meditate during the target-recognition task.

The researchers saw a difference in changes over time. All 17 practitioners improved second-target detection at the short interval, compared with 16 of the 23 novices. The authors found a larger reduction in the blink for the retreat group, while reporting that identification of the first target was not sacrificed. The report includes the actual group comparisons, statistical tests and variability; its result is better described as an association between prolonged intensive training and changes in a particular attention task than as a magical sharpening of all perception.

The brain recordings made the experiment especially memorable. Scalp electroencephalography measured an event-related component called P3b, which the researchers used as one indicator of allocation of processing resources to the first target. Among analyzed participants, reduced P3b response to T1 was associated with better T2 detection. The authors interpreted this as less elaborate investment in the first item leaving resources available for the second.

It is a compelling hypothesis about flexible allocation, not an EEG readout of a person's overall intelligence, spiritual attainment, or lifetime brain capacity. Only subsets of the participants had enough usable trials for certain detailed EEG analyses. The researchers themselves discuss possible overlap in responses to the first and second targets and how they tested for it. Details like these are not footnotes to be discarded by a sales page; they are the part of the science that keeps its claims in proportion.

III. The missing control is the product

The obvious temptation is to compress the story into a slogan: meditation makes your brain notice what others miss. That is already too broad.

The retreat participants gave huge amounts of time to a particular contemplative practice. They were not randomly assigned to a paid audio recording versus a matching control. They were not tested after one twelve-minute listening session. Their EEG findings do not establish that an isochronic tone, a binaural beat, an instrumental background or a recorded voice reproduces the retreat effect. And the attentional blink is a specific laboratory outcome—not direct proof of better grades, executive work, accident avoidance or a reliably quieter mind.

An essential methodological point is that the practitioner and novice groups had different histories, interests and exposures. Longitudinal before/after measurement is better than a one-time comparison, yet unequal training and self-selection leave room for alternative explanations. Even genuine improvement on the task does not by itself tell us how far benefits transfer to ordinary life.

A 2009 follow-up by Slagter and colleagues investigated theta phase synchrony in the same broad program of research. It explored an additional neural interpretation of reported T2 perception. It was not a separate trial showing that purchasable sound frequencies deliver the effect. Read the follow-up abstract.

In 2014, van Vugt and Slagter explored how the magnitude of attentional blink relates to a meditator's current state, another reminder that training history, state during observation and particular tasks can interact. These studies ask different questions; none is a validated Zen12 product trial. Study record.

IV. When the research gets bigger, the answer gets more complicated

A small experiment can change the question scientists ask. It cannot settle the entire field. Later evidence makes a more complicated picture.

In a 2021 review and meta-analysis, Millett and colleagues considered 29 studies of group-based mindfulness programs and executive functioning. They reported small favorable estimates in some domains after removing outliers, but no significant pooled effect for attention shifting; results varied with choices of controls and study features. Their overall conclusion was that evidence for improvements in executive functioning was not robust. Review abstract.

A 2024 meta-analysis by Zainal and Newman assembled 111 randomized controlled trials, totaling 9,538 participants. It reported small-to-moderate advantages in some measures of cognition and attention, including sustained attention, against several comparison types. It also reported outcomes that did not improve, including some measures of processing speed and cognitive errors, and differences according to sample and intervention characteristics. The authors studied mindfulness-based interventions that teach skills, not Zen12's recorded program. Review abstract.

These summaries are not simple opposites, one for meditation and one against it. They combine different trial populations, program types, statistical decisions and definitions of cognition. The tension is precisely what a responsible reader should encounter: promising findings in some circumstances; null findings, design caveats and disputed transfer in others.

There is no need to flatten that tension into either miracle marketing or a dismissal of all contemplative practice. Science advances by asking which practice, how long, under what conditions, compared with what, and measured by which outcome.

V. What would count as evidence for an audio product?

Imagine a reader arriving from a 40-second Final Vestige video. The reader has seen a second target go unnoticed, encountered the 2007 retreat, and now wants a convenient way to practice.

The commercial temptation is obvious: put a button next to the study and imply that the product makes the effect happen faster. But the mechanism connecting those two things has not been demonstrated.

Consider Zen12, marketed by Inspire3. The official Zen12 website describes a downloadable course with 12 levels, four listening versions per level, and 12-minute sessions. Its checkout page advertises 48 MP3 files as part of a larger bundle. Those are seller descriptions of format—useful facts for comparing the product with a person's schedule. The same seller says its audio can bring an hour's meditation benefit in twelve minutes and describes specific brainwave effects. We have not found independent product-specific trials that establish those outcomes. The 2007 retreat study did not compare Zen12 with silence, ordinary music, guided instruction, a placebo audio or another program. The 2024 meta-analysis did not test its specific bundle. It would be a category error to borrow the authority of those studies for a product claim.

An honest evaluation can still be useful. Readers might value short sessions, downloadable files, a choice between guidance and ambient sound, or a simple routine. Those are preferences to consider, not clinical outcomes to guarantee. A recorded program may function for some people as a reminder to sit down, while another reader may prefer a free timer and quiet room. Without an adequately controlled product trial, the better-performing choice for a particular person's attention remains unknown.

Our editorial rule is simple: teach the science in the investigation, evaluate the product in a separately marked guide, and disclose any financial connection directly beside the eventual earning link. A citation next to a product does not silently upgrade a seller claim into an independent finding.

VI. A five-minute practice that asks for nothing

You can explore one aspect of attention without an account, a purchase or a score.

Choose a harmless, quiet setting. For the first minute, notice a comfortable sensory anchor: the feeling of your feet against the floor, a nearby sound, or the ordinary movement of breathing. There is no obligation to control or deepen your breath.

During the second minute, notice when you begin telling yourself a story about a distraction—I am bad at this; I should have focused longer. You do not have to win an argument with that thought. Notice it, then decide where to place attention next.

For the third minute, broaden the field: what is happening at the edges of awareness? A distant sound, a change of light, the pressure of clothing. Let an observation be an observation rather than a task to perfect.

During the fourth minute, return to the first anchor. Ask whether you can notice a distraction without chasing it. During the fifth, stop trying to notice anything in particular and write down one sentence: What did I observe about the movement of my attention?

This is a nonclinical reflection exercise, not a reproduction of the 2007 retreat, not an attentional-blink test, and not proof of treatment or enhanced cognition. If inward attention is uncomfortable, stop. The point is to acquire language for an ordinary experience, not to manufacture a result.

VII. Why the old archive belongs here

Our published Puységur Animal Magnetism Files trace how eighteenth-century witnesses, predictions, failures and claims of extraordinary states were gathered into a case for animal magnetism. The documents matter not because their strongest conclusions have been vindicated, but because they show the history of people attempting to turn subjective experience into evidence.

Modern attention research uses different instruments, control conditions and standards, yet it faces a related interpretive discipline: an experience may be real without every explanation offered for it being true. A subject who cannot report the second number has had a legitimate failure of report. That does not tell us the soul left the body, that cognition can be accelerated by a commercial soundtrack, or that a particular self-help method is proved.

The strongest line from Puységur's files into the modern laboratory is not a claim that eighteenth-century magnetism predicted neuroscience. It is a question about proof: who observed what, under which circumstances, and what could actually follow?

That question should govern both our articles and our recommendations.

VIII. The last second

There is a final indignity in the attentional blink. Once you know about it, you may become confident you will catch the next target. Sometimes you will. Sometimes you will not. Awareness of a limitation is not the same as exemption from it.

That is also how to approach a meditation offer. It is reasonable to prefer a twelve-minute recording over a silent room, and reasonable to reject the recording in favor of a free habit. It is not reasonable to take a laboratory study of three months of intense retreat practice and promise an equivalent result from pressing play.

The second number was present. The research offers one account of why it slipped through—and evidence that attention may, under demanding training conditions, be more flexible than it first appears. The rest is the work of careful distinctions.
