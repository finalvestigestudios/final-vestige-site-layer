# The Gorilla in the Scan: When Experts Miss the Unexpected

*Why a trained eye can find a tiny abnormality and miss an enormous intruder—and why that is not proof that a brain-training product works.*

Imagine that your job is to search a series of medical images for small signs of disease. The images arrive in slices, one after another. You have been trained to distinguish what matters from the immense amount of background detail. You know what a lung nodule might look like, and you have spent years getting better at finding one. Now imagine that someone hides a gorilla in the scan.

Not an actual animal. A deliberately inserted silhouette. In a laboratory study, the figure was much larger than the abnormalities the observers were asked to identify.

You might expect that an expert would notice it immediately. That expectation is precisely what makes the evidence interesting.

In 2013, Trafton Drew, Melissa L.-H. Võ and Jeremy M. Wolfe reported an experiment involving **24 radiologists** who were asked to search for lung nodules in CT images. An unexpected gorilla image appeared in the final case. **Twenty of the 24 radiologists—about 83 percent—did not report seeing it.** The finding made a memorable headline. The explanation is more careful, and more useful, than the headline. [1]

It does not mean radiologists are unobservant. It does not show that they fail to do their jobs. It does not establish that unexpected abnormalities are routinely missed in ordinary medical practice. It shows that, under one particular experimental setup, highly trained observers could perform their assigned search while failing to report an unusual item that was irrelevant to the target category.

What you are looking for helps determine what you notice.

## The assignment that narrowed the world

Most people experience sight as a continuous inventory of the room around them. A desk, a face, a doorway, a person entering the frame: they seem simply *there*. The harder question is what portion of that scene becomes consciously reportable when attention is already committed to something else.

The participants in Drew and colleagues' study were not instructed to find everything unusual. They were instructed to search for lung nodules. They viewed five sets of computed-tomography images. On the final set, the researchers inserted the unexpected animal figure. The gorilla was reported as **48 times the size of the average lung nodule** used in the experiment. [1]

This was not a real clinical examination, and the gorilla was not a clinically meaningful abnormality. It was a probe: could a striking but irrelevant object enter awareness while experts did the precise task their expertise had trained them to perform?

The response pattern was surprising, but it needs the denominator. **Four of 24 radiologists noticed it; 20 did not.** A second experiment with non-experts also produced a high miss rate, but the 2024 review of inattentional blindness in medicine warns that the tiny number who noticed the gorilla across groups makes it difficult to infer a reliable *difference caused by expertise*. [2]

“Experts missed it” is supported for those 24 observers in that study. “Expertise makes you blind,” or “novices see more,” is not.

That distinction matters. A careful investigation preserves the result without inventing a lesson that the comparison cannot bear.

## Looking at a region is not the same as registering its meaning

The study used eye tracking. More than half of the radiologists who missed the gorilla had their gaze directed to the relevant region. [1,2] Readers sometimes turn that observation into a slogan: *they looked straight at it and saw nothing*.

Eye-position measurements are not recordings of conscious experience. A fixation near an object does not prove what the observer perceived, understood or retained. Nor does a participant's later report provide a perfectly transparent window into the fleeting contents of awareness. The strongest defensible description is narrower: some participants' gaze passed through the unexpected figure's location, yet their subsequent reports did not identify it.

This is a particularly good example of why Final Vestige distinguishes **a recorded observation**, **an interpretation of that observation**, and **a sweeping claim made after the fact**.

The observed behavior: a high proportion failed to report the figure while searching for nodules.

The interpretation: a highly constrained visual task can sharply reduce noticing of unexpected events.

The unsupported leap: expert humans are bad at looking, or a particular intervention would correct the effect.

A reader who keeps those categories separate has learned more than the person who only remembers the gorilla.

## Before there was a scan, there was a basketball

The 2013 experiment was a deliberate echo of an earlier demonstration.

Daniel Simons and Christopher Chabris published **“Gorillas in Our Midst”** in 1999. Their work used dynamic scenes in which participants monitored a specific activity while an unexpected object passed through the display. The probability of noticing it changed with the observer's assigned task and the object's relationship to the attended elements. [3]

The demonstration became famous because the unexpected thing seemed so obvious once the viewer knew to look for it. Yet that hindsight is part of the trap. The second time you watch, you do not have the same task. You already expect the unexpected object.

A familiar clip can no longer serve as a fresh, blind demonstration for someone who knows its twist. Replaying the video also cannot tell you whether you are “better at attention” than your friends. Different instructions, expectations, screens and exposures change the task.

The lesson is not that every eye is broken. It is that perception is organized around selection. When attention is directed toward one class of information, another may fail to become a reported event.

And that sets up an important distinction with another Final Vestige topic: **change blindness**.

In change-blindness experiments, an object or person changes across a disruption, and the observer may fail to compare the before and after correctly. In inattentional-blindness experiments, the unexpected item is present, but it is not the task target and may go unreported. They are related problems of selection and awareness, not interchangeable tricks. The 1998 pedestrian-replacement experiment examined the former; the original gorilla tasks and the CT study examined the latter. [4]

Our existing [change-blindness investigation](https://finalvestige.com/2026/11/02/change-blindness/) provides the companion question: what if the world *changes* while attention is directed elsewhere?

## The review that complicates the headline

A 2024 review, **“Inattentional blindness in medicine,”** examined research in clinical and medical-image contexts, including five studies concerned specifically with radiology. It discusses the gorilla experiment as a memorable entry point, but it does not treat one small laboratory result as an estimate of real-world diagnostic error. [2]

The review's caveats matter: experimental tasks differ from clinical workflows; the unexpected objects range from absurd images to clinically plausible findings; and the criteria for counting someone as having noticed something can vary. In the original radiology study, according to the review, the exact coding criteria for noticing were not specified in sufficient detail. [2]

This is the ordinary work of scientific interpretation. A provocative finding deserves attention; a generalization requires comparison, replication, and precise definitions.

One clinical question might be whether an incidental finding falls outside the examiner's search target. A very different question is how often real patients are harmed by such misses. The gorilla experiment alone does not answer the second. Nor does it establish which workflow, checklist or training approach would prevent a specific miss.

When an article moves from a laboratory image to a claim about medical safety, it crosses an evidentiary boundary. That boundary must be marked rather than glossed over.

## What the observer thinks they would have seen

Ask someone to predict whether they would notice a large gorilla in a scan. They may be confident. But prediction takes place in a different mental state from the original search. The predictor knows there is a gorilla. The participant did not.

This difference helps explain why these demonstrations circulate so well on social platforms. The reveal is vivid and economical. An audience can understand it in seconds. Yet a useful social teaser should not misrepresent the experimental conditions or imply that a single viewer's response diagnoses their attention.

For a Final Vestige short, the ethical hook is not “your brain is secretly failing.” It is: **“The researchers inserted a gorilla 48 times the size of a nodule. Twenty of twenty-four specialists didn't report it. Here's the question the study did—and didn't—answer.”**

Those last words are an invitation to the source, not a promise of a personal transformation.

A small demonstration can make this difference tangible without reproducing the study's copyrighted scans or turning a psychological effect into a game of humiliation.

### A no-flash observation exercise

Take a familiar paragraph from a book you have permission to use. Read it once while counting how many sentences contain a number. Then read it again, this time looking for changes in punctuation or italicized words. Notice what information becomes obvious when the assignment changes.

The exercise is **an illustration, not an experiment measuring inattentional blindness**. There is no control condition, random assignment, standardized exposure or validated outcome. You cannot use it to rank the attention of two people—or to infer that a meditation product made you more observant.

It simply helps readers feel the difference between being shown information and being asked to select it.

A more rigorous reader can make a two-column evidence note: what was actually observed in the study, and what a promotional retelling claims about it. If those columns differ, investigate the distance.

## The much older problem of witnesses and suggestion

Long before eye trackers and CT scanners, investigators confronted versions of the same interpretive difficulty: observers could earnestly report an experience without establishing its cause.

The [Puységur Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) examine the early history of magnetic somnambulism through historical source rows, case dossiers, claims of cure and problems of witness evidence and consent. The archive treats those accounts as evidence of *what was reported*, not automatic proof that the reported causal theory was correct.

There is no demonstrated scientific causal line between Puységur's eighteenth-century practices and a twenty-first-century CT-scanning experiment. The bridge is methodological. In both cases, the honest investigator asks how a report was produced, what expectations were present, and what evidence is missing.

It is tempting to compress both into a single modern story about “the subconscious mind.” That compression replaces history with a slogan.

The radiology gorilla study does not show that mesmerism worked. Puységur's records do not predict modern inattentional blindness. They demonstrate why an account of human experience should not be mistaken for a full theory of the mechanism that produced it.

## Can a meditation recording make the gorilla easier to notice?

This is where a scientific investigation can be misused by marketing.

Researchers have studied attention and meditation under many designs. Findings from sustained training or tightly controlled tasks may raise worthwhile questions about cognition. But an intervention cannot inherit every favorable result merely because it uses the words “attention,” “mindfulness,” or “brainwaves.”

To establish that a particular recording reduces inattentional blindness in a meaningfully repeatable way, investigators would need to specify the exact product, exposure, suitable control, participants, target outcomes, blinding where feasible, and analysis plan; then test and replicate the claim. None of the gorilla studies cited above tested Zen12, Hypnosis Live or Brainwave Shots.

Zen12's seller advertises a structured twelve-level, 48-MP3 program and claims that twelve minutes can substitute for an hour of meditation. Those are **seller claims** from its product page—not outcomes demonstrated by the gorilla study and not product-specific benefits established here. [5,6]

Final Vestige does not recommend buying any audio tool to “cure” inattentional blindness or to improve diagnostic skill. We have no basis for that promise.

The more useful question for a reader interested in focus is simpler: what do I actually want to do in the next twelve minutes? A free timer and one small, specific task provide a starting point without borrowing authority from research that did not test them.

## The finding worth keeping

A specialist searched for something small. An unexpected, very large object appeared. Many did not report it.

It is tempting to end with the punchline. The better ending is a question: **what did the assignment make relevant, and what did it make easy to miss?**

We can train skills, design search procedures, and learn to verify claims. We should not confuse any one experiment with proof that a new intervention works, or treat experts' errors as entertainment rather than data.

The lesson of the gorilla is not to distrust every human eye. It is to distrust conclusions that outrun the evidence presented to support them.
