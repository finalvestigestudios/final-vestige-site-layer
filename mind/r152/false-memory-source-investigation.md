## The missing word

Imagine hearing a careful succession of words: bed, rest, awake, tired, dream, wake, snooze. Now set the list aside. A moment later, a friend asks whether the word *sleep* appeared. It seems as though it must have. Each earlier word pointed toward it. You may even have a distinct feeling of having heard it.

In this example, *sleep* was never spoken. The scene is an **original illustration**, not a verbatim experiment or a report about a real participant. Its central trick is not visual. It happens where language meets expectation: the topic of a list can become easier to remember than its precise contents.

The phenomenon is a useful antidote to a seductive idea: that a memory's vividness proves its accuracy. A recollection can be clear, familiar, and sincere without being a faithful transcript. But the evidence also deserves its own warning label. Scientists can create controlled illusions using short lists of associated words; that does **not** give a commentator license to dismiss the complex memories of actual people.

## The experiment that made an absent word memorable

In 1995, Henry L. Roediger III and Kathleen B. McDermott reported two experiments building on James Deese's earlier work. Volunteers heard lists in which each item was associated with an unspoken “critical” word. Participants later sometimes remembered that central word as if it had been presented. The paper reported false recall of unpresented associates in **40% of opportunities in its first experiment**, and **55% in its second** [1]. Those are results of the particular procedures, not population-wide percentages of human memory failure.

The remarkable detail was not only that an absent item could be supplied. Later recognition judgments sometimes came with confidence. There was a subjective quality of remembering, even though the test record could establish that the item had not been in the list.

Why does this happen? The experiment did not prove one simple mechanism. Related words may activate a shared concept; people may retain a gist rather than each original item; the sense of familiarity can be misattributed to an actual encounter. Researchers have debated their relative contributions. The most defensible conclusion is narrower and more interesting: **the mind's success at finding meaning can sometimes produce an error about the source of that meaning**.

A laboratory list provides something ordinary life rarely supplies: a complete independent record of what was presented. The researchers knew the “critical” word had not been played. Outside the laboratory, an investigator often possesses neither a complete recording of the event nor an uncontaminated initial account. In that setting, confident assertion and instant dismissal are equally poor substitutes for evidence.

## A pattern, not a magic spell

In 1997, Kerry J. Robinson and Roediger investigated what happened when researchers changed the number of related words and the strength of their associations. Their experiments linked the frequency of false recall and recognition to the quantity of relevant associates, rather than simply the existence of one evocative phrase [2]. That matters because memory illusions are not arbitrary switches that a hypnotist or advertiser can flip at will.

The wider literature also resists a neat, one-way conclusion. Freyd and Gleaves, writing in 1996, challenged the leap from false recall in short verbal lists to contested accounts of childhood abuse [3]. Those are different kinds of material, collected under different conditions and with different consequences. The criticism is not a denial that memory errors occur. It is a demand that the scope of an experiment be taken seriously.

In 2005, Whittlesea, Masson and Hughes examined false recognition with rapidly presented lists and the role of surprise [4]. Their work speaks to how a judgment can emerge under particular presentation conditions. It does not identify a method for predicting the reliability of any single person's autobiographical memory.

If an experiment shows that two photographs can be confused, it would be reckless to announce that photographs do not exist. If it shows that a word can be falsely recalled, it would be equally reckless to declare that all remembered experiences are invented. Reliable interpretation begins with the boundaries of the instrument.

## Suggestion enters the room

Why does this matter to Final Vestige's historical investigations? Because the history of hypnosis often treated unusual inner experiences as a revelation about hidden realities. Armand-Marie-Jacques de Chastenet, Marquis de Puységur, became associated with a form of magnetic somnambulism in eighteenth-century France. His descriptions and later accounts are historical documents: they show what observers said and believed, not that animal magnetism had the supernatural or physiological properties assigned to it.

The site's existing [Puységur Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) provide the appropriate archive route. Historical witness testimony deserves respectful scrutiny. A dramatic narrative is a fact about a source; the narrative's truth still requires corroboration.

Modern hypnosis research gives us a reason to keep the distinction. In a 2002 experiment, Scoboria, Mazzoni, Kirsch and Milling exposed students to a story, then posed misleading or neutral questions, in or out of hypnosis. Both misleading questions and hypnosis increased memory-report errors in that procedure, with the misleading questions generating more errors; their effects were additive [5]. This is an experimentally specific result. It does not show hypnosis always produces false memories or that every suggestive question does so.

A separate study by Neuschatz, Lynn, Benoit and Fite used a related false-memory paradigm to compare hypnotized and nonhypnotized participants. False recognition was high in both groups; the researchers did **not** find that hypnosis improved recognition accuracy, but it also did not cause extra impairment beyond the nonhypnotic condition in their comparison [6]. That is an important counterweight to sensational claims on either side.

We therefore cannot responsibly infer from an intense remembered image under hypnosis that the image depicts an event. Nor can we infer that the person's subjective experience was insincere.

## Confidence is not a chain of custody

There is a practical parallel between an investigator handling archival evidence and a person recalling an event. An archivist asks when a document first appeared, who copied it, what changed between versions, and whether there are independent records. A person can ask a similar set of questions of a memory—without demanding impossible certainty.

What did I witness directly? Which details came from someone else's telling? Was the question itself suggestive? Did I hear the same version repeatedly before I began to remember it in that form? What independent records survive? And what remains unresolved?

These questions are not weapons for interrogating another person's trauma. They are safeguards against converting certainty into evidence. A reader seeking help with distressing or traumatic memories should speak with an appropriately qualified clinician rather than try to “recover” or test those memories with commercially sold audio.

The temptation to turn a demonstration into a personal diagnostic tool is especially strong online. A viral clip has twenty seconds to astonish. A real memory claim may need months of contextual work—or may remain uncertain forever.

## A nonclinical demonstration, with a correction step

Try a brief exercise using your own ordinary, low-stakes information. Choose a mundane paragraph from a printed article you can revisit. Read it once, close it, and write down five specific claims you believe it made. Put a question mark next to anything that feels familiar but may be an inference. Reopen the article. Underline exactly which phrases support each remembered claim; mark which parts are your own paraphrases or deductions.

This is an original **reading-accuracy reflection**, not a validated intervention or an attempt to induce false memories. Do not use it to assess someone else's credibility. Its value lies in retaining the source so you can compare memory with text rather than guessing at the accuracy of your confidence.

A second version makes the distinction even clearer. Record one sentence about what you directly saw, then a separate sentence about what you inferred. The act of labeling does not guarantee accurate memory. It does give the later reader a better evidence trail.

The first lesson is not “never trust yourself.” It is “keep the source.” A stable record helps us treat recollection as testimony that can be examined, rather than a courtroom verdict waiting to happen.

## Keep the witness, keep the record

An absent word remembered as present sounds like a small laboratory trick. It reaches a much larger truth about research: humans are good at organizing experience, and organization changes what feels familiar. That ability makes storytelling, scholarship and everyday interpretation possible. It can also confuse what we inferred with what we saw.

The answer is not to stop remembering; it is to pair memory with source trails. In historical research, in the laboratory, and in commerce, an assertion should never become proof merely because it arrives with confidence.
