# The Face That Changed When You Turned It Right Side Up

*For a moment the portrait looks merely odd. Turn it upright, and the expression becomes almost impossible to ignore. What changed was not the picture. It was the way you were looking at it.*

Imagine being handed two copies of the same portrait. In the first, the eyes and mouth have been turned upside down inside an otherwise normal face. In the second, nothing has been altered. You are told you will see the portraits upside down and then right side up. You expect the difference to be equally obvious in both positions. After all, the altered eyes and mouth are the same pixels either way.

But for many observers, they are not equally obvious. With the whole head inverted, the changes can be surprisingly difficult to notice. Turn the picture upright and the altered expression leaps into view. The eyes and mouth that seemed merely unusual suddenly appear disconcertingly wrong.

This is the **Thatcher illusion**, named for the photograph used in Peter Thompson's short 1980 paper, *Margaret Thatcher: A New Illusion* [1]. It is among the most memorable experiments in the psychology of seeing faces because it exposes something that everyday confidence conceals: seeing is not a simple inventory of features. The visual system also interprets the orientation of what surrounds those features.

Yet the popular explanation—"our brains see whole faces rather than parts"—is too tidy. Three decades after the original image, researchers found that even isolated eyes and mouths can display the relevant orientation sensitivity [2]. The better story is stranger. The effect may depend not only on seeing a face as a whole, but on how familiar feature shapes are coded when they face their expected way.

## The original two-page provocation

Thompson published his paper in *Perception* in August 1980, pages 483–484 [1]. The original image used a portrait of Margaret Thatcher. It was an arresting demonstration rather than a trial of a meditation treatment or a claim about personality. A face was altered by locally inverting prominent features; the complete image was then viewed upright and inverted.

The result is often taught as a single rule: upright faces are understood holistically, while inverted faces are processed piece by piece. Inversion does impair many aspects of face processing. But a classroom slogan is not the same as a complete mechanism. It is also misleading to conclude that the brain stops processing upside-down faces, or that the illusion happens identically for every viewer.

The essential manipulation must be kept straight. *Thatcherization* is not simply rotating a photograph. A researcher turns the **eyes and mouth inside a face** while leaving the rest of the image intact. Then the researcher changes the **orientation of the whole face**. There are therefore two different operations—local feature inversion and global image inversion—and the effect depends on their interaction.

Without this distinction, a social video can accidentally turn a psychological experiment into a parlor trick. A viewer may believe that the unsettling face is created by a sudden digital substitution when, in fact, the point is that the same changed face is present in both orientations.

## A method you can understand without copying the famous photograph

Here is a conceptual demonstration that does not require using Thompson's copyrighted portrait or anyone's real face. Draw a simple upright face with two eyes and a smiling mouth. Next, make a second copy, but invert each eye shape and the mouth inside the outline. Rotate **both** drawings by 180 degrees. Compare the normal and altered versions while rotated, then turn both upright again.

The drawing is **only a schematic illustration**. It is not a validated perceptual stimulus, and a hand-drawn icon may fail to generate the striking effect of a photographed face. A scientifically useful comparison would need carefully controlled facial images, consistent lighting and size, and a way to record observers' judgments without telling them in advance what to look for.

Even if the sketch produces no illusion, it can teach the central experimental logic: keep the local changes constant while varying orientation. To ask whether viewing position changes perception, do not quietly change the face at the same time.

A second free exercise needs no face at all. Write two columns headed **what the image actually contains** and **what I initially noticed**. When you encounter a legitimate research demonstration, compare those columns. They will not tell you whether you have a disorder or how suggestible you are. They will reveal how observation reports can differ from visible details.

## Why turning a face matters

Face recognition is not interchangeable with recognizing random objects. We spend years examining eyes, mouths and expressions, often under conditions where the head is upright or near upright. The Thatcher effect exaggerates an orientation dependence that normally helps us notice expressions.

In 2014, Lilia Psalta, Andrew Young, Peter Thompson and Timothy Andrews reported a series of perceptual matching experiments examining whether the illusion truly requires the entire facial configuration [2]. They found that participants could distinguish normal from Thatcherized versions when faces were upright but had much more difficulty with the same comparison when the images were inverted.

More revealingly, the orientation effect was present **when only an eye region or only a mouth region was visible**. A whole face was not always required. This matters because it challenges the simple account that the Thatcher illusion is exclusively a consequence of disrupted holistic processing—the special perception of relations among all facial features.

The authors argued that orientation-specific coding of the expressive features themselves is an important part of the phenomenon. That is a narrower claim than saying all facial perception is local, or that holistic processing is irrelevant to every face task.

The distinction is worth protecting. Experimental psychology advances when later work shows that a beloved explanation was broader than the evidence. The spectacular demonstration survives; the explanation gets sharper.

## The brain-imaging result, and what it cannot say

A related paper by Psalta and colleagues, published online in November 2013 and appearing in *Psychological Science* in 2014, used functional magnetic resonance imaging [3]. The authors examined adaptation in the superior temporal sulcus, a region associated with processing changeable facial information, including aspects of expression.

When upright images changed from normal to Thatcherized configurations, the researchers observed a release from adaptation in this region. The corresponding effect was not evident under the inverted-face condition. This adds neural evidence that orientation matters for processing the altered facial expression.

But brain imaging is not mind reading. It does not tell us whether a particular viewer enjoyed the experience, whether meditation would remove the effect, or whether a paid audio program could change the result. It identifies a task-related pattern under measured experimental conditions.

Nor does one neural correlate decide the whole theoretical debate. Face perception involves distributed processes and different demands for recognizing identities, expressions, and isolated features. The neural and perceptual experiments belong together because they constrain a mechanism; they do not turn a striking image into a universal law.

## The surprising divergence: expression versus grotesqueness

There is a second complication. In a 2014 *Perception* article, Psalta and Andrews investigated the recognition of facial expressions in Thatcherized images [4]. Inversion of normal faces produced only a relatively small reduction in expression recognition in their tests. Locally inverting eyes and mouths in upright faces produced a much larger reduction, and rotating a Thatcherized face upside down could **relatively improve** expression recognition.

That is not a contradiction. "Does this look grotesque?" and "Can you identify the depicted expression?" are different judgments. They engage overlapping but not identical representations. When an altered face loses its grotesque quality while inverted, a viewer might still discriminate some expression information.

This is one reason an online demonstration should not ask one ambiguous question like "Did the brain fail?" It should identify what was measured: discrimination of the altered face, perceived grotesqueness, or recognition of emotion. Results for one cannot automatically be copied to the others.

The older literature already cautioned against treating inversion as an all-or-nothing effect. Research on inverted faces reaches back before 1980 [5], and work explicitly examining configuration and inversion followed in the 1990s [6]. Thompson's famous picture belongs inside that longer line of scientific argument, not outside it as a standalone miracle.

## What a short social clip can—and cannot—prove

A short video is exceptionally good at showing the before-and-after experience. It is much worse at preserving all the qualifications. The temptation is to caption a face rotation: "Your mind was tricked; you can't trust anything you see." That conclusion is far too large.

The controlled claim is that the detectability or perception of a specific alteration in facial features depends strongly on orientation under particular conditions. It does **not** follow that eyewitnesses are always unreliable, that everyone has the same response, or that the experiment measures intelligence, honesty, attention span, trauma, meditation skill or hypnotic responsiveness.

An ethical creator could instead ask: **"Why do the same altered eyes seem normal upside down and wrong upright?"** Then pause long enough for viewers to compare the frames, identify that the eye and mouth edits remain constant, and link to sources that explain the competing theories. That is a stronger hook because the surprise survives scrutiny.

An original creator demonstration should use a self-produced or properly licensed face image with the person's informed permission. The transformation needs to be disclosed accurately. Don't reproduce Thompson's original Margaret Thatcher photograph, a research figure, or another creator's altered portrait without examining rights. If a synthetic face is used, label it as synthetic. Final Vestige's own CSS schematic on the companion page is *not* a scientific reproduction.

## What does this have to do with suggestion?

There is a meaningful historical bridge between perception and suggestion, but not the one that commercial copy often proposes.

Final Vestige's [Puységur Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) investigates how eighteenth-century observers described striking experiences and what those descriptions can establish about their historical setting. In the modern Thatcher experiments, researchers similarly distinguish a participant's experience from the mechanism inferred to produce it. A report can be sincerely felt without supporting every explanation offered for that report.

That methodological link is valuable. But a face-orientation experiment does not test hypnosis, self-hypnosis, brainwave entrainment, meditation, or product-specific audio effects. An offer cannot honestly use the Thatcher illusion as proof that it can "reprogram your perception," overcome cognitive biases, or induce a better brain state.

Readers exploring modern attention methods can use the existing [Seven Day Focus Fieldbook](https://finalvestige.com/focus/), which supplies a free planning routine and questions for evaluating audio claims. It is a different intervention, not a cure for the Thatcher illusion and not part of this experiment.

## The trap of a beautiful explanation

One thing makes the Thatcher illusion unusually useful for an evidence-led archive: it invites overconfident explanations just as readily as it invites overconfident seeing.

The eye catches a grotesque expression. The mind supplies an interpretation. A lecturer says, "You see faces holistically." A short video repeats it. Soon a vivid demonstration becomes a one-sentence theory. Yet experiments using the eyes or mouth alone tell us that even the correct-sounding theory may need revision.

A scientific claim earns confidence through controls, replication, compatible methods and openness to rival accounts. The most interesting part of the demonstration is no longer simply the surprise when the portrait turns upright. It is how a two-page paper in 1980 generated questions precise enough for later perceptual and brain-imaging experiments to test.

The face did not magically change its features. Our recognition of those features changed with orientation. And our explanation of why has changed as researchers looked more closely.

That is the story worth carrying away from the picture: **not that the mind can be sold a shortcut, but that what looks obvious can be worth investigating twice.**
