The Second Number Fell Between Frames

In 1992 researchers showed that recognizing one thing could make the next thing surprisingly hard to report. The eye never closed. The second target still arrived. So where did it go?

Editorial status: original, sourced investigation draft · 1 November 2026 · Not yet published

Imagine a narrow screen in a dark laboratory. Characters replace one another at an almost absurd speed: letter, letter, number, letter, letter, number, letter. Your assignment is plain. Watch the whole sequence; afterward, report the numbers. You are not asked to decipher a crowded image or locate a hidden object in a room. Everything appears in the same place. And yet something remarkable happens. The first number comes through. The second, although presented to the same eyes, is often absent from the answer.

The disappearance has a peculiar timing. Move the second number farther from the first and people report it more reliably. Put it almost immediately after the first and performance can recover, a counterintuitive exception known as lag-1 sparing. Place it a few positions later, and the report falters again. It is tempting to call this a little blackout of consciousness. But that phrase is both memorable and treacherous: it pictures a shutter where researchers were measuring behavior.

This is the attentional blink. It has become a favorite of popular psychology, sometimes with the implication that the mind literally switches off for half a second, or that one extraordinary method can teach you to see everything. Neither implication is what the experimental record establishes.

1992: a failure arranged in milliseconds

Source-design note: The opening scene's two digits are a deliberately simplified illustration—not a reproduction of the 1992 stimuli. Raymond and colleagues asked participants to identify a partially specified letter target and detect a later fully specified letter probe. The distinction changes precisely what the original experiment measured. Their report described weak probe detection during a 270-millisecond interval beginning about 180 milliseconds after the first target; the exact timing is not a universal property of every RSVP task.

Jane Raymond, Kimron Shapiro and Karen Arnell reported the original phenomenon in Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance in 1992. Their rapid serial visual presentation procedure—RSVP to researchers—made timing unusually tractable. Items appeared sequentially in one location. A first target claimed attention; a second target, inserted soon afterward, was less likely to be identified. A carefully designed task could thus expose a limitation invisible in ordinary perception. [1]

A crucial detail is what was actually scored. The question was not whether light from target two reached the eye. Nor was it whether a participant's brain had become electrically inactive. The outcome was a report: could the participant name or identify the second target under the instructions given? In experiments, “did not see it” often abbreviates “did not report it correctly.” Those descriptions overlap in ordinary conversation but are not logically interchangeable.

Later summaries often place the vulnerable interval at roughly 200–500 milliseconds after the first target, with the exact pattern varying across display rate, masks, targets, and tasks. Shapiro, Raymond and Arnell's 1997 overview describes two masked targets arriving within about half a second; attention to the first can reduce accurate report of the second, whereas ignoring the first or spacing them farther apart changes the result. [2]

The interval is short enough to be dismissed as a technicality. It is also long enough to have consequences. Half a second is enough for a scene to change, an object to pass, or a second word to arrive. But a laboratory deficit is not a diagnosis of everyday blindness: real-world scenes supply motion, repetition, peripheral cues, priorities, and opportunities to look again. A controlled stimulus stream buys precision by sacrificing much of the world's complexity.

The first target is not just an obstacle

Why should noticing one object interfere with reporting the next? Early interpretations emphasized a limited processing resource: the system commits to the first target, leaving too little capacity for the second. Others proposed that selection, consolidation into memory, or temporary control settings could explain the findings. The differences matter because “the brain is overloaded” sounds like a complete explanation even when it is only a metaphor.

In 1994, Shapiro, Raymond and Arnell reported seven RSVP experiments that tested how the nature of the task mattered. Their abstract describes a blink when the first target was an object, but not when it was defined merely by a temporal interval; difficulty estimates did not simply track the blink's appearance. The authors offered a late-selection account rather than the earlier early-selection interpretation. This is not a minor footnote. It warns us that the deficit depends on what participants are asked to do with the stream, not merely how many photons hit the retina. [3]

In a 1997 experiment, researchers also found evidence of priming from a second target that was not successfully reported. The paper's title makes the distinction: visual “types” could have been processed even when the exact “token”—the specific occurrence in the stream—failed to reach reportable awareness. Calling the period a universal sensory shutdown would overlook that distinction. [4]

The evidence boundary

An attentional blink measures a reduction in second-target report under specific conditions. It does not prove that every subsequent event was unprocessed, that consciousness turned off, or that an observer can never improve at the task.

The most awkward exception: a second target can arrive sooner and survive

If the brain were merely occupied for a fixed half-second, the earliest second targets ought to fare worst. Often they do not. When the second target immediately follows the first, performance can be unexpectedly strong. Experimentalists call it lag-1 sparing. The exception has been especially important in separating a simple refractory-period account from more nuanced accounts of selection and temporal grouping.

Hommel and Akyürek investigated the costs as well as the benefits of this apparent sparing in 2005. When identity and temporal order both mattered, performance on the immediate second target could look good even as other aspects of reporting suffered. Two events may be integrated rather than independently stamped and filed. The mind's success at preserving information can coexist with uncertainty about which event came first. [5]

That is a particularly useful challenge to the popular picture of a fixed blindness interval. The mechanism is more like a fast sorting operation with compromises than a camera that momentarily loses power. A good theory must explain not only why reports fail, but also why some arrangements escape the failure.

Sometimes the error is in the timestamp

Edward Vul, Mark Nieuwenstein and Nancy Kanwisher approached the phenomenon through temporal-selection errors. Their 2008 experiments examined distractor intrusions in a rapidly presented stream. Their conclusion was not that selection has one kind of failure. During the blink, temporal selection could be suppressed, delayed, and spread out across time—and these components returned toward baseline on different schedules. [6]

Picture a clerk receiving a stack of envelopes stamped milliseconds apart. The clerk still reads some writing, but the wrong label sometimes lands on the wrong envelope, the filing is late, and the boundary between consecutive items blurs. That picture is only an analogy, yet it gets us closer to the paper than the claim that the eyes “go blind.” The experiment asked how accurately information could be selected from a sequence; it did not photograph the inside of subjective experience.

Christian Olivers reviewed this wider tension in 2007. Evidence that attention is briefly reduced after important events sits beside evidence that those events can also enhance subsequent perception. The attentional blink may reflect useful selection processes operating under unusual demands, rather than a single rigid deficiency. [7]

Can training change the blink?

Here the story becomes attractive to meditation marketers, and the responsible answer becomes more demanding. There is a peer-reviewed experiment connecting intensive meditation training to this task. In 2007, Heleen Slagter and colleagues examined participants before and after three months of intensive Vipassana meditation and compared them with a less intensively trained comparison group. The researchers used an RSVP task and scalp-recorded electrical responses. After training, the practitioners showed a smaller attentional blink and reduced P3b responses to the first target; within the study, greater reductions in the latter tended to accompany greater improvement in second-target detection. [8]

That is interesting evidence about the trainability of attention under the studied conditions. It is not a reason to claim that any twelve-minute audio file will reproduce the result. The intervention in the study was three months of intensive practice, involving specific instruction, people and circumstances. A branded sound recording is not the same intervention. A change in a laboratory task does not automatically translate to better mental health, perfect daily focus or safer driving. And a measured group effect does not guarantee any individual's outcome.

There are further limitations. Participants knew whether they were attending an intensive training program. A relatively specialized group and task constrain generalization. EEG components such as P3b are informative measurements, not simple gauges of inner calm. Interpretation should respect what the study compared, how participants were selected, and which outcomes were recorded.

A useful distinction is that people can investigate and practice attention without buying anything. The work of a rigorous historical and scientific archive is to keep the claim tethered to the actual procedure, even when the claim becomes less glamorous.

The old temptation: testimony mistaken for mechanism

Final Vestige's Puységur's Animal Magnetism Files follows eighteenth-century case reports in which statements by patients, witnesses, operators and observers were assembled into a persuasive argument about animal magnetism. The surviving record contains observations and failures, but testimony alone does not establish the mechanism those observers proposed. The archive's source register deliberately preserves failed predictions and ambiguous outcomes rather than smoothing them away.

The comparison here is about method, not a theory of hypnosis. A participant who misses a digit and an eighteenth-century patient reporting an extraordinary sensation are describing vastly different situations. Neither record grants the investigator a license to retrofit a preferred explanation. In both, the questions are: What was actually observed? What was controlled? Who knew the expected outcome? What would count against the proposed cause?

The answer is seldom as satisfying as a singular miracle or a singular debunking. It is more durable.

Try a free, low-stakes observation—not an experiment

With a trusted friend, ask them to prepare a sequence of ordinary letters on paper and place two digits in it. Have them reveal the characters one at a time at whatever pace is comfortable, with each character replacing the previous one. Then write down the digits you remember. Try again with the digits farther apart. Keep your guesses before reviewing the sequence.

Do not treat a failure to remember a number as a diagnosis or a demonstration of the laboratory attentional blink. Manual timing, pacing, mask differences, expectations, vision, memory and the friend's knowledge all affect what happens. The point of the exercise is not to reproduce a controlled result. It is to experience the gap between seeing a stimulus and being able to report it—and to notice how easily a striking experience invites an overconfident explanation.

If flashing or rapidly changing images are uncomfortable, skip the exercise. Reading this investigation conveys the central finding; nothing is lost by not trying it. No animated demonstration, forced autoplay or attention test is required to use this article.

What the second number actually teaches

In the laboratory stream, the first target did not command a magical power over the next. It changed the work the observer was already doing. Under particular task conditions, that work made accurate reporting of nearby information harder. Later experiments complicated the apparent blackout with exceptions, partial processing, temporal errors and training-dependent effects.

The most reliable lesson is not that your mind is helplessly blind or that a commercial shortcut can fix it. It is that perception becomes scientifically interesting when a claim is narrow enough to be tested. A second number fell between frames of report. The difficult work was finding out precisely when, how, and why.
