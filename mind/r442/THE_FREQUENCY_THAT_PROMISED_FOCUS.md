# The Frequency That Promised to Make Us Focus
## What binaural-beat research can—and cannot—tell a reader choosing meditation audio

**Final Vestige Studios | Mind & Perception | November 17, 2026 | unpublished research manuscript**

A person puts on headphones. In the left ear, a tone hums at one pitch; in the right, another, slightly higher. Yet the listener reports something more: a faint, regular pulse that neither speaker emitted. It seems to arrive from somewhere inside the head.

That third rhythm is real as an experience. But is the rhythm also a remote control for concentration?

This is the pivotal distinction in commercial brainwave-audio claims. A listener's perceptual event, a measurable electrical response, a change in an attention-test score, and a lasting improvement in everyday work are **four different propositions**. Supporting one does not automatically prove the others. They demand different experiments, different controls and different measurements.

### An auditory illusion before a promise

Binaural beats arise when separate, sufficiently close frequencies are delivered to opposite ears. An example discussed in the 2023 systematic review is 400 hertz in one ear and 420 hertz in the other: the beat is experienced at the 20-hertz difference. The extra rhythm is not a third tone playing in the room. It is produced by auditory processing. That is scientifically intriguing on its own, without invoking a transformed mind.

A **monaural beat** is a different arrangement: two frequencies are combined before reaching the ears, creating an actual amplitude variation in the acoustic signal. The distinction matters. Claims about binaural stimulation cannot casually borrow findings from monaural or isochronic sound, and the phrase *brainwave audio* by itself does not identify which method a commercial recording uses.

Nor does the label “beta” supply the missing proof. Brain rhythms observed on an EEG are patterns associated with neural activity. Playing an audio beat with a numerical rate associated with an EEG band is not logically equivalent to producing a desired psychological state. The chain from stimulus to neuronal response to reliable useful behavior must be demonstrated.

**Primary source:** Ingendoh, Posny and Heine, *PLOS ONE*, May 19, 2023, https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0286023 ; full text https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10198548/ .

### Fourteen studies: no tidy verdict

The 2023 review is an especially useful antidote to both promotional certainty and overconfident debunking. The authors selected fourteen published studies examining whether binaural-beat stimulation produced brainwave entrainment. Five found results consistent with the entrainment hypothesis, eight contradicted it, and one was mixed. But the authors did not convert that tally into “science has disproved binaural beats.” The studies differed in stimulus construction, EEG metrics, research designs and analytical methods.

The distinction between a **mixed evidence base** and a **definitive negative result** is important. A poorly standardized field might contain a real, parameter-dependent effect that disappears when different conditions are averaged together. It could also contain experimental artifacts that look promising in small, uncontrolled studies. The available synthesis does not let us select a commercial track and declare that it will entrain the listener, much less improve employment, productivity or mental health.

The review's emphasis was reproducibility: standardization of how beats are delivered and how EEG responses are defined. Without that, two studies with the same headline may be testing meaningfully different things.

A reader might ask a seemingly simple question: *If five studies supported the claim, isn't that enough?* No—not if the intended claim is stronger than the one those studies examined. Evidence of an EEG response in a defined laboratory task does not show a retail MP3 causes better concentration all afternoon. The independent variable, population, comparison condition, measurement, effect size and follow-up would all need to match.

### The experiment that found something—and also did not

In a parametric study published in *Scientific Reports* in 2025, investigators randomized eighty undergraduate participants into sixteen combinations of binaural-beat parameters. The design crossed beat-frequency category (beta or gamma), carrier tone, stimulation onset, and the presence of background masking noise. Participants experienced two visits in a comparison of beats with control audio, and the investigators recorded EEG as well as attention performance.

The headline is tempting: under some settings, gamma-frequency stimulation with a lower carrier tone and white-noise background improved general attention performance. The researchers also reported EEG entrainment that varied with sound parameters.

Yet the study **did not show a reduction in the vigilance decrement over time**. That matters because many readers seeking a focus recording want the ability to stay attentive as minutes become hours, not merely a change in an average score during a specific laboratory test. The study offers a positive, bounded result, not an unlimited productivity guarantee.

Its design also makes an uncomfortable demand of advertising: if the effect is sensitive to carrier frequency, noise, onset and beat category, a company cannot simply cite “binaural beats work” and assume its own track replicates the tested protocol. The exact recording and customer circumstances matter. A recording described only by general product language might differ in several relevant ways.

**Research source:** *A parametric investigation of binaural beats for brain entrainment and enhancing sustained attention*, *Scientific Reports* (2025), https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-88517-z . Consult the paper for exact protocol and outcomes; this was not an Inspire3 product test.

### When an electrical effect is not a mood effect

A separate controlled neuroscience study compared binaural-beat stimulation with monaural beats across several stages of auditory processing. Both kinds produced some measured auditory responses; the patterns of cortical and connectivity effects differed. Yet the experimental manipulation did **not** yield the expected mood modulation.

This is a useful caution. A study can demonstrate that a nervous system responds to an audio stimulus and still fail to show a change in the human benefit a product page promises. In other words, *something measurable happened* and *the customer obtained the advertised benefit* are not interchangeable statements.

**Research source:** *Binaural Beats through the Auditory Pathway: From Brainstem to Connectivity Patterns*, 2020, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7082494/ .

### Twelve minutes without a miracle

There is a useful alternative to deciding that an effect either works for everybody or is worthless: establish a plain, inexpensive baseline.

Choose a small task you can actually finish or measure—sorting ten notes, editing a short paragraph, or reading and summarizing one page. Set a twelve-minute timer. Before you begin, write down the specific finish line. Work quietly, with notifications off, and record what you finished; do not score “focus” based entirely on how impressive you felt.

On a separate comparable occasion, try the same type of task with **ordinary, non-proprietary neutral audio** if you already like listening while you work. Keep the session length, time of day, task difficulty and interruptions as similar as practical. If the sound irritates or distracts you, stop it. Do not listen while driving, operating machinery, or in a situation where attention to real-world alarms matters.

A self-test cannot prove the mechanism of an audio frequency; expectancy, preference, task difficulty and ordinary variation remain confounds. It can, however, help answer a more humble question: *Was this particular listening routine compatible with my work?* You do not need a vendor's claimed universal benefit to make that choice.

### The historical echo

In Puységur's eighteenth-century magnetism records, the most fascinating development may not be the claimed phenomenon but the way an experience accumulated apparent authority. The operator recorded voices, tests, signed accounts and failures; a richly documented paper trail gradually became an explanation. Yet a witness can accurately describe a striking event without possessing the true mechanism behind it.

Final Vestige's **Puységur Animal Magnetism Files** preserves precisely this distinction. A notarial statement can verify that someone signed a statement; it cannot establish that animal magnetism was the cause. The archive also refuses to hide failed cures, consent problems, or the risks of an operator shaping a subject's report.

Modern audio research is not animal magnetism. But the analytical discipline transfers intact. An observed pulse is not a proof of entrainment; entrainment is not a proof of focus; a laboratory focus measure is not a promise of all-day work performance. The moment we stop distinguishing those layers, a plausible story begins impersonating a test result.

### Five questions before spending money on a mind-audio tool

**1. Which exact files are offered?** A twelve-level course, a single hypnosis track and multiple length variants answer different buying needs. Verify what is actually included at the particular offer page.

**2. What would count as success?** If the answer is only “feel different,” it may be impossible to separate preference from benefit. Choose a finite, observable task or a subjective relaxation target, without pretending either proves clinical efficacy.

**3. Is there research on this named product?** General meditation studies and laboratory sound experiments can provide context; they are not automatically trials of a seller's MP3.

**4. What does the actual checkout say?** Confirm current currency, total price, taxes, any upsell, delivery, recurring obligations, support and refund exceptions at the authorized destination. An older promo page is not proof of a referred checkout.

**5. Is the recommender being paid?** A credible resource should clearly say so near the earning link, and the investigation should remain readable and useful even if nobody buys.

### Our evidence boundary

The most useful finding is not that every brainwave claim is false. It is that different studies support different, narrower claims. The 2023 review shows heterogeneous evidence. The 2025 controlled study reports parameter-sensitive attention and EEG findings but not improved resistance to vigilance decline. A neuroscience study observes auditory responses without the hypothesized mood change. None of those studies, alone or together, establishes that Zen12, Focus Shot or Total Focus produces the vendor-advertised benefits.

A person might enjoy a guided meditation recording or find a neutral soundtrack pleasant. That subjective value can be real without entitling an editor to promise neurological transformation. Our responsibility is to keep those two sentences separate.
