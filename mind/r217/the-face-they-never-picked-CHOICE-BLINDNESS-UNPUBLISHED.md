# The Face They Never Picked
## Choice blindness, the explanation that arrives after the decision, and the importance of keeping an original record

**FINAL VESTIGE STUDIOS — MIND & PERCEPTION / UNPUBLISHED INVESTIGATION**  
**8 November 2026 | Editorial category: Perception & Attention / evidence history**  
**Publication gate:** Research and art copyedit required. No public slug, active affiliate link, medical claim, or invitation to deceive another person.

Imagine choosing between two photographs. Not in a courtroom, not for a high-stakes job, but because someone asks which face seems more attractive. You look at both, make an ordinary judgment, and point to one.

The researcher returns the photograph to you and asks a second question: why did you choose it?

There is a problem. The photograph in your hand is the other face.

The remarkable event is not simply that someone missed a card trick. It is what can happen *after* the switch: a person may explain the photograph they were handed as though it were the photograph they selected. A reason emerges in the presence of evidence that has been quietly substituted.

That is the unsettling premise of **choice blindness**, a line of research whose original 2005 paper still rewards careful reading. It is also a useful lesson for an evidence archive: a persuasive explanation of how something happened is not, by itself, a record that it happened that way.

### The unnoticed exchange

In a paper published on 7 October 2005 in *Science*, Petter Johansson, Lars Hall, Sverker Sikström and Andreas Olsson reported a decision task built around pairs of female-face photographs. Participants were asked which face in each pair they preferred. On selected trials, the researchers secretly altered the outcome presented to them: rather than receiving their chosen image, they received the rejected one. Participants were asked to explain their choices. [1]

The design matters. This was not an experiment in which people were specifically asked to police a magician's hands. The manipulation appeared during an otherwise ordinary preference task. The researchers wanted to test whether a mismatch between the intended choice and the experienced outcome would always be noticed.

Many such mismatches went undetected, and people sometimes offered reasons for the face in front of them. A later reading of the trial could contain a coherent-sounding explanation while the physical record of the selection told a different story.

Popular retellings often announce one dramatic percentage and then declare that human beings have no idea why they choose anything. That is too much weight for a face-selection experiment. Detection depends on the procedure, what counts as detection, when a person is questioned, how vigilant they have become, and what the alternatives look like. These are not interchangeable denominators. [1, 4]

The memorable observation is narrower and stronger: **under some experimentally arranged conditions, a person can fail to flag a substituted decision outcome and offer an explanation of it as their own choice.** That is an empirical vulnerability, not a total theory of free will.

### A card trick becomes an experimental instrument

A stage magician uses attention to disguise a substitution. The result is usually applause. In a laboratory, a similar device can create a carefully timed mismatch between an earlier act and a later report.

The distinction between performance and science is important. The trick alone tells you that an object was switched. The experiment asks what a participant noticed, how the participant accounted for the result, and how a manipulated trial compares with other trials under described conditions.

The original researchers did not simply interview people about why they make choices. They created a known discrepancy. The experimenter could identify which image the participant actually selected and which image the participant was later presented. That independent record is what made the reported explanation interpretable.

Remove the record, and the same conversation looks entirely different. An interviewer would hear a reason and have no way to know whether it corresponded to an actual decision. This is the very problem that makes eyewitness accounts, oral histories, product testimonials and archival documents hard to evaluate: reports are valuable evidence, but the report and the underlying event are not the same object.

A good investigator therefore wants the negative, the original selection card, the dated note, the chain of custody, or the unedited recording—something against which a later reconstruction can be checked.

### The jam counter

Five years later, the researchers asked whether this problem could appear outside the world of face photographs.

In a 2010 paper in *Cognition*, Lars Hall, Petter Johansson, Betty Tärning, Sverker Sikström and Thérèse Deutgen described a sampling stand at a supermarket. Shoppers tasted jam or smelled tea, expressed a preference, and were then asked to sample the supposedly chosen item again and explain why they preferred it. The experimenters used two-ended containers that could be inverted during initial sampling before the preference was recorded; on manipulated trials the subsequent presentation therefore did not match the participant's original sample choice. [2]

This is an unusually vivid setting. Taste and smell can be immediate and intensely personal: a jam may taste sharp, a tea may smell floral. Yet the investigators reported detection in approximately one-third of manipulated trials when concurrent detection, later recognition and sensory-change testing were considered together. That is not a one-third rate of immediate noticing, nor a universal fraction of consumers who can be deceived. [2]

It would be a mistake to transform the study into a marketer's instruction manual. First, deception in research was subject to an experimental context, ethics review practices and subsequent participant treatment; it is not a recommendation for a business to hide information from customers. Second, people in a low-stakes supermarket task have no reason to suspect their chosen jam will be replaced. Third, detecting a switch while talking to an experimenter is different from accurately distinguishing flavors under a deliberate blind test.

The deeper question is why explanations can be so ready. Once a person is presented with a particular outcome, the question “why did you choose this?” carries an assumption: *you chose this*. The participant may try sincerely to make sense of the immediate situation. A sincere explanation can still start from a false premise.

### The difference between a reason and a trace

Imagine finding two notes in an archive. The first was written at the time of a decision: “I selected option A.” The second was written an hour later: “I chose option B because it seemed clearer.”

A historian should not discard the second note. It may reveal what the author believed later, which arguments were available, or how a decision was publicly justified. But it cannot supersede the dated trace without further evidence.

Choice-blindness experiments engineer a tiny version of this conflict. The original selection and the later explanation can disagree. Instead of choosing one document as the sole truth, the researcher investigates the discrepancy.

That is different from saying people always lie about decisions. Lying implies a degree of awareness or intention that the tasks do not establish. *Confabulation* is a term sometimes used for explanations that go beyond available knowledge, but it too can be misunderstood as proof that a participant invented a story knowingly. The experimental result concerns the relation between selection, presented outcome, detection and report.

Nor should we assume all introspection is unreliable. Someone may have excellent reasons for an important decision, notice a mismatch immediately, or have clear records that can be checked. The point is to be less impressed by fluency alone.

### A replication with a question at the end

One weakness of a memorable first demonstration is that audiences may confuse the behavior of one group under one task with the behavior of everyone, everywhere. A 2014 paper by Fumihiko Taya, Swati Gupta, Ilya Farber and O'Dhaniel A. Mullette-Gillman introduced a computerized version. Participants selected faces, received manipulated feedback on some trials, and sometimes typed explanations. The researchers examined detection and later preference ratings, including a follow-up around two weeks later. [4]

They observed failures to detect substitutions under their procedure, but the design also showed why the question *when did you notice?* matters. Detection was not a single timeless property. Previous detection, the nature of the choice, later questioning and the study's definitions shaped the results. The paper reported short-term changes in later preference ratings: comparisons among trial types differed on day one. Of 32 participants, 25 completed the later online reassessment after 9–29 days (mean 16.1 days); the difference among trial types at that follow-up was not statistically significant (p = .93). The short-term laboratory response therefore must not be converted into a claim about permanent or general preference change.

That is a critical boundary. A temporary response shift in an experiment is not a lifelong personality transformation. An observed association between explanation and subsequent preference does not entitle a seller to promise that an audio file changes values, cures trauma, creates wealth or permanently reprograms thought.

Good science measures the thing it claims to measure. Good commercial copy should do the same.

### The consequential survey

The laboratory photographs also inspired a more consequential question: what if the substituted material concerned opinions rather than faces?

Hall and colleagues reported a 2013 *PLOS ONE* study conducted during Sweden's 2010 general election (with the results published in 2013). Participants answered questions about political issues. The researchers manipulated selected recorded responses and invited participants to explain the altered positions; they also examined a subsequently presented political-profile summary and stated voting intentions. [3]

The paper reported many undetected manipulations and shifts in measured responses in the experimental condition. The study is interesting because it moved from a fleeting attractiveness choice to expressed positions on public issues. But its findings are bounded by a particular sample, campaign context, survey instrument and intentionally manipulated feedback.

It is not proof that voters can be reliably converted by a magic trick, that every political belief is superficial, or that an online quiz can read someone's real convictions. The researchers themselves distinguished apparent openness on their measures from the far larger question of actual election behavior. A conscientious archive should not turn one political experiment into a partisan persuasion tactic.

For Final Vestige, the relevant warning applies to *any* issue: a survey report, headline or summary can alter the frame within which someone interprets their own prior position. Where the original response survives, checking it is a simple defense against accidental reconstruction.

### Three things the experiment cannot prove

It cannot establish that no one knows why they choose. Most decisions in life are not made between two photographs handed across a table under covert manipulation.

It cannot establish that a changed report indicates a false memory in the clinical sense. The task can change the outcome someone believes was presented, what they are willing to report, or how they explain the current object. Those processes are not automatically identical to erasing an earlier memory.

And it cannot establish that commercial hypnosis, meditation audio or binaural beats cause the same effect, prevent the effect, or enhance reasoning. The choice-blindness studies were not evaluations of Zen12, Hypnosis Live or Brainwave Shots. Any product-specific psychological or health claim must stand on product-specific competent evidence; its truth cannot be borrowed from an unrelated laboratory effect. The FTC's health-products guidance emphasizes the need to assess the actual claimed outcome and the adequacy of the relevant research. [6]

This last distinction protects the reader and the studio alike. There is a legitimate audience for a structured audio library. There is also a legitimate question about whether a library suits a person's listening preferences, schedule, device and budget. But neither question is answered by a swapped photograph.

### A field exercise that does not trick anyone

The original study involved covert substitution. **Do not repeat that deception on an unsuspecting friend, student, customer or child.** You can explore the evidentiary lesson without manipulating a person's choice.

Take two ordinary alternatives—two book covers you have already seen, two neutral typefaces, or two unrelated public-domain illustrations you own the rights to reproduce. Privately choose one. Record the choice, with a date, *before* explaining it.

Next write two or three reasons for your decision. Then deliberately examine the alternative you rejected and write the strongest good-faith case **for that alternative**, clearly labeled as a counterargument. Compare the two accounts with the original note.

The question is not whether you can make yourself believe you chose the other object. You will know which one you picked; there is no hidden swap. The useful observation is that fluent reasons can be generated for more than one option, while the recorded decision remains fixed.

For an accessibility-first classroom version, allow spoken or written responses, avoid judging faces or physical attractiveness, and make the purpose explicit in advance. Never collect named preference records without an educational need and an appropriate privacy basis. The exercise is an analogy about evidence and reasons, **not a replication, diagnosis or demonstration that anyone experienced choice blindness**.

If there is a disagreement later—about a selected file, the date an instruction changed, the version of a document, or a decision that shaped a project—check the original trace before you debate the explanation. This is a modest habit with enormous investigative value.

### A bridge to Puységur, without granting the theory

The Mind & Perception Archive also preserves the historical record of suggestion, including Final Vestige's [Puységur's Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/). That eighteenth-century material documents observations, testimony and competing interpretations emerging from animal-magnetism practice. It is not a modern randomized trial of an invisible fluid, and the choice-blindness experiments do not vindicate its theories. [5]

The intellectual bridge is a question, not a mechanism: what happens when a person's report is shaped by the circumstances in which it is requested? Historical suggestion narratives invite source criticism. Modern choice-blindness paradigms offer a controlled mismatch to study. Both deserve precise language about what was actually recorded.

A historical witness may be sincere and inaccurate. An experimental participant may be cooperative and unaware of a switch. A product reviewer may be enthusiastic and still lack evidence for a specific health claim. None of these statements is a judgment of character; they are reasons to keep the event, the report and the conclusion separate.

### Before the story settles

Return to the first scene. A person points to a photograph and then explains another. If you only hear the explanation, you may never learn there was a switch.

What made the original experiment powerful was not that people could be fooled. That is an old lesson. What made it scientific was that the investigators preserved the choice and introduced a known mismatch. The underlying record allowed a later account to be tested.

That is the discipline Final Vestige should carry across its research. Preserve what happened. Preserve what was said about it. Put the two side by side. Let the discrepancy sharpen the question instead of smoothing it away.

A polished reason may explain a decision. It is not automatically proof of the decision.
