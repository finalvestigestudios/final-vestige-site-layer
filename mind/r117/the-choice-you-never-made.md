# The Choice You Never Made
**Final Vestige Studios | Mind & Perception Archive | Editorial manuscript — UNPUBLISHED | 4 November 2026**
**Editorial classification:** Evidence & Investigation / Decision-Making | **Proposed slug:** /mind/choice-blindness/ | **Commercial links:** none

*You can tell a convincing story about a decision. That does not always mean the decision happened the way you remember it.*

## The exchange

Imagine a researcher places two portraits on a table. You are asked which face you find more attractive. You point to one. A moment later the researcher returns what appears to be your selected photograph and asks why you chose it. You answer easily. Something about the expression, perhaps, or the eyes. Only the portrait in your hand is not the one you selected.

That exchange was the premise of an ingenious 2005 experiment by Petter Johansson, Lars Hall, Sverker Sikström and Andreas Olsson. Their article in *Science* was titled "Failure to Detect Mismatches Between Intention and Outcome in a Simple Decision Task." Participants compared pairs of faces; the researchers covertly manipulated which image was subsequently displayed as the participant's choice. Some people did not detect the mismatch. Asked to explain their preference, they nevertheless supplied reasons for the picture they had actually rejected. [1]

No grand deception about a person's childhood was required. No drugs, trance or elaborate apparatus. The researchers created a small discontinuity between *intention* and *apparent outcome*—then studied the stories people told to bridge it. The name they gave the phenomenon was **choice blindness**.

The finding is unsettling because an explanation usually feels like evidence of a memory. We assume we recall a decision and then give the reason. In these trials, reasons were also generated when the offered outcome differed from the original choice. A fluent explanation could accompany a mistaken account of what had happened.

That does **not** mean every explanation is invented, or that most everyday decisions are made without awareness. The original result belongs to an experimentally manipulated task, under particular conditions, with a specific detection measure. Its importance is the possibility it demonstrates: the mind's capacity to make sense of an outcome can outrun its recognition that the outcome was switched.

## The laboratory's controlled surprise

The face-choice task is often retold as a magic trick, and the trick matters. Participants were given a meaningful preference task but did not expect the identity of their selection to be tampered with. The manipulation created a moment in which the person's prior intention, the physical item presented, and the explanation requested by another person no longer aligned.

Consider three separate questions that a careful observer should ask.

First: **Did the person choose an item?** Yes; the initial choice was made. Second: **Was the later item the one selected?** On manipulated trials, no. Third: **Did the person's explanation reveal that discrepancy?** Not reliably. A persuasive reason could be offered for the wrong item. These are different measurements, and their difference is the heart of the investigation.

The researchers did not merely study poor memory of a photograph after a long delay. They engineered a mismatch in a simple decision and asked for an immediate explanation. That is why the original result is especially useful to readers interested in testimony, interviews, sales presentations and the reliability of their own recollections. It warns against treating confidence and narrative coherence as proof of an unbroken chain from perception to choice.

It is equally important not to overstate it. The study did not prove that people lack preferences or that every preference is manipulable. People sometimes detected the mismatch; the manipulation was not uniformly successful. Detection depends on what is being changed, how much contrast there is between alternatives, the opportunity to compare, and what the observer expects to need to verify.

## A paper that argued back

The investigators followed this idea beyond pictures of faces. In a 2012 *PLOS ONE* paper, Lars Hall, Petter Johansson and Thomas Strandberg studied responses to a self-transforming questionnaire on moral issues. The experimental instrument concealed reversals of some participants' answers, allowing researchers to present the participant with an altered position as if it were the one originally endorsed. [2]

Here the object at risk was not merely a portrait. It was an assertion of belief. The authors reported that **69% of participants failed to detect at least one of two reversals**. Some participants went on to explain and defend the position they had not initially expressed. The exact denominator and manipulation matter: the 69% figure concerns failing to detect *at least one of two experimental changes*, not a claim that 69% of moral beliefs are false, unstable or implanted.

A moral response on a survey is not the same thing as an enduring conviction. There may be ambiguity in wording, situational uncertainty, weak attitudes, social pressure, or different ways of understanding a question. The method deliberately takes advantage of a participant's trust that the answer in front of them matches the answer they supplied. The resulting defense is evidence about explanation under altered feedback, not a universal measure of character.

The most interesting question is almost historical. If a source presents a statement as a witness's own, does the witness challenge it, or begin to explain it? A researcher with a page of questions can control the wording and the feedback; an archivist examining a century-old document cannot. We must be even more careful about distinguishing what a person *said*, what an interviewer *wrote*, and what a later editor *inferred*.

## Touch was not a guarantee

Another small experiment moved the effect into the hands. Catherine Steenfeldt-Kristensen and Ian Thornton published a 2013 study of **haptic choice blindness**, using real objects that could be touched but not seen. Participants explored pairs of objects inside an apparatus that allowed the items to be switched between choice and justification. For similar object pairs, reported detection rates were around **22%**. For more distinctive pairs, detection rose to approximately **70%**. [3]

That contrast matters far more than an isolated viral number. The phenomenon was not an all-purpose failure to recognize a choice. Greater distinctiveness made changes easier to detect. It is possible to improve the conditions of verification; choice blindness is not a fate from which no careful observer can escape.

There is a practical lesson buried in the mechanics. If someone hands you an object and says, "This is what you chose," you can compare the object to a recorded choice. Without that original record, your brain is being asked to reconstruct both the choice and the reason from the scene it now occupies.

An independent record changes the problem. It turns a memory test into a comparison. The external note does not make your judgment right. It makes the chain of events auditable.

## What the experiment does not settle

Choice blindness is sometimes used in sweeping claims that the human mind invents all its reasons after the fact. The experiments are more specific. They show that **under certain manipulations** people may miss discrepancies between their earlier choice and the outcome shown to them, then provide explanations consistent with that altered outcome.

They do not establish the absence of conscious deliberation, the uselessness of testimony, or a general claim that nobody knows what they want. They also do not establish the efficacy of hypnosis, subliminal audio, meditation recordings, or any other commercial intervention. The mechanism in the face experiment was a physical substitution, not neurological control through sound.

It would be misleading to claim that a moral questionnaire experiment proves a method for changing political convictions on demand. In related research on political attitude reports, investigators examined how altered survey feedback can shape recorded opinions under study conditions. [4] That is an experimental question about responses, not an ethical endorsement of deceptive persuasion or evidence that a media campaign can reliably convert voters.

A good research article asks not only whether an effect was observed, but **what was actually manipulated**, **what outcome was counted**, and **which generalizations have not been tested**. The more astonishing the apparent conclusion, the more these boundaries matter.

## The archive and the guided witness

Final Vestige's historical reporting examines a parallel problem in different language: the relationship between a witness, an authority figure and a record. The published *Puységur's Animal Magnetism Files* traces episodes in which observers tried to make sense of unusual behavior and claims of altered mental states. The 1784 investigations into animal magnetism belong to their own archival and intellectual context; modern choice-blindness studies do not establish that animal magnetism worked.

The connection is methodological, not supernatural. If an examiner suggests the interpretation, a subject may incorporate that framing into what they report. If a later narrator accepts an outcome as already established, the account can grow more confident without becoming better verified. Historians face precisely the question a careful experimentalist does: **what can we attribute to the participant, and what entered the record through the observer?**

This is why the original document matters. In a historical case, look for the sequence: who asked which question, what was recorded at the time, what happened before the account was published, and what alternative explanation was available. A published conclusion is not the same kind of source as a contemporaneous observation. The distance between them is where unsupported certainty accumulates.

Readers can approach our published [Mesmerism investigation](https://finalvestige.com/2026/11/02/mesmerism/) and the [Puységur source archive](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) with that distinction in mind. One project is about eighteenth-century practices and claims; the other about contemporary cognitive experiments. They meet at the problem of what counts as evidence.

## A harmless exercise: the decision receipt

Here is an original **reflection exercise, not a validated psychological test**. It is not designed to replicate Johansson and colleagues' deceptive manipulation. You do not need to involve another person or try to fool anyone.

Choose a low-stakes decision: one of two essays to read, the next historical topic to research, or which of two equally priced notebooks you prefer. Before you choose, write down the two options and three reasons that matter to you. Make the selection, date the note, and leave it unchanged.

Later, revisit the decision and explain in a fresh paragraph why you chose as you did—*without looking at the note first*. Then compare the fresh explanation with your contemporaneous record. Did the same reasons recur? Did a consideration you had not noticed become prominent? Did the original reason stop feeling persuasive?

No single outcome proves choice blindness. The exercise contains no concealed swap, control group, randomization or blinded coding. A changed explanation might reflect ordinary reflection, new information or a perfectly reasonable reassessment. The point is more modest: a dated record lets you distinguish what you said *then* from what makes sense to you *now*.

A useful journal is sometimes more valuable for the corrections it permits than for the confidence it records. The Seven Day Focus Fieldbook provides an optional free reflection structure for readers who want to keep that practice; it is [already available through Final Vestige's existing Dispatch route](https://finalvestige.com/focus/). It is not a condition of reading this investigation, and it is not a treatment or evidence of psychological improvement.

## Why the effect belongs in a media-saturated world

Today, explanations travel farther and faster than the choices and observations they describe. A clipped interview can show the confident answer but not the original question. A comparison video can present the final object while omitting the moment it was selected. An edited screenshot can display a person apparently endorsing a sentence they never wrote.

Choice blindness does not explain every misleading clip. But it makes one discipline unusually valuable: preserve the original sequence. When a stranger assures you that an expert "admitted" a claim, seek the uncut source. When a product review summarizes your options, ask whether the compared items were actually the ones presented. When a memory feels obvious, identify the contemporaneous record against which it can be checked.

The central lesson is not to distrust human beings. It is to build procedures that help honest observers catch honest mistakes—and deliberate substitutions. A source note, an original image, a complete transcript and a clearly dated statement of uncertainty are not aesthetic extras. They are practical defenses against the temptation to make the present explain the past.

There is something disarming about the face experiment. The participant did make a choice. The researcher did change the outcome. The participant could still construct an answer. For a moment, explanation and evidence parted company.
