# The Room Outside the Photograph: Why Memory Changes the Frame

**Final Vestige Studios | Mind & Perception | ORIGINAL UNPUBLISHED INVESTIGATION | Human editorial review required**

The photograph stops at a table leg. The floor disappears at the bottom of the frame, the window is cropped at the top, and an ordinary wall extends beyond the right edge. Later you remember the room as though the camera had stood farther back. You may even sketch the missing floor and wall without noticing that those portions were never recorded. What did you actually see, and what did you plausibly complete?

Psychologists call one version of this phenomenon **boundary extension**. It is often introduced as an example of the mind predicting space beyond a picture. That explanation contains an important grain of truth, but the experiments tell a more interesting story: for some images, memories systematically **contract** rather than extend the view. An investigator who reports only the extension has left out the crucial challenge.

## The remarkable drawings of 1989

Helene Intraub and Michael Richardson's 1989 paper *Wide-angle memories of close-up scenes* studied memory for photographs. In one experiment, 37 undergraduates viewed 20 pictures for 15 seconds apiece. The authors reported that 95% of participants' drawings included plausible information extending outside the original image boundaries. The paper also examined recognition judgments. [S1]

That 95% number is not an estimate for everyone, every photo, or everyday eyewitness reports. It applies to the drawing results under the conditions the investigators used. The added information was not recovered footage. It was content that *might reasonably have existed* outside the crop.

It is understandable why a visual system might do this. In ordinary life a table continues beneath our field of view, and turning our heads reveals previously unseen parts of a room. A photo abruptly ends that continuity. One hypothesis is that mental scene representations incorporate expectations about what lies just beyond the border. But a plausible explanation must be tested against scenes that do not invite the same kind of reconstruction.

## The unexpected reversal

In 2020 Wilma Bainbridge and Chris Baker investigated 1,000 images with 2,000 participants using rapid recognition tasks. They found that object-centered, close-up photographs tended to produce extension, whereas many broad, scene-oriented photographs produced **boundary contraction**: a narrower remembered perspective. Drawing tasks showed related transformations, sometimes even with the original image visible. [S2]

These results complicate the familiar slogan that memory 'always fills in the edges.' A closely framed object and a distant landscape carry different visual statistics, spatial cues, and competing contents. The authors challenged the idea that extension is a universal visual-memory distortion. Subsequent debate has asked how much of the pattern is attributable to image composition, viewpoint, memory, attention or drawing itself. Such disagreement is a feature of real science, not a failure to deliver a neat conclusion.

Consider an archival photograph of a single instrument in a workshop and another photograph showing the whole workshop. Applying the same explanation to the memories of both pictures is a methodological mistake unless the evidence supports it. The frame and photographic context are variables, not neutral decorations.

## Distance, depth and what looks natural

Researchers later manipulated apparent viewing distance while controlling other features of scenes. Across seven experiments published in *Psychological Science*, perceived distance affected the direction of remembered boundary transformations. [S3]

In 2023, Marco Gandolfo, Hendrik Nägele and Marius Peelen investigated another factor: photographic **depth of field**. A photograph taken at a very narrow aperture may keep distant and nearby material sharply focused in a way that differs from ordinary human vision. Across four experiments involving 125 adults, boundary extension was stronger under more naturalistic depth-of-field conditions. [S4]

The studies do not establish a single final mechanism. Predicting unseen space and shifting toward a typical perspective are competing or overlapping accounts. Some researchers also point to properties of the task and the attentional demands of the image. Crucially, none of these experiments tested a commercial meditation recording.

## A source isn't the entire scene

Suppose a century-old newspaper image captures half a wall calendar beside a door. A narrator later describes the calendar's full shape and a lamp just beyond the left edge. Perhaps the narrator saw a different original photograph; perhaps those details came from another witness; perhaps they were plausible guesses. A boundary-extension study does not let us determine which explanation applies to this particular case.

A responsible evidence archive should keep three layers separate: **the photographed fact**, **the reported memory**, and **the historian's inference**. This is especially important when the surviving item is already cropped, color-corrected, or reproduced at a lower resolution. Every edit changes the frame that later readers encounter.

Final Vestige's [Puységur Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) trace an eighteenth-century attempt to establish extraordinary claims using patient speech, signed statements, witness accounts and repeated procedures. [S5] This comparison is methodological, not causal: boundary extension does not explain Puységur's subjects or prove or disprove magnetism. Both cases show why investigators must distinguish a person's report from independent verification of the reported event.

## A free twelve-minute edge audit

This is an educational demonstration, not a validated diagnostic or personal memory test.

**Minutes 0–2:** Select an ordinary photograph you own, ideally a room or landscape without people. Save the original unmodified image and note its dimensions and source.

**Minutes 2–4:** View it for fifteen seconds. Hide it. Draw a rectangle and reconstruct the most important objects, marking where they touch the boundaries.

**Minutes 4–6:** On a second sheet, draw the scene you think may extend outside the photograph. Use dotted outlines for guesses. Do not label plausible surroundings as observed facts.

**Minutes 6–8:** Compare the first drawing with the source. Did you remember a wider, narrower or similar scene? Which new details appeared? One attempt cannot establish a pattern.

**Minutes 8–10:** Consider alternative causes: rough drawing skill, delayed recall, composition, focal object, depth of field, familiarity, or simple guessing.

**Minutes 10–12:** Write three sentences: 'The surviving image shows…'; 'I drew or claimed…'; and 'I cannot verify…'. Preserve the distinction.

No data needs to be submitted. The exercise cannot reproduce the laboratory results without controlled stimuli, comparison groups and analysis. Its purpose is to practice source discipline.

## What the research does not prove about audio products

These visual-memory experiments are **not** product-efficacy trials of Zen12, Hypnosis Live or Brainwave Shots. Even if their sellers describe guided listening, brainwave audio or attention benefits, it would be misleading to imply that boundary memory research proves the effectiveness of those products. Claims about therapeutic or objective benefits require appropriate substantiation under FTC guidance. [S6]

For a no-cost practice path, the existing [Seven Day Focus Fieldbook](https://finalvestige.com/focus/) provides a voluntary routine and a checklist for evaluating meditation audio. No purchase is required to read this investigation, follow its sources, or complete its exercise.

The evidence itself supplies the ending. An experimental effect that once seemed to tell a simple story grew more revealing when researchers changed the photographs. Before building a mystery around a remembered scene, preserve the original border. Everything outside that border deserves to be called an inference until another source can show it.
