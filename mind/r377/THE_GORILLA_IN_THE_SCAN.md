# The Gorilla in the Scan: What Experts Can Look At and Still Not See

**Final Vestige Studios | Mind & Perception / Evidence-led investigation | Unpublished editorial candidate | November 14, 2026**

*An unexpected silhouette appeared in a medical image. The investigators were trained to find abnormalities, and some eyes passed over the inserted shape. What, precisely, does that tell us about expertise, attention and the stories we build around seeing?*

## A shape where no one expected one

Imagine a reader examining one chest CT slice after another, looking for a particular sort of small spot. The task requires discipline: do not be distracted by irrelevant textures or artifacts; attend to signs that fit the search. Now insert something that emphatically does not fit. It is not a subtle nodule, not a questionable shadow, and not something with a medical meaning. It is an image of a gorilla.

That is the unusual design of Trafton Drew, Melissa Võ and Jeremy Wolfe's 2013 experiment, *The Invisible Gorilla Strikes Again: Sustained Inattentional Blindness in Expert Observers*. Twenty-four radiologists searched for lung nodules in CT images. In the last case, the researchers inserted a gorilla silhouette. Its area was described as 48 times that of the average nodule. **Twenty of the twenty-four radiologists did not report seeing it.** Eye tracking showed that a majority of those who missed it nevertheless looked at its location. [Primary article and methods](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3964612/); [journal record](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797613479386).

It is a startling finding, but it is not a verdict that radiologists are negligent, incapable of diagnosis, or uniquely vulnerable. The gorilla was an artificial surprise inserted into a research task. Radiologists were instructed to detect nodules, not to catalogue all whimsical imagery. The experiment examined what happens when task-relevant expertise meets an object outside the expected category. It did not measure patient harm, routine clinical error rates, or the performance of a particular radiology department.

The better question is not “How could an expert fail?” It is “What does it mean to see something while searching for something else?” That question points toward a weakness in a familiar metaphor: eyes as cameras and attention as an always-on recorder. The visual system is neither a complete internal photograph nor simply an empty screen. It selects, interprets and discards in the service of a task.

## From basketball court to CT slice

The study belongs to an established line of work. In 1999, Daniel Simons and Christopher Chabris examined sustained inattentional blindness: observers counting passes in a basketball sequence could fail to notice an unexpected event. Their findings addressed the role of task demands and similarities between expected and unexpected events. This is **inattentional blindness**, not identical to *change blindness*, in which a change between versions of a scene fails to register. [Simons and Chabris, 1999](https://doi.org/10.1068/p281059).

The distinction matters. A person missing a switched pedestrian behind a passing door has failed to register a change. A reader missing a gorilla that appears during nodule search has failed to consciously notice an unexpected object. Both involve selective attention; the methods, demands and interpretations differ. Final Vestige's existing [change-blindness investigation](https://finalvestige.com/2026/11/02/change-blindness/) explains the door and flicker paradigms in detail. This story examines the further question of *expert search*, rather than reproducing that account.

The design in the 2013 paper made expertise the point. If an untrained participant overlooks a surprise during a taxing task, perhaps that says little about trained observers. By placing the unexpected gorilla within a familiar task, the authors tested that intuition. The result contradicted the simple assumption that expertise eliminates inattentional blindness. It did not contradict the ordinary value of training. Being highly practiced at detecting particular signs is not equivalent to being guaranteed to detect every object that could ever appear.

One detail deserves special care. In popular retellings, the phrase “they looked right at it” can sound as though a camera proved conscious recognition and willful disregard. Eye tracking estimates gaze location. It does not read awareness. Fixating a region and recognizing an object are different observations. The study supports the more precise claim that some observers' measured gaze crossed the inserted gorilla while they did not report detecting it.

## What the number actually means

The memorable figure is **83 percent**: twenty out of twenty-four. It is the observed fraction of radiologists who did not report the gorilla in that experimental context. It is not a universal constant of perception, and it is not an estimate of missed real-life abnormalities. A sample of twenty-four is informative for a controlled demonstration but cannot produce a universal personal prediction.

The researchers also included naive observers trained briefly for the task. Those observers did not detect the inserted gorilla in their comparable trial. This comparison helps avoid the sensational framing that professionals did “worse than ordinary people.” The expert participants were better prepared for the nodule-search objective; the surprise was outside that objective. Those two statements can both be true.

A useful three-column ledger keeps the categories separate:

| Observed | Inferred with care | Not demonstrated |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 20 of 24 radiologists did not report the added gorilla | Expert search can be selective and miss unexpected objects | That 83% of clinicians miss important medical findings |
| Eye tracking placed many nonreporters' gaze at its location | Gaze is not equivalent to conscious report | That the object was fully recognized and ignored |
| The unfamiliar silhouette was inserted into a CT task | Search set shapes what gets noticed | That all surprises are equally likely to be missed |
| Original research used a controlled stimulus | The effect is testable in an experiment | That any audio product can train this vulnerability away |

Numbers gain explanatory power when connected to what was actually measured. Detached from methods, they can become entertainment masquerading as scientific authority.

## Is the brain “deleting reality”?

That is a tempting headline but a misleading description. The researchers did not show that a visible object was erased from the world or that an observer secretly knew about it. What exists in the visual scene, what reaches the retina, where the eyes point, and what becomes reportable awareness are distinguishable things. Calling the entire process “deletion” implies a mechanism the study did not establish.

Nor is a surprise guaranteed to be unnoticed. Task instructions, the object's relevance, display duration, observer knowledge and stimulus characteristics all matter. The 1999 study itself showed that detection varies with properties of the task and unexpected event. The famous gorilla is useful as a demonstration, but it should not be treated as a law that applies identically to every person in every circumstance.

A caution also works in the opposite direction. The experiment does not license the claim that a person's failure to notice a detail proves that they are lying. Failure of attention is one possibility among many. In ordinary investigative work, investigators still need documents, timings, vantage points, corroborating observations and careful interviews. One laboratory effect is not a universal explanation for conflicting testimony.

This is where the subject joins the Final Vestige mission. To investigate a record is not merely to ask what somebody said they saw. It is to ask how the observation was made, what the observer was looking for, what information could be recorded and which independent traces survive.

## A historical archive asks the same kind of question

In the late eighteenth century, Amand-Marie-Jacques de Chastenet, Marquis de Puységur, presented observations of animal magnetism and magnetic somnambulism through an elaborate trail of accounts, signatures, predictions, patient statements and procedural details. The archive can be historically rich without proving the invisible force those witnesses believed they were documenting.

Final Vestige's [Puységur Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) reconstructs the claims through 153 reviewed source rows and sixteen case dossiers. Its core task is to separate the history of testimony from the question of an underlying cause. A signed statement proves that a claim was recorded, under certain documentary conditions. It does not automatically verify the causal explanation attached to the statement.

It would be anachronistic to diagnose the eighteenth-century witnesses with inattentional blindness or to use a twenty-first-century CT experiment as proof of what occurred in Puységur's chamber. The historical and laboratory episodes are not the same event, nor the same kind of source. Their connection is methodological: *a disciplined record tells us what was reported; causal claims require further evidence.*

A historian who wants to understand expectation must not assume that all surprising reports are false. The challenge is to stay open to what the record contains while remaining demanding about what its contents can establish.

## An original twelve-minute observation audit

You can practice the distinction without manipulating another person, reproducing a clinical scan, or trying to diagnose your own attention. This is not a validation study, a cognitive treatment or a claimed method for preventing mistakes. It is a simple way to make the observation/inference boundary visible.

**Minutes 0–2: Choose a harmless scene.** On your own desk, select six ordinary items. Avoid private documents and objects belonging to someone else. Write down what you intend to observe: number of items, color, arrangement or visible labels. Choose just one criterion. Do not look away to stage a trick or ask a friend to deceive someone.

**Minutes 2–4: Record your selection.** Look for twenty seconds, then make a short written inventory. Note which details you felt confident about and which were guesses. Do not mark an unrecorded feature as definitely absent; not noticing and not existing are different propositions.

**Minutes 4–7: Compare without self-scoring theater.** Return to the scene and compare your inventory with the actual arrangement. Where your notes are incomplete, write “not recorded,” not “missed because my brain erased it.” The distinction is small but important. It protects the evidence.

**Minutes 7–10: Change the task, not the world.** Look again, this time attending to a different criterion such as the number of edges or the printed words. Compare the new notes with the earlier ones. The exercise is not a clinical test of performance. It simply illustrates how instructions shape what we choose to document.

**Minutes 10–12: Write the two-column conclusion.** On one side record what you actually observed and checked. On the other write your interpretation, clearly labeled as such. If you are uncertain, leave the question open. That habit matters in research, journalism and everyday decision-making.

## The commercial boundary: attention research is not audio efficacy proof

The discovery that trained observers can miss an unexpected object is fascinating. It is **not** proof that an MP3 recording improves attentional capacity, makes a user more observant, changes brainwaves in a beneficial manner, treats anxiety or prevents clinical errors.

Neither obligation is discharged by an exciting laboratory abstract attached to a different commercial product.

## The result worth remembering

In the 2013 study, the radiologists were not doing nothing. They were doing what they had been asked to do: inspect CT images for particular findings. Some of them were looking at the location of the unexpected gorilla and still did not report it. That separation between gaze, task and awareness is the valuable discovery.

The historical archive supplies the companion lesson: documents can preserve testimony beautifully while leaving its mechanism unresolved. Both lessons caution against easy certainty. They do not demand cynicism. They demand a better question: **what exactly was observed, and what would it take to support the next claim?**

That is a question worth carrying from the imaging lab to the archive, and from an intriguing product promise to the decision about spending your money.
