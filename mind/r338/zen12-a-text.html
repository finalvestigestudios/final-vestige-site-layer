A gorilla, a curtain and a more useful question

In a classic experiment, viewers tracking basketball passes often missed an unexpected gorilla. Later researchers filmed a version with other surprising events; knowing the gorilla trick did not mean viewers would catch the rest. A 2023 study of 4,493 people in three experiments further showed that the speed of an unexpected moving object changes how readily it is noticed. Those controlled studies reveal conditions under which attention changes. They did not test Zen12, hypnosis downloads or brainwave audio.

There is a lesson for a buyer: a genuinely surprising scientific result is not evidence for every product that mentions concentration. Judge the seller's format and support claims on their own terms. 1999 paper · 2010 follow-up · 2023 speed study.

One useful free experiment first

Choose a small reading task. Spend two minutes writing the exact thing you want to notice—for example, dates in a page of historical text. Work on it quietly for eight minutes. Then use two minutes to record what you found and what your chosen question made you overlook. This is a practical self-observation, not a diagnostic assessment or blinded clinical test.

What would justify the purchase—and what would not?

Reasonable format preference: you like timed recordings, want a larger sequence instead of one track, and prefer guided, music, nature or white-noise choices. If you don't enjoy the format, start with quiet or a free tool instead.

Not established: that Zen12 produces an hour's meditation effect in twelve minutes, treats a medical condition, guarantees productivity, or alters the brain in a clinically useful way. Seller copy makes broad promises. Those statements require their own competent, product-specific evidence rather than inference from unrelated experiments.
