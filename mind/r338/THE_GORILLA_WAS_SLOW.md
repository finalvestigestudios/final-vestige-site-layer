# The Gorilla Was Slow. That Matters.
## What a 2023 experiment changes about the most famous failure of attention
**Final Vestige Studios — Mind & Perception · Unpublished editorial research, 12 November 2026 · Fact review required**

A stranger crosses a basketball court. Nobody is supposed to miss him. He is dressed as a gorilla, and at one point he stops to pound his chest. Later, roughly half the viewers of one well-known experimental condition say they never saw him.

It is an irresistible story. It is also too tidy. Repeated across talks, short-form videos and marketing pitches, the gorilla often becomes proof of a grander slogan: our brains miss what matters, so someone else's method must train us to notice everything. The first proposition has a substantial experimental history. The second does not follow.

In May 2023, four researchers published an especially revealing challenge. They didn't deny inattentional blindness. They asked a narrower question: **does the speed of an unexpected object change whether we see it?** Their experiments suggest that motion speed can radically change the outcome. The famous failure is real; the idea that it is an inescapable property of concentration is incomplete.

### A filmed event and the limits of seeing

Daniel Simons and Christopher Chabris published *Gorillas in Our Midst* in 1999. Participants watched people passing balls, attending to one team and counting passes. An unexpected event appeared. In a memorable version, a gorilla-suited actor entered the scene, faced the camera, struck its chest and left. The unexpected event lasted about nine seconds. A later account by Simons says only about half noticed in the striking version. [1999 primary paper](https://doi.org/10.1068/p281059); [2010 author account, open full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3563049/).

The experiment is not a claim that people's eyesight stopped working. Nor does it show that a person failed to encode every physical feature of the scene. It measures whether an unexpected object entered conscious report under a particular task. A narrow attention instruction—count the passes of a designated team—shapes what someone is trying to observe. What is irrelevant to that goal can be extraordinarily visible in the picture and absent from their answer.

This is why a video clip detached from the question asked of participants is not the whole experiment. Once you know the punch line, your own response is no longer equivalent to that of an unsuspecting first-time subject. A teacher can use the clip to demonstrate a limitation. An advertiser cannot quietly turn that limitation into clinical proof that its soundtrack or training program repairs attention.

### Knowing about the gorilla isn't a general cure

In 2010 Simons published a follow-up with a changed scene. Viewers could recognize the gorilla because they knew the trick. But the new clip also had two other departures from the expected action: a stage curtain changed color and a player disappeared. People familiar with the original were not better at noticing every new surprise; the study reported they were slightly less likely to notice the other two events. [2010 open paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3563049/).

The lesson is not “knowledge makes you blind.” It is that one memorized hazard can absorb attention while other changes go unattended. In practice, training a person to spot a particular trick is not the same as establishing a broad, transferable attentional advantage. Measuring transfer demands a different experiment—new settings, new events, comparisons, and outcomes that go beyond recognizing one taught stimulus.

Simons's original task and its follow-up also carry an elementary warning about media: knowing a result before testing someone changes the conditions. In a TikTok reenactment, commenters may have seen the original video, read the caption, or been told to watch for the gorilla. Their comments are not interchangeable with randomized study observations.

### The 2023 speed challenge

Pascal Wallisch, Wayne Mackey, Michael Karlovich and David Heeger published *The Visible Gorilla* in *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* on May 22, 2023. Across three experiments with a total of 4,493 participants, their parametric tasks varied features of unexpected moving objects. They found that fast unexpected objects were readily noticeable, unlike otherwise comparable slow ones. In their interpretation, fast motion can redirect attention even when an observer is focused elsewhere. [PNAS primary study](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2214930120).

That distinction is consequential. A rule such as “attention makes the entire unexpected world disappear” predicts too much. People remain capable of responding to some unexpected signals. One plausible function of attention is to balance ongoing task demands with cues that may warrant interruption. Speed can be such a cue. The new study narrows the conditions of the phenomenon, rather than invalidating the older findings.

This does **not** mean that fast-moving objects are always safe, that motion alone guarantees detection, or that a lab result automatically tells us what someone will notice on a road, at work or in a medical setting. The experiments use defined tasks and measurements. Generalization to daily life requires further evidence. Similarly, the authors' findings cannot be used to promise improved attention from passive audio. There is no Zen12 arm, no Hypnosis Live arm and no Brainwave Shots arm in the published design.

The speed result helps the story because it makes us ask what else the classic “50 percent missed it” narrative omits: instructions, similarity of objects, what participants ignored, and the temporal structure of the scene.

### What gets ignored can be as important as what gets attended

A separate 2023 paper examined similarity among the attended items, ignored items and unexpected item in a sustained inattentional-blindness task. The authors described three experiments, with sample sizes of 813, 610 and 1,044. They found an important role for similarity to both attended and ignored items, with the relationship to ignored items especially consequential in some conditions. The paper does not establish that every visual cue has the same effect; it refines how selection and suppression may work in those tasks. [Study and methods](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13414-023-02794-2).

Why does that matter? Because a broad story about “your brain filtering reality” can sound like an unchangeable personality flaw. A closer reading shows manipulable experimental conditions. A change in what participants are asked to attend to, what they are instructed to ignore, or how an unexpected object moves can alter the chance of report. The effect is not a character judgment.

The strongest practical reading is modest: attention is selective and dependent on task demands. When the cost of overlooking something is high, environments should use dependable checks, redundant signals and clear procedures rather than relying on an individual's confidence that they would surely notice.

### A twelve-minute demonstration that isn't a sales test

You can try a small, no-cost demonstration without copying an experimental video or claiming to reproduce a controlled study.

Choose a short page of unfamiliar, non-sensitive text. Give yourself one minute to scan it for a specific kind of detail, such as dates. Close the page and write down the dates you found. Then reopen it and note one different kind of information, such as place names, that you did not originally seek. Finally, spend the remaining time identifying which details were absent from your first notes because you were pursuing a different question.

Record your initial instruction, what you missed, and what you learned after changing the task. Avoid declaring an accuracy percentage unless you specify a real denominator and consistent scoring. This is an informal observation about your own search strategy, **not** an assessment of cognitive impairment, a validated measure of awareness or a test of an audio product.

### A long history of persuasive reports

In the 1780s, Amand-Marie-Jacques de Chastenet, Marquis de Puységur, assembled accounts of what he described as magnetic somnambulism. His files include reported speech, predictions, witnesses, failed cures and other elements intended to make a remarkable system credible. [Final Vestige's Puységur primary-source archive](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) carefully separates what the documents say from what modern evidence would establish.

The comparison is methodological, not an assertion that Puységur anticipated cognitive psychology or that animal magnetism was scientifically validated. In both cases an observer's confidence is one kind of evidence, while an independently controlled comparison is another. The history of suggestion tells us why claims can feel persuasive; it cannot certify modern hypnosis tracks.

The 1784 Franklin commission's encounter with animal magnetism belongs in this history as another example of testing explanations against controls. ### What the gorilla can still teach us

The most interesting thing about the gorilla is not that viewers failed a trick. It is that a well-defined task produced a surprising limitation, and subsequent well-defined tasks revealed where that limitation bends. The science got better when researchers resisted turning an unforgettable image into an absolute.

Our eyes do not need a miracle to remain useful. Our explanations need limits. The first question is what the experiment measured. The next is what it did not measure. Only then does a practical recommendation deserve a place beside the evidence.
