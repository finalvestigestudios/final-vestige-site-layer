The Syllable That Wasn't Spoken

When a voice says one thing and a face another, some listeners hear a third. The story of a 1976 experiment—and why its most famous number needs a footnote.

Primary investigation: McGurk & MacDonald, Nature (1976). Peer-reviewed later studies and methodological objections appear below. This is not a live audiovisual demonstration.

The sound was ba. The answer was da.

A short film shows a person's mouth forming a syllable. A speaker plays a voice. For a moment, the two disagree. The lips make the movements associated with ga; the sound is ba. Asked what they heard, some viewers say da.

There was no da in the soundtrack.

That is the disquieting observation behind a 1976 report by psychologists Harry McGurk and John MacDonald. The article appeared in Nature under the deceptively simple title “Hearing Lips and Seeing Voices.” Its finding challenged a comfortable assumption: the act of hearing speech is not sealed off from the face in front of us. Vision can influence what a listener reports as a sound, even when the audio signal has not changed. [1]

There is an important difference between saying vision can influence a report and saying every brain will hear the same illusion. The first proposition is supported by decades of research. The second is false. Understanding that difference turns a viral party trick into a useful lesson in evidence.

A researcher, a film, and an unexpected answer

McGurk and MacDonald were studying how children make sense of visible and audible speech. Their laboratory needed controlled audiovisual material: filmed faces and separately recorded voices. In that setting, a mismatch between what the mouth showed and what the soundtrack supplied was more than a production error. It revealed how strongly seeing the speaker could change the experience of hearing them.

The original report described a filmed articulation visually corresponding to /ga/ dubbed with auditory /ba/. Adults commonly reported a third consonant, /da/. Reverse the pairing and the effect was not merely a mirror image; many adults instead reported combinations such as /bagba/ or /gaba/. The precise pairing matters. It is misleading to tell readers that any conflicting face and voice will produce the same new sound. [1]

The researchers used controls in which subjects heard the soundtrack without the incongruent face or watched untreated audiovisual speech. Those controls were essential: they helped establish that the unusual report belonged to the mismatch, not simply to confusion about the spoken syllable in ordinary listening. In the original article's adult replication data, 98 percent of subjects gave a fused response for a particular /ba/-voice, /ga/-lips presentation. That is a striking result for that stimulus and procedure—not an estimate that 98 percent of all people will see any internet McGurk video and hear /da/. [1, 2]

The name “McGurk effect” subsequently stuck. But the label sometimes hides what made the finding scientifically interesting: the audiovisual combination is the independent variable, while the reported syllable is the observation. That gap is where a hypothesis can be tested.

What does the eye contribute to a word?

We seldom listen to a human voice as if it were a radio in a dark room. The movement of lips, jaw, cheeks and tongue offers clues about speech. In noisy surroundings, seeing someone's mouth often helps a listener identify what they are saying. The McGurk effect is one especially vivid case where the visual clue is deliberately put at odds with the sound.

The ordinary language “your eyes trick your ears” is memorable but incomplete. A perceptual system that combines multiple signals is doing something useful most of the time. When a face and voice normally come from the same person, combining them can improve understanding. The laboratory illusion deliberately breaks that everyday relationship. It does not show that one sense is always dominant, that perception is arbitrary, or that a person has lost control of their hearing.

The distinction is worth carrying into the record of any witness. A person can sincerely describe an experience while misidentifying the physical information that produced it. That does not make the person dishonest. Nor does an impressive subjective report, on its own, establish the proposed cause. The relevant question becomes: what changed in the stimulus, what stayed fixed, and which observations could a second investigator reproduce?

The famous number and the people who never hear it

In 2015, researchers studying 360 people across two experiments reported substantial variability. Some subjects almost never experienced a fusion; others did so nearly every time. Different stimulus clips also produced markedly different proportions of fusion reports. A study using open responses rather than forced-choice options found that response format itself made a measurable difference. [3]

This matters for every video maker who promises “You will hear something that isn't there.” The viewer may not. Their failure to experience the effect would not show they are inattentive, unusually rational, neurologically different in any clinically meaningful way, or somehow resistant to suggestion. It may be the chosen voice, the talker, the screen, the task, the language background, the response categories—or a real difference in audiovisual perception.

In 2018, Brown and colleagues examined which traits predicted susceptibility. They found an association with a fine-grained lipreading skill—extracting information about place of articulation. They did not find corresponding associations with their measured attentional control, processing speed, working memory or auditory perceptual gradiency tasks. The result is a good warning against turning a speech illusion into a crude “focus score.” [4]

Research on Mandarin-speaking twin pairs found both substantial differences among individuals and some stability over time. Shared factors accounted for only a small share of the variation. Even closely related people need not report the same third syllable. [5]

The argument over what the illusion measures

For many years, McGurk responses became a convenient experimental shorthand for audiovisual speech integration. But an experimental measure is not automatically a transparent window into one underlying mechanism.

Getz and Toscano revisited the effect in 2021. Across task and stimulus variations they observed fusion responses ranging from 10 to 60 percent with the same audio and visual information. Changing which answers were offered and how participants were questioned could change the result. Their criticism was not that visual information never matters in speech. It was that this particular illusion is too context-sensitive to carry every theoretical claim placed upon it. [6]

A 2023 paper advanced an even more provocative alternative: some responses conventionally described as integration may reflect how the auditory system categorizes ambiguous sounds under the experimental setup. That is an argument about mechanism, not proof that the 1976 observation was imaginary. It belongs beside the stronger integration interpretation, not beneath it as an inconvenient footnote. [7]

The mature conclusion is narrower and more useful than the viral one. Audiovisual context can alter speech reports. The size, reliability and causal explanation of a specific McGurk response depend on participants, stimuli and tasks. Researchers still disagree about what the classic paradigm best measures.

Why an eighteenth-century archive belongs beside a 1976 experiment

In the historical files assembled around Armand-Marie-Jacques de Chastenet, Marquis de Puységur, visitors encounter reports of altered awareness, remembered and forgotten conversations, predictions, claimed cures, statements from witnesses and retrospective interpretation. Final Vestige's Animal Magnetism Files tracks 153 active source rows and 16 case dossiers. The project explicitly retains failed predictions, failed cures and disagreements rather than writing them out of the story. [8]

These archives are not a nineteenth-century preprint of the McGurk effect. We cannot replay an eighteenth-century conversation with alternate synchronized soundtracks or assign the same participant to randomized sensory conditions. It would be irresponsible to claim McGurk's lab demonstrates the cause of every report of magnetic somnambulism. It would be equally mistaken to treat an extraordinary report as proof that Puységur's explanation was correct.

The connection is methodological. In both settings, one must separate the experience reported, the physical circumstances recorded, the explanation proposed and the evidence capable of discriminating among explanations. An honest archive keeps those layers visible. One telling historical term may have multiple meanings across translation; one sincere witness may not know what influenced a response. Neither observation licenses a diagnosis of the witness.

A responsible demonstration you can actually try

For a proper demonstration you need permission to use a source video's audio and visuals, or your own synchronized recordings. Find a research/teaching clip clearly labeled with its audio and visual syllables, then use headphones only if appropriate for the clip. First listen with the face hidden, and write the syllable you hear. Then replay with the face visible and record what you perceive without choosing from a list if possible. Finally, close your eyes again.

Do not edit the underlying soundtrack between conditions. Do not pretend that captions reading “BA,” “GA,” or “DA” are equivalent to real filmed articulation. A silent graphic of letters can explain the experiment, but it cannot demonstrate the McGurk effect.

Record both a changed answer and an unchanged answer. Either is worth noticing. One person's three replays are not an experiment with blinded participants and controlled presentation; they are a guided observation that can lead you to the real paper. It is also an accessibility question: a hearing-impaired visitor, someone who cannot see the face, and a visitor without sound should all be able to understand the scientific claim from the transcript even if the stimulus cannot be experienced.

Our original forty-second explainer deliberately uses text and schematic imagery, not a fake lips-and-audio test. It tells you what the researchers tested, and what they did not show.

What it does not prove about mind-training audio

There is a tempting jump from “two senses can interact” to “a particular commercial sound can program your brain.” The McGurk research does not bridge it. Its experiments concern audiovisual speech perception, not meditation quality, clinical stress relief, treatment, wealth, suggestibility training or the efficacy of a packaged recording.

Likewise, studies of binaural beats investigate a different phenomenon. A 2023 systematic review examined fourteen EEG studies of binaural-beat stimulation and found inconsistent outcomes: five aligned with the entrainment hypothesis, eight contradicted it, and one was mixed. The review did not test any named commercial meditation recording. Even definitive evidence for a generic binaural-beat effect would not automatically validate a particular seller's claim that twelve minutes equals an hour of meditation. [9]

Readers deserve both things: a serious account of an unusual perception and freedom from a retail promise smuggled into the conclusion.

The answer, and the unanswered question

The soundtrack did not contain the syllable da. Under the right audiovisual conditions, some listeners nevertheless reported it. That gap is not a defect in the participants' honesty; it is a chance to learn how a complex perceptual judgment is made.

But the more consequential lesson may be what happened afterward. The extraordinary demonstration became popular; a wonderfully portable number began circulating; and later scientists had to remind everyone that the particular participant, recording and question mattered.

When perception and a record disagree, the way forward is not to pick whichever story feels strongest. Preserve the original materials. State the conditions. Include the people for whom the illusion failed. And let a result become no larger than the evidence that produced it.
