# The Choice You Didn't Make: When an Answer Is Swapped and a Reason Appears

*Final Vestige Mind & Perception Archive · original editorial manuscript · source-only draft · November 16, 2026*

A shopper tastes two jams, chooses one, and is handed the other. When asked why that flavor won, some shoppers offer a perfectly intelligible explanation. The problem is that the thing being explained is not the thing chosen. This is not a parable about gullibility. It is an experimental result that poses a hard question for anybody who works with testimony, evidence—or persuasion.

The phenomenon is called **choice blindness**. It describes a failure, under specific experimental conditions, to notice a mismatch between a decision and the result apparently returned to the decision-maker. Not everyone misses the mismatch. Nor does it mean that preferences are meaningless. It shows that a confidently offered *reason* does not always preserve the history of the choice that preceded it.

## The photograph that changed hands

In 2005, Petter Johansson, Lars Hall, Sverker Sikström and Andreas Olsson published “Failure to Detect Mismatches Between Intention and Outcome in a Simple Decision Task” in *Science*. Participants compared two photographs of faces and chose the more attractive one. On some trials a sleight-of-hand procedure substituted the unchosen face when the experimenter presented the purported result. Participants then explained why they had chosen the face now in front of them.

The experiment deliberately split three things that ordinarily seem continuous: the original intention, the experienced outcome and the later explanation. Many substituted outcomes went undetected. People could give reasons for the substituted face as though it were the face they had selected. A person may sincerely describe what seems to be their decision while the record of what they actually did tells a different story.

It is important not to turn that into a stronger claim than the evidence supports. Some participants noticed. The task involved pairs of photographs and a covert physical manipulation. It did not show that every personal preference is an invention, that a buyer can be made to believe any sales pitch, or that people lack the capacity to choose. The authors demonstrated a detectable mismatch under a designed set of conditions.

Primary source: Johansson et al., *Science* 310 (2005), 116–119, DOI https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1111709 ; PubMed https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16210542/ . The abstract reports both failure to notice switches and explanations of the returned choice, without testing commercial products.

## Jam, tea, and the explanation that followed

In 2010, the same line of research moved into a supermarket. Lars Hall and colleagues invited passing shoppers to taste pairs of jams and teas, choose what they preferred, then sample their chosen item again and explain that preference. On manipulated trials researchers covertly swapped the content of the samples so the supposed winner was actually the rejected alternative.

In the report's abstract, **no more than one third of the manipulated trials were detected** overall. Even distinctly different flavors and smells did not always result in recognition of the swap. People were not necessarily unable to distinguish the tastes. Rather, the sequence from selection to returned item to verbal justification sometimes came apart. A coherent reason could describe an unchosen result.

This is striking partly because nothing mystical is required. It is not a demonstration of hypnosis, clairvoyance, audio entrainment, or machine control. It is a simple disruption in what a person believes is a continuous process of choosing and reporting. Yet the marketplace is exactly where a bad reading could be weaponized. A marketer might be tempted to suggest that any preference can be planted. The study does not establish that. Its effect was generated by a hidden switch, in a defined task, with an investigator present.

Source: Hall, Johansson, Tärning, Sikström and Deutgen, “Magic at the Marketplace: Choice Blindness for the Taste of Jam and the Smell of Tea,” *Cognition* 117 (2010), 54–61, DOI https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2010.06.010 ; PubMed abstract https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20637455/ .

## A survey that rewrote its own answer

A 2012 study explored a related question using a self-transforming paper survey. Participants recorded views on moral issues; researchers manipulated some returned answers so they appeared to endorse the opposite position. The study reported that **69% of participants failed to detect at least one of two reversals**. Some also supplied reasons for the reversed stance.

This is a finding about detection and argument in that specific experimental procedure. It is not a finding that 69% of people permanently changed their ethical commitments. A rewritten answer on a form is not equivalent to a real change of belief, and a subject's effort to explain an apparently authentic answer is not proof of long-term persuasion. The researchers used the mismatch to examine self-attribution and post-hoc rationalization.

Source: Hall, Johansson and Strandberg, “Lifting the Veil of Morality,” *PLOS ONE* 7 (2012), e45457, DOI https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0045457 ; institutional record https://www.lunduniversity.lu.se/publication/261789cd-c588-4dbd-afeb-f708fae90c52 .

## Three blindnesses, three different questions

Choice blindness is often mixed up with two other experiments that Final Vestige studies.

**Change blindness** concerns noticing changes between visual presentations or during a short interruption. In the famous door-swap experiment, a person giving directions could miss the replacement of their conversation partner behind a door. The first observation and second observation differ, and the change is not recognized. Final Vestige's published analysis explains the small samples and experimental limitations: https://finalvestige.com/2026/11/02/change-blindness/ .

**Inattentional blindness** concerns failing to notice an unexpected event while attending to another task. A classic demonstration asks observers to count passes and measures whether they notice something unexpected in the scene. No previously chosen result must be substituted for inattentional blindness to occur.

**Choice blindness** instead asks whether someone recognizes that what they are given as their decision result is not the same as what they selected. The second part—how someone reasons about that result—is essential.

The distinctions matter for the same reason source labels matter in an archive. A witness might fail to see an event, overlook a change, remember a decision inaccurately, or explain an outcome that was replaced. Those are not interchangeable failures. Neither are the interventions or conclusions that might follow from them.

## Why a compelling reason is not the same as evidence

When a participant offers a reason for an unchosen photograph, the explanation may be honest. The experiment does not require participants to lie. It requires the investigator to break the connection between the initial action and the feedback that follows. The resulting explanation is an artifact of that broken connection.

A historian faces a similar structural problem when a document tells us not merely what happened but what the event meant. Did the recorder witness the event? Did the record preserve the exact sequence? Was the result revised? Did the speaker have authority or incentives to shape the reported experience? These questions do not discredit witnesses by default. They establish what their testimony can and cannot show.

That makes choice blindness a useful neighbor to Final Vestige's historical investigations, not a diagnostic label to place on people in the past.

## Puységur and the limits of reported experience

In eighteenth-century France, the Marquis de Puységur built a network of testimony, signed statements and case descriptions around animal magnetism. The [Puységur Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) preserve different editions, witnesses, predictions and failed outcomes. The archive records episodes involving subjects who were dependent, frightened or subject to operator authority. It also retains cases in which apparent cooperation clashes with wishes stated in ordinary or altered states, and the author's own warning about pressure and compliant responses.

There is **no basis here to claim Puységur produced the modern choice-blindness effect**. The eighteenth-century records are not randomized trials. Their meaning is more basic: a signed or witnessed statement may establish the provenance of a statement without establishing the mechanism proposed to explain it. An operator may both sincerely document what happened and supply a theory the documentation does not prove.

The [Franklin commission investigation](https://finalvestige.com/2026/11/02/mesmerism/) provides another, different historical comparison. The commissioners tested magnetic-fluid claims through concealed or sham treatment, distinguishing expectation and reported effects. They examined Deslon's practice rather than an abstract universal theory. Their result is evidence about a claimed mechanism, not a demonstration of choice blindness. The shared value of these cases is methodological restraint.

## One useful exercise that doesn't deceive anyone

A safe reflection exercise can help protect the record of your own choices. It is **not a validated test for choice blindness**, because it neither uses concealed switches nor can reproduce the research protocol.

Pick an ordinary low-stakes decision: which article to read, which of two tasks to start, or whether to try quiet time rather than a packaged audio. **Before you read the recommendation**, write down the alternatives, your preferred option, two specific reasons, and what new information would actually change your mind.

Read another source for four minutes. Then, without looking at the note, write down what you remember choosing and why. Compare the original record to the new explanation. A mismatch might reflect ordinary forgetting, new evidence or a reframing of the task; you cannot infer a lab effect from it. No mismatch is also an entirely reasonable result. This is a way of keeping a decision trace, not a measure of your cognitive powers.

Do not perform a covert switch on another person. Do not collect personal medical or financial decisions to demonstrate the point. The experiment belongs in consented research, not in interpersonal deception or consumer manipulation.

## The uncomfortable ethics of selling a mental tool

A seller who knows that readers can attach confident explanations to outcomes has an obvious, unethical shortcut: tell people what they must already want, show a dramatic claim, and leave the terms ambiguous until after commitment. An evidence-based publisher should do the opposite.

This becomes especially important when the offer is about meditation, focus or suggestion. Claims about brainwaves can sound scientific even when the evidence concerns different stimuli, different people, or different outcomes. Whether a particular audio contains one frequency or another does not independently demonstrate that buyers experience its advertised benefit. A supplier's use of “scientifically proven” is not a substitute for data about the specific product.

Official Inspire3 (https://inspire3.com/) lists Zen12 and other mind-oriented products. Zen12's exact public sales page (https://zen12.com/) promotes twelve-minute listening sessions and makes a comparison to an hour of meditation. The public order page (https://zen12.com/order) describes a full program containing twelve levels and forty-eight MP3s. These are seller descriptions; the efficacy comparison is **not independently established by the choice-blindness research**. Different offers or checkout paths may package the material differently.

Hypnosis Live's Total Focus page (https://hypnosislive.com/mental-skills/total-focus) describes a separate thirty-three-minute, 47 MB recording. Brainwave Shots' Focus Shot page (https://brainwaveshots.com/focus-shot) describes fifteen- and thirty-minute recordings. Their focus, safety, duration-of-effect and “brain reprogramming” promises are **seller claims**, not independent proof of product-specific results.

The distinctions matter more than any clever headline: a consumer can choose an enjoyable audio experience for its format, pacing or convenience without having to believe extraordinary health, productivity or neurological promises.

The public catalog and consumer marketing emails do **not** authenticate Final Vestige-specific affiliate acceptance, approved Zen12 channel/SKU scope, an issued earning URL, exact referred price, returns or actual settlement. ## Five questions to ask before the price

A buyer can compare the actual product and their original reason for wanting it without any promise of miraculous focus.

1. **What exactly is delivered?** Is this the full twelve-level, forty-eight-file package, another tier, or a limited promotion? Does the offer describe playback formats and access clearly?
2. **What is the desired outcome?** A pleasant, guided twelve-minute listening session is not the same claim as clinically significant improvement in anxiety, memory or sustained attention.
3. **Which evidence tests that outcome for this product?** A study about decision illusions cannot validate Zen12. Research on meditation in general may still leave a product-specific gap.
4. **What are the exact checkout and refund conditions?** Compare the final referred package, price and exclusions, not only a promotional banner. Avoid any invented commission or savings figure.
5. **What would you do without buying?** A simple timer, a notebook, or the free Focus Fieldbook may answer the user's immediate need. That is a legitimate competitor, not a failure of a sales funnel.

Those questions may lead someone to buy, wait, or do nothing. A high-quality conversion is a legitimate informed transaction, not the maximum number of impulsive outbound clicks.

## The record should survive the explanation

In the experiments a face was switched, a jam was switched, and a survey answer was reversed. The surprising result was that the explanation sometimes continued as if no switch had taken place. Science made that discontinuity visible.

For the historian, the test is whether the original evidence survives the story imposed on it. For a consumer, the test is whether the chosen offer still matches the reasons written down before the page began persuading them. Both are exercises in protecting an original record.

**The sound of confidence is not a substitute for what was actually chosen.**
