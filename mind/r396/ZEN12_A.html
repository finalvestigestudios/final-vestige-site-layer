Before you buy a meditation audio, try this attention test.

A gorilla crossed a basketball scene. People concentrating on passes sometimes failed to report it. That is not proof that a recording will improve your focus. It is an invitation to ask better questions before paying for one.

A source-backed micro-story

A visitor in plain sight

In 1999, Simons and Chabris published an experiment showing that selective attention can leave a fully visible unexpected event unnoticed. In 2013, Drew and colleagues studied 24 radiologists searching for lung nodules; 83% failed to report an unexpected gorilla-shaped insertion in the research image. These observations depend on specific tasks and do not establish that trained professionals generally overlook pathology, or that meditation products fix perception.

Read the original research: 1999 experiment and 2013 expert-observer experiment. Both links lead to scientific sources, not merchants.

Try a useful free twelve-minute alternative

Pick a page with several dates, people and qualifying notes. Spend three minutes searching only for dates. Then three minutes searching for qualifiers or exceptions. Use another four minutes comparing which details each goal revealed. Finish with two minutes stating what your first search proved—and what it did not. This is an original source-checking exercise, not a validated test, therapy or claim of improved performance.

What science can—and cannot—tell you

Supported research observation

Concentrating on a visual task can reduce reports of an unexpected visible event. This result does not give a universal rate at which all people miss events.

Not established by this research

A twelve-minute audio recording is equivalent to an hour of meditation; a specific commercial listening program cures stress or improves attention; a purchaser will experience a guaranteed result.

A free experiment cannot replace independent product-specific controlled studies. This guide has not tested Zen12 or administered a blind comparison.

Five questions worth asking first

  1. Do I want a structured listening routine, or primarily evidence of a specific cognitive effect?
  2. Can I listen to an adequate free sample before paying, and do I understand the actual program contents?
  3. Is the price shown at the genuine checkout the one I expected, and what taxes or charges appear?
  4. What conditions, deadlines or contacts govern the seller's refund promise?
  5. Would a free twelve-minute observation habit serve my goal before any purchase?
