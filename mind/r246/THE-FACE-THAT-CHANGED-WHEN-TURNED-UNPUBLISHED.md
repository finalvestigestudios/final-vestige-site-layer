# The Face That Changed When Turned
## How a cut-up election poster became an experiment in what we think we see

**FINAL VESTIGE STUDIOS / THE MIND & PERCEPTION ARCHIVE / INVESTIGATION / UNPUBLISHED — EDITORIAL REVIEW REQUIRED**

**Evidence status:** Original narrative synthesis of the linked publications; no original human-subject research, product trial, or licensed reproduction of Thompson's photograph. **No affiliate endorsements or commercial calls to action.**

A face lies upside down on the floor. The eyes are in the right place, roughly. The smile seems plausible enough. Someone might notice an oddness, but there is nothing quite like the shock that arrives when the picture is rotated upright. Suddenly the mouth has become a grimace and the eyes seem to belong to a different creature. Yet the pixels inside those features have not changed during the rotation.

This is a good beginning for a mystery because the apparent transformation happens entirely in the observer. It is also an unusually honest psychological experiment: the investigator can show the evidence, turn it around, and ask whether the observer was ever receiving the whole story.

The experiment became known as the **Thatcher illusion**. Its name belongs to the British prime minister whose election portrait was modified by the visual scientist Peter Thompson in 1980. But its real subject is an inference we make thousands of times a day: that seeing the individual pieces of a scene amounts to understanding the relationships between them. [1, 2]

## A razor blade, two posters and an accident

Thompson's later first-person account is more interesting than the mythology that sometimes gathers around famous illusions. In a 2009 retrospective, he recalled preparing a demonstration for engineering students at the University of York. The initial idea arose not from a theory of politics or manipulation, but from a boiler-room window: patterned glass made a nearby picture visibly distorted while leaving a recognizable image when viewed from farther away. Thompson wanted a classroom illustration of changes in spatial-frequency information. [2]

He obtained two posters of the newly elected Margaret Thatcher, cut out the eyes and mouth of one, turned those features upside down, and fitted them back into the face. Upright, the altered poster looked grotesque. His intended demonstration was about how a familiar image might still be identifiable even after some detail had been perturbed.

Then an ordinary trip out of the room did the work that his experimental plan had not anticipated. The two posters were lying upside down as he approached them on the floor. The distortion that had been so obvious appeared unexpectedly tame. He had changed neither the prints nor their features. He had changed the *orientation of the whole face relative to the viewer*. [2]

The original 1980 note, reprinted as an appendix in Thompson's 2009 anniversary paper, was brief. Decades of follow-up work have not made the effect less strange. They have made the question behind it much sharper: which properties of a face do we use when we see it as a *face*, rather than as an inventory of eyes, nose and mouth? [1, 2]

There is an editorial constraint worth emphasizing. The famous Thatcher photographs have a copyright and publication history. They are not public-domain clip art simply because the experiment is old. Final Vestige should use an **original, rights-cleared diagram** to explain the principle, or reproduce an archival image only with documented permission. ## What actually turns upside down?

The canonical trick contains two levels of rotation. In the manipulated image, the eye regions and mouth region have been rotated 180 degrees *within* an otherwise upright face. That produces a conspicuously strange expression. Now rotate the *whole manipulated picture* 180 degrees. The result is an upside-down face with locally upright eye and mouth regions. Many observers find the manipulation much less objectionable in this position. Set it beside an unaltered inverted picture and the difference can be surprisingly hard to spot. [1, 2]

A casual description might say that the brain fails to notice the eyes. That is not the experimental conclusion. The eyes are still present and their pixels are processed. What changes is the ease with which the observer registers the arrangement as a violation of normal facial structure. Seeing individual features and processing their configuration are related but different operations.

Faces are familiar objects with unusually consistent layouts. Two eyes sit above a nose; a mouth sits beneath. Our ability to recognize people and read expressions involves information about local features, their orientation, and their relationships. Researchers often call some of this **configural** or **holistic** processing. Those terms are useful, but they do not describe one single magic switch. The exact result also depends on whether an experiment asks someone to notice an uncanny expression, identify the person, discriminate two images, or judge emotion. [3, 4]

Thompson's accident provides a vivid illustration of that distinction. It does not prove that upside-down faces are invisible, that all facial features are ignored, or that the brain completely stops processing an inverted image. The question is not whether something was *seen at all*, but what its configuration meant to the observer.

## The experiment became harder to explain

In 2003, Luc Boutsen and Glyn Humphreys reported four experiments comparing ordinary and Thatcherized faces. Some tasks used isolated face parts; others used whole faces. Some comparisons involved the same person's face, others different people's faces. This variation mattered because observers could sometimes solve a matching task by comparing surface features without needing the configuration strategies that face recognition often uses. [3]

Their conclusion was not simply that inverted faces always produce bad performance. They argued that inversion disrupts **local configural processing** more than the ability to process image features, and that the Thatcher manipulation itself disrupts configural processing in upright faces. The surrounding face can help or hinder attention to wrongly oriented parts depending on the matching task. [3]

Another challenge appeared when experimenters asked about facial *expression*, rather than only which picture seemed grotesque. In work published in 2014, Lilia Psalta and Timothy Andrews reported that turning a normal face upside down caused only a relatively small reduction in expression recognition in their tasks. By contrast, rotating the eyes and mouth within an upright face disrupted it substantially. Paradoxically, inverting the entire Thatcherized face could improve expression recognition relative to the upright Thatcherized version. [5]

The result sounds contradictory only if we assume that recognizing an expression and reacting to a grotesque face are one measurement. They are not. The study argues for distinct processing contributions, including an orientation-sensitive representation involved in the perceptual oddity and comparatively orientation-insensitive information that can support expression recognition. It would be inaccurate to tell viewers that inversion simply switches all face perception off. [5]

The Thatcher illusion therefore became a research tool, not a slogan. When a visual result changes with the exact task, the problem is not that science has failed. The experiment has begun to isolate competing explanations.

## What a brain scanner does and does not tell us

A separate investigation by Psalta and colleagues used functional MRI adaptation while participants encountered normal and Thatcherized faces. It appeared online in November 2013 and in *Psychological Science*'s January 2014 issue. One result concerned the **superior temporal sulcus**, a region implicated in processing facial expressions. Responses to changed facial configurations differed when faces were upright; the corresponding sensitivity to the Thatcher manipulation was not evident in that region for inverted faces. [4]

In contrast, the investigators reported a different pattern in the **fusiform face area**, with sensitivity to some image changes even in the inverted configuration. That contrast is a stronger finding than the usual popular claim that one hemisphere of the brain is fooled while another is not. Neither scanner region is a tiny switch labeled "correct face" or "wrong face." BOLD responses are indirect measures, and an adaptation contrast is not a recording of a person's thoughts. [4]

The investigators' own interpretation was that expression-sensitive processing has a pronounced dependence on orientation. Their design does not measure whether a listener to a commercial sound recording can "reprogram" face perception. It does not establish that concentration practice makes a person immune to the illusion.

It also helps explain why an effect can be simultaneously **real** and **limited**. The illusion survives many settings. Its explanation is still refined by the distinctions between identity, expression, parts and whole, and by the procedures used to ask an observer a question.

## Could a six-month-old see the difference?

A further test of any easy explanation is development. If the Thatcher effect were solely a trick learned from years of viewing adult faces, infants might be expected to behave differently.

In 2004, Evelin Bertin and Ramesh Bhatt studied six-month-old infants with upright and inverted Thatcherized faces. The published report describes discrimination of altered and unaltered faces in the upright orientation but not in the inverted orientation. This supports the idea that sensitivity to some relational properties of upright faces is present early in development. [6]

A study of newborns published in 2009 reported an analogous upright-versus-inverted distinction under its own habituation procedure. It is tempting to translate these results into a grand story about an inborn face module. The evidence supports a narrower claim: **under these experimental procedures**, very young observers showed differential discrimination associated with orientation. An infant's looking-time behavior is not an adult's report of a face becoming grotesque; the tasks should not be conflated. [7]

There is also an important observation about the work of explanation. Infant research can challenge simplistic accounts based only on lengthy visual experience without demonstrating that every aspect of adult face processing is present at birth. The brain is developing, and the methods ask different questions.

## The monkey result that did not stay settled

In 2009, Adachi, Chou and Hampton reported a Thatcher effect in rhesus monkeys, interpreting it as evidence for similarities in face processing across primates. The study used a nonverbal experimental approach, because no monkey can report that a portrait looks grotesque in the language of a psychology student. [8]

Then another research group reported a more complicated pattern in 2013. With computerized discrimination tasks, chimpanzees showed an effect while rhesus monkeys did not, and the authors performed additional experiments to examine the disagreement with earlier rhesus findings. [9]

Both results belong in the record. There is no responsible reason to erase the study that complicates a clean evolutionary claim. Animal species, stimulus faces, training histories, presentation conditions and response measures all make a difference. A viral caption saying "all primates see exactly the same illusion" would go further than the evidence allows.

This is an especially useful case study in Final Vestige's method. A credible investigation doesn't hide the conflicting record simply because the first result makes a more satisfying headline.

## A small experiment that doesn't pretend to be a clinical test

An original demonstration can be made with a rights-cleared illustrated face, but it should be described as an **informal classroom observation**, not a replicated laboratory experiment.

First, take one clearly drawn upright face and make a duplicate. In the duplicate, rotate the eye shapes and the mouth shape independently by 180 degrees while leaving the outline and nose untouched. Do not alter color, size, contrast, or expression between the two except for those internal rotations. Save the two images, and keep the source files as an audit trail.

Second, compare both drawings upright. Write down what you notice *before* reading an explanation. Now rotate each complete image, including its outline, upside down. Compare again without moving their relative positions. Note whether it becomes easier or harder to spot which was manipulated, and whether the feeling of oddness changes.

Finally, put the images upright again. Observe the shift; don't infer that your eye "missed" an object or that you possess a diagnostic condition. One viewer, one illustration and no randomized presentation provide no effect-size estimate. A real experiment would need controlled stimuli, multiple participants, order balancing, pre-specified measures and documented analysis.

For accessibility, a site demonstration must not make the manipulated picture the sole means of learning the result. It needs a textual explanation of what was changed, a fully descriptive caption and a non-moving, no-JavaScript alternative. Avoid rapid flashing or forced animation. The original historical photograph is not necessary to understand the experiment.

## The point where marketing starts making claims the science did not

Why is a perception illusion on a website that also reviews meditation and hypnosis audio? Because both belong to the same ethical question: **how do we distinguish a genuine observed effect from an explanation or promise layered on top of it?**

In a laboratory, changing orientation can predictably alter perception under controlled conditions. The evidence says something about face processing. It does **not** show that a recording can create "super focus," cure a disorder, remove all cognitive biases, or guarantee a state of mental mastery. Those would be different interventions and different outcomes requiring their own tests.

The official Inspire3 catalog advertises Zen12, Brainwave Shots and Hypnosis Live as self-improvement products; the exact offers describe particular audio formats and various benefits. They are **seller descriptions**, not independent demonstrations of product-specific effectiveness. [10] General research into attention and perception should never be cited as if it were a controlled trial of those products.

Someone who came here from a short video about the Thatcher illusion deserves the original research first. A free observation worksheet or the existing Final Vestige Focus Fieldbook may be a useful *optional* next stop, but neither should be presented as a treatment or as the same intervention used in the cited studies. If a separate purchasing guide is offered later, it belongs in a clearly labeled commercial review, with a disclosure beside any earning link. The Federal Trade Commission's endorsement guidance explains why proximity matters and its health-claims guidance explains why objective benefit claims require substantiation. [11]

The same standard protects Final Vestige. A visitor who wants to buy an audio library should know its actual contents, seller terms, limitations and alternatives—not be promised a brain transformation from an unrelated optical illusion.

## The conclusion that stays when the face is turned again

A poster lay upside down. Its eyes were altered. The scene was fixed, but a person looking at it received a different impression of the whole face. That small surprise gave researchers a way to ask much better questions about local features, facial configuration, expressions and what counts as detecting a change.

The studies that followed did not all produce the same explanation or the same cross-species result. Some sharpened the distinction between processing a grotesque expression and identifying ordinary emotion. Others pushed the effect back into infancy or connected it to orientation-sensitive responses in visual cortex.

That is the value of the illusion. Not proof that sight is a lie. Proof that **a correct inventory of the pieces is not always a correct account of their relationship**.

The records are there for readers to inspect. Turning the face around is only the beginning.
