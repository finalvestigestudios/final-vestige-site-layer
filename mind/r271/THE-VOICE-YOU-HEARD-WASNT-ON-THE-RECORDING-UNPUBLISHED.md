# The Voice You Heard Wasn't on the Recording
**Final Vestige Studios · Mind & Perception · Original investigation · UNPUBLISHED / editorial review required · 10 November 2026**

*An apparent syllable appears in your hearing even when it is absent from the soundtrack. The McGurk effect reveals how seeing a face can alter what we think we hear—but it does not prove that an audio product can alter your mind.*

Imagine a room with a monitor, headphones, and an utterly ordinary recording of someone speaking a single syllable. Nothing is concealed in the audio: play it alone, and it sounds like “ba.” Now switch on the image of the speaker. The lips seem to form a different syllable: “ga.” For some listeners, the sound changes into “da,” even though the soundtrack has not changed.

The remarkable thing is not that somebody has been fooled by an elaborate magic trick. It is that their perceptual system is doing a job it ordinarily performs extremely well. In real conversations we do not receive separate parcels called “sound” and “sight” that remain politely independent. We use every available clue to understand speech amid noise, accents, imperfect recordings, and shifting light. Usually those clues agree. When researchers deliberately make them disagree, the machinery of perception becomes visible.

That is the central observation of the **McGurk effect**, first reported by Harry McGurk and John MacDonald in *Nature* on **23 December 1976**. [1] It is a particularly revealing example of why an honest account of experience—“I heard da”—is not always an accurate account of the physical stimulus—“the tape contained ba.”

## The sound that had not been recorded

McGurk and MacDonald were studying how children perceive speech. They presented audiovisual recordings in which the visible articulation and the soundtrack had been mismatched. The famous example combined an auditory /ba/ with visual /ga/. Adults frequently reported an intermediate percept, often /da/. In a different mismatch, reversing the pairing, listeners could report combinations such as /bagba/ or /gaba/. Those are not interchangeable outcomes; the particular mismatch matters. [1]

A crucial control made the demonstration scientifically important. When participants heard the soundtrack without the conflicting moving lips, they could report its syllables accurately. When audiovisual cues agreed, they also gave the expected reports. The third percept was not secretly embedded in an alternative soundtrack. The visual information was affecting the interpretation of the sound.

This does **not** mean that every human necessarily hears /da/ in that pairing, nor that a listener is permanently changed. It means that under particular stimulus and presentation conditions, the brain's speech-perception system can combine conflicting sensory evidence into a report that differs from either input taken in isolation.

In ordinary life that combination is an advantage. Watching a speaker's mouth can help us resolve speech. During a mismatched research demonstration, the same useful tendency exposes an error. An illusion can reveal the architecture of a functioning system without implying that the system is generally defective.

## Why the eyes have a vote in what the ears hear

We tend to describe hearing as if it happens in the ear and ends there. But recognizing speech requires more than measuring a sound wave. A listener needs to infer which word or syllable is being spoken. The mouth, timing, context, and acoustic signal can all contribute evidence.

A lip shape is not a transcription. Some speech sounds look similar. Lighting, a beard, camera angle, blur, compression, and familiarity with the talker can change what a viewer picks up. The experimental question is therefore more precise than “Are the eyes stronger than the ears?” It asks how the brain responds when visual and acoustic indicators point toward different explanations.

One influential explanation is **causal inference**. A perceiver does not merely average two signals indiscriminately. The system may implicitly estimate whether the sights and sounds came from a single source and should be combined, or from separate sources and should be treated differently. John Magnotti and Michael Beauchamp tested a causal-inference model in a 2017 *PLOS Computational Biology* paper and showed that it could account for responses to incongruent audiovisual speech stimuli, including McGurk-type trials. [2]

A model that explains observed response patterns is not a direct picture of the mind, and several computational accounts can sometimes fit related data. Nonetheless, this work usefully moves the story away from “a video rewrites the ears” toward testable predictions: What happens when sounds and visible articulations differ slightly, dramatically, or ambiguously? When do people integrate them, and when do they separate them?

One way to understand the phenomenon is to imagine an investigator assembling a case from two witnesses. Ordinarily the witnesses agree, and their accounts reinforce one another. If they differ, the investigator must decide whether the discrepancy reflects a single event seen from two angles or two separate events. Speech perception solves a version of that problem rapidly and usually without conscious deliberation. This analogy explains the inference problem; it is not a literal claim that the brain holds a courtroom hearing.

## The illusion that does not work on everyone

An internet demonstration is often presented as a guaranteed mind-blower. The science is more interesting—and less deterministic.

In a 2018 investigation, Violet Brown and colleagues reported that McGurk fusion responses can range from **0% to 100% across individuals in some studies**, even when group-level results reproduce the effect. Their analyses found an association between susceptibility and the ability to recover place-of-articulation information from lip movements. They did **not** find the same predictive relationship for the tested measures of attentional control, processing speed, working-memory capacity, or auditory perceptual gradiency. [3]

That finding is easy to misuse. If the effect is absent in one person, it does not certify exceptional intelligence, immunity to suggestion, or unusually strong attention. If it appears strongly, it is not evidence of gullibility. A perceptual response depends on the stimuli, delivery equipment, sensory information available to that person, and the tasks used to measure it.

Researchers also study how signal quality changes audiovisual integration. Hirst and colleagues' 2018 study tested different age groups and altered visual and auditory clarity. Auditory noise and visual blur influenced the likelihood that seen articulation would affect heard speech, with developmental differences in those thresholds. [4] This matters for any viewer who tries a compressed demonstration on a phone: the device, its volume, the video synchrony, and the listener's environment are not neutral details.

The strongest fair claim is that the phenomenon is a well-established **class of experimental speech-perception responses**. The weakest claim would be that watching an arbitrary short clip proves how every visitor's brain works. Final Vestige should make the former, not the latter.

## The third variable: what happens afterward

The story did not end with a curious 1976 illusion. In 2018, Claudia Lüttke, Alexis Pérez-Bellido and Floris de Lange investigated rapid recalibration of speech perception following the illusion. [5] Their work illustrates that audiovisual experience can influence subsequent speech judgments under experimental conditions.

But there is an important boundary. Short-term adaptation in a constrained phoneme experiment is not evidence for a universal method of “reprogramming the subconscious.” The measurement concerns responses to speech stimuli. It does not test long-term concentration, anxiety, income, intelligence or a particular commercial audio program.

Similarly, the research by Brown and colleagues does not establish the effectiveness of guided meditation. Their result about individual variation in lipreading-related information is a question about audiovisual speech, not an intervention trial.

These distinctions can feel excessively careful until one encounters promotional language that invokes an actual study and then jumps to a product conclusion. Scientific credibility is not transferable in that way. The fact that sound and sight interact does not demonstrate that a specified audio file improves cognitive performance. Establishing that claim would require product-relevant testing with appropriate controls, outcomes, and independent scrutiny.

## A safe demonstration without manipulating anyone's senses

You do not need to play flashing images or turn up a soundtrack to learn from this case. The following paper exercise asks a simpler question: **What evidence would distinguish a sensory illusion from an edited recording?**

Imagine three conditions with the same participant:

1. The participant hears the original /ba/ recording with the monitor off.
2. The participant hears the same recording while seeing lips articulate /ga/.
3. The participant hears the original /ba/ recording with the monitor off again.

Before reading further, write down what each condition would need to show to support the claim that visual information changes perceived speech. What would rule out the possibility that the audio track was quietly swapped? Would every participant have to report /da/ to establish an average effect?

Then make the control more demanding. Ask for the original audio file, its waveform, the video frames, their synchronization, the order of conditions, the exact question asked, and the denominator of participants tested. If only the surprising trials are published in a montage, how would you know the rate of the effect?

The value of this exercise is not in discovering an illusion inside your own hearing. It is in learning how an investigator separates **stimulus**, **experience**, and **interpretation**. An observation can be sincere, an experimental effect real, and a careless explanation still wrong.

This is also a useful habit when encountering social videos. A striking clip can point viewers toward a fascinating paper; it should not be treated as a clinical or personality test. Viewers who are Deaf, hard of hearing, or unable to see lip movement may have entirely different access to a stimulus. A source-led text explanation should be complete without requiring video or sound.

## A historical echo, and a major difference

In the eighteenth century, Armand-Marie-Jacques de Chastenet, Marquis de Puységur, recorded accounts associated with animal magnetism and what he called magnetic somnambulism. The preserved material interests historians not because every extraordinary reported effect is accepted as established, but because it preserves how participants and witnesses described unusual experiences within a particular framework of expectations and authority.

Final Vestige's published **[Puységur's Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/)** examines those accounts, witness evidence, consent, and the problem of proof. A historical actor's report is evidence that the report was made. Whether the mechanism they proposed existed is a separate question.

It would be historically and scientifically wrong to equate Puységur's eighteenth-century practices with the McGurk effect, or to suggest that the latter validates animal magnetism. What connects the two investigations is a **methodological discipline**: experiences deserve careful recording, while explanations require independent tests.

By 1976, researchers could deliberately isolate two sensory inputs, exchange them, and test how response patterns changed. That experimental control is exactly what distinguishes a modern perceptual account from a collection of extraordinary testimonials.

## What the silent soundtrack teaches

The recording stays the same. The perceived syllable can change. That asymmetry is the entire surprise.

The McGurk effect does not reveal that perception is a lie. It reveals that perception is an inference, often assembled from sources we barely notice. The responsible reaction is not to distrust every sensation, but to ask which observation came from which source and what would happen if one cue were removed.

The human mind can be both astonishingly capable and demonstrably fallible. A good investigation leaves both facts intact.
