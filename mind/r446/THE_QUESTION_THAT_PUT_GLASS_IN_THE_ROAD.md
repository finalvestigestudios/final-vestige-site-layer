# The Question That Put Glass in the Road
**Final Vestige Studios | Mind & Perception | Research manuscript | 17 November 2026 | UNPUBLISHED / SOURCE-CONTROLLED DRAFT**

**Dek:** A simple change of verb in an eyewitness question altered how fast people thought a crash happened. A week later, some remembered broken glass that had never been shown. What the experiments really established—and why the distinction matters to anyone selling an experience of the mind.

**Research status:** Original editorial synthesis; no clinical or retail-audio efficacy claim. Do not treat this draft as a live article. Editorial owner: Mind & Perception; cross-reference to existing published Puységur and Mesmerism investigations rather than replacing them.

## The glass that was never on the road

Imagine watching a brief film of two vehicles colliding. Minutes later someone asks you how fast they were traveling when they **hit**. Someone else, who watched the very same film, is asked how fast they were traveling when they **smashed** into each other. It sounds like a matter of vocabulary. It is also a different invitation to reconstruct the event.

In 1974, Elizabeth Loftus and John Palmer published two studies in the *Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior* asking what happens when the wording of an eyewitness question changes. After viewing accident films, participants estimated speed in response to verbs ranging from relatively mild to more violent. The stronger verb was associated with a higher estimate. A follow-up experiment included a delayed question about broken glass. There had been no broken glass in the presented film, yet the group previously questioned using “smashed” was more likely to report seeing it than comparison groups. [S1]

That result has survived as a memorable demonstration of suggestibility. It deserves to be remembered more carefully. The first measure was an estimate, not a tachometer reading, and the later one was an answer about a missing detail, not a direct window into the contents of a person's mind. The experiment shows a measurable vulnerability in reported memory. It does not mean a single word can rewrite every memory, that all witnesses are unreliable, or that an investigator can manufacture any chosen belief.

The question itself is the unsettling part. Language was not simply a neutral tool for retrieving an intact copy. It could become part of the information available when a person later tried to remember.

## What the two 1974 experiments actually tested

The first experiment began with short films of traffic accidents. Participants supplied speed estimates after hearing questions containing different verbs. The investigators were testing an interaction between the observed event and the post-event words used to describe it. Nothing in this design required participants to be hypnotized, meditating, or particularly gullible. These were ordinary perception-and-memory tasks conducted in an experimental context. [S1]

The second experiment is the part most often turned into a slogan. Participants watched an accident film, answered a speed question with one of the verbs, or did not answer that speed question, and returned to questions later. The broken-glass item asked about something that had not appeared in the event. It made the boundary between perception and post-event suggestion especially visible.

There are at least three possible explanatory levels, and they should not be casually flattened into one. The wording could influence a participant's inference about how violent the crash probably was. It could change the way information about the event is later stored or reconstructed. It could alter the response strategy used when answering a question whose correct answer is uncertain. The original authors argued that later information may be integrated into memory, while subsequent research has examined additional mechanisms. The reported results alone do not grant us unmediated access to which mechanism operated inside each person.

It is worth emphasizing what the research did **not** measure: an enduring personality change, neurological “reprogramming,” a clinical cure, the value of hypnotic audio, or financial behavior. Turning this study into a sales claim about a modern product would require evidence that the study did not supply.

## When a road sign changes after the accident

Four years later, Loftus, David Miller, and Helen Burns expanded the question. Their 1978 paper used a series of slides showing an automobile–pedestrian accident and later introduced information that was consistent with, misleading about, or irrelevant to the viewed event. Across five experiments and a pilot, the report describes 1,242 participants. Misleading post-event information reduced accurate responding on both a yes/no test and a forced-choice recognition test. [S2]

This is important because the research moved beyond the emotional tone of a single verb. It examined whether a person could encounter visual information and later have incompatible verbal information affect a memory test. Such results help explain why police interviews, journalism, public inquiries, oral histories, classroom teaching, and informal family storytelling all need care with the ordering and framing of questions.

But even here there is an important limit. A slide-based laboratory task compresses time and simplifies the original event. Real crimes and historical events have stronger stakes, unequal exposure, social pressures, repeated conversations and many sources of corroboration. Applying a laboratory effect to a specific accusation or witness requires evidence about that situation, not just a citation to a general cognitive phenomenon.

Suppose an interviewer asks, “What happened after the man threatened the crowd?” That question has quietly introduced a threat, whether or not anyone saw one. A more careful approach begins, “Tell me what you remember seeing, from the beginning,” records the answer before follow-up prompts, and identifies which facts came from the witness versus the interviewer's question. This is a practical interviewing safeguard, not a foolproof lie detector.

## Repetition is not the same thing as corroboration

The modern misinformation literature has become more specific, not less. In 2023, Rachel O'Donnell, Jason Chan, Jeffrey Foster and Maryanne Garry reported two experiments and meta-analytic evidence testing whether misinformation repeated by multiple purported witnesses becomes more powerful simply because it appears to come from different sources. Their findings supported an effect of **repetition**, but did not support an additional independent effect of **source variability** under the tested conditions. [S3]

That is a useful corrective to a tempting oversimplification: “If three people say it, it becomes three times more believable.” Their experiments asked a narrower question, under particular conditions, and found that repeating misleading information mattered more than multiplying apparent speakers. The null finding for source diversity is not a universal claim that the identity, credibility or independence of sources never matters.

For historical investigators this distinction is foundational. Three printed accounts that copy one diary are not three independent eyewitnesses. A newspaper clipping quoted by a later book and then paraphrased in a documentary is a chain, not a corroborating chorus. A provenance table should record how each report came to exist, when it was written, and whether its author had access to earlier reports.

There is a parallel with digital audiences. A sensational claim repeated in short videos may feel familiar. Familiarity does not establish independent verification. The correct response is to trace sources, identify repeats, and examine primary records, rather than assume that every repetition adds fresh evidence.

## Where Puységur enters the picture

The case files of Armand-Marie-Jacques de Chastenet, Marquis de Puységur, are an unusually revealing historical bridge. In his animal-magnetism accounts, he preserved patient speech, witnessed demonstrations, failed predictions, private notes and notarial materials in an attempt to make an extraordinary claim evidential. Final Vestige's published **Puységur's Animal Magnetism Files** traces 153 source rows across 16 case dossiers, distinguishes original-edition reference from later verification scan, and keeps failures and consent conflicts visible. [S4]

The modern experiments cannot be retroactively applied to diagnose eighteenth-century witnesses. They cannot tell us that a particular patient's report was implanted, nor that Puységur's subjects were lying. What they can do is supply questions the historian should ask of any testimonial archive. Was the answer volunteered or elicited by a leading question? Who supplied the vocabulary? Were the same accounts repeated to later witnesses? Was the prediction recorded before its outcome? Was the witness in a position to disagree?

One of the most revealing features of the Puységur project is that the operator's own warnings survive alongside his claims. In the later notes, operator pressure and compliant false responses become part of the historical evidence problem. In the Charlotte and Catherine cases, the record also raises consent questions that no modern experiment about word choice can erase. [S4]

A notarized account establishes something about the existence and transmission of a statement. It does not establish the physical mechanism of animal magnetism. Likewise, a sincerely repeated memory demonstrates that someone reports remembering. It does not, by itself, make every reported detail an independently observed fact.

This is not a cynical doctrine that people are to be disbelieved. It is a method for treating people and records fairly: preserve their words, document the setting, and resist supplying stronger interpretations than the record allows.

## The surprising connection to everyday attention

A mind does not record the world like a surveillance system. It selects, interprets, fills gaps and revisits old information through present concerns. Final Vestige has already published a separate investigation of **change blindness**, including a flicker demonstration and a stranger-swapped-behind-a-door study. [S5] Change blindness concerns failures to notice changes between scenes. The misinformation effect concerns the influence of information introduced *after* an event. They are related as lessons in the limits of observation, but they are not the same experiment or the same mechanism.

Confusing them creates a new problem: a catchy umbrella word—“perception”—can make distinct findings appear to demonstrate one universal power. They do not. A missed change is not necessarily a false memory. A false recognition answer is not necessarily a deliberately dishonest testimony. A moment of intense attention does not guarantee durable recall.

The difference matters especially when consumer meditation and hypnosis products are introduced. A seller may say a recording makes it easier to relax or concentrate; a listener may find the format pleasant or useful; and controlled research may show something about attention or expectation. None of those facts alone establishes that the named recording prevents false memories, treats illness, increases intelligence, or reliably transforms a person's financial life.

## A free twelve-minute source-check exercise

You do not need to buy a recording to observe the influence of wording on your own interpretation. This is a **reflection exercise, not a diagnostic test or replication of Loftus and Palmer**. Do it alone, without deceiving another person or trying to produce a false memory.

For the first three minutes, recall a low-stakes event from the last week: a short conversation, a walk, or an ordinary errand. Write only what you directly remember seeing and hearing. Where you are uncertain, write “uncertain.” Avoid filling blank spaces with confident guesses.

For the next three minutes, write two questions about it. Make the first as neutral as possible—“What happened next?”—and the second subtly interpretive—“Why was that so frustrating?” Read them in sequence and notice what each question assumes. You are not attempting to prove that your memory has changed. You are identifying the assumptions hidden in the invitation to answer.

For minutes seven through nine, separate your notes into three columns: **observed directly**, **learned later**, and **inferred**. If a friend told you a detail, record that source. If you inferred somebody's motivation, mark it as an inference. It is fine for a row to remain unknown.

For the final three minutes, rewrite your account using only statements you can support. Keep uncertainty in the text. The useful outcome is a clearer record of how you know what you think you know, not a spectacular optical effect.

The site's promises about brainwave entrainment, rapid mental change, an hour's benefits in twelve minutes, or assured relaxation are **seller efficacy assertions**, not conclusions from the eyewitness studies above.

The product might appeal to someone who wants structured, short listening sessions instead of silence. That is a format preference, not an independently verified clinical or cognitive outcome. ## The standard worth keeping

There is an irony in using the misinformation effect to market products that claim to improve the mind. An article about the persuasive effect of wording could itself use persuasive wording to make a reader infer an unsupported benefit. To avoid that, the boundaries must remain unusually plain: a study of eyewitness questions is not an evaluation of a branded meditation track; an eighteenth-century claim is not a modern medical mechanism; and a reader's curiosity is not permission to overstate evidence.

What happened on the road in 1974 was modest in scale and profound in implication. The film did not change. A verb did. A week later, what some participants said they remembered had changed as well. It is a finding worth carrying into every archive, interview, and sales page—not as proof that the mind can be programmed at will, but as a reminder that a question may contribute to the answer.
