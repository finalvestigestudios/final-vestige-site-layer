# The Choice You Didn't Make — And the Explanation You Gave Anyway
**Final Vestige Studios | Mind & Perception Archive | Original investigation | Editorial draft, not published | 14 November 2026**

*An investigator swaps the evidence of a choice. A shopper explains the decision anyway. What does that reveal about our confidence in our own reasons?*

## The face in the magician's hands

Imagine two printed photographs. The instruction is ordinary enough: look at both faces and choose which one you find more attractive. You point. The researcher seems to hand you the photograph you chose and asks you to say why. Perhaps you mention an expression or an appealing feature. The difference between the photograph you selected and the photograph you now hold is the heart of the experiment.

In a set of studies published in *Science* in October 2005, Petter Johansson, Lars Hall, Sverker Sikström and Andreas Olsson surreptitiously changed the outcome presented after some participants' choices. On manipulated trials, the experimenters presented a face the participant had *not* selected. Participants sometimes failed to identify the mismatch and offered accounts of why they had made the apparent choice. The researchers named the phenomenon **choice blindness**.

The surprise is not that a person can be fooled by a sleight of hand. The surprise is what sometimes follows: the brain supplies a perfectly coherent explanation for an outcome that did not match the person's expressed decision. In the experiment, a verbal explanation could accompany a misrepresented choice. A sincere explanation was not necessarily a reliable record of the preceding event.

It would be tempting to generalize this into a sweeping assertion that people never know what they want. That would be as careless as accepting every explanation at face value. The studies demonstrated failure to notice some deliberately engineered discrepancies under specific conditions. They did not show that all introspection is fiction or that a buyer's reasons are inherently worthless.

**Source:** Johansson et al., “Failure to detect mismatches between intention and outcome in a simple decision task,” *Science* 310, 116–119 (2005), DOI [10.1126/science.1111709](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1111709); [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16210542/); [Lund University publication record](https://www.lunduniversity.lu.se/publication/c579a9b6-1b4a-4572-932e-0ff15f9832b4).

## What the experiment actually tested

It helps to separate the stages. First came a visible choice. Then came an intervention: the researcher covertly altered the presented result. Third came an opportunity to detect the mismatch. Fourth came the request to explain. The most interesting finding emerged not from any single stage but from their separation.

The researchers did not read participants' minds. They could record the selection, engineer an outcome discrepancy and compare a person's later response to what had actually happened. That made the experiment more revealing than a simple question such as “Why do you like that?” A normal interview contains no independent record of what the respondent would have said without being given the premise. Here the experimenters deliberately introduced a difference between action and supposed outcome.

A participant who did not detect the switch could be consistent with more than one underlying cognitive process. Attention might have been directed elsewhere. The two options might have been poorly distinguished. People might rely on an assumption that an experimenter has faithfully returned their selection. They may reconstruct an explanation from features of the object in front of them. The design demonstrates a dissociation between selection and report; it does not, by itself, uniquely identify which mechanism accounts for every participant or trial.

This limitation makes the effect more useful rather than less. When evidence supports several mechanisms, the honest conclusion keeps those possibilities open. The demonstrated fact is that post-choice narratives can be influenced by a false presentation of the chosen outcome. We should not silently upgrade that finding into a universal explanation of human decisions.

It also matters that a laboratory instruction to pick between faces is not the same thing as deciding about medicine, spending savings or consenting to a procedure. The risks, time horizon, emotional investment and opportunities to inspect an outcome all differ. The study provides a carefully bounded warning about a process that might matter elsewhere, not a calibrated probability for every real-world decision.

## The jam stand

Five years later the same line of research left the controlled photographic choice and entered a supermarket.

In *Cognition* in 2010, Lars Hall, Petter Johansson, Betty Tärning, Sverker Sikström and Thérèse Deutgen invited shoppers to taste samples of jam and smell teas. Participants selected the alternative they preferred. When asked to sample their apparent choice again and explain the preference, some received the alternative they had not selected because the researchers had secretly switched sample containers.

The research team reported that no more than a third of the manipulated trials were detected overall. Even some strikingly different taste or smell pairings were not reliably noticed. That does not mean all shoppers were indifferent to flavor. It means that, within this protocol, detection of a switch was far from automatic.

The market setting mattered because the alternative was not an abstract photograph. The participant had a sensory experience, then was asked to judge and explain it. The manipulation also showed why the investigator needed an independent record of the original selection. Without it, a persuasive flavor description could be mistaken for evidence that the reported choice had really been made.

**Source:** Hall et al., “Magic at the marketplace: Choice blindness for the taste of jam and the smell of tea,” *Cognition* 117(1), 54–61 (2010), DOI [10.1016/j.cognition.2010.06.010](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2010.06.010); [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20637455/); [Lund record](https://www.lunduniversity.lu.se/publication/52cc857a-0c24-48f4-b81f-a0407a2ebfe9).

What this does *not* establish is a neat law of commercial persuasion. The study did not test the effectiveness of an affiliate link, the conversion rate of a video, or whether anyone who bought a meditation program would regret it. It tested a more specific proposition: people can sometimes fail to register when the thing presented as their choice is not the thing they selected.

## The explanation is not a time machine

We normally experience reasons as if they preceded and caused a decision. That may often be a reasonable interpretation. We remember wanting the blue notebook, preferring the stronger coffee or choosing the safer route. But when an event can be covertly substituted between a choice and an explanation, the experimenter can examine whether the explanation tracks the original intention.

The 2005 researchers' follow-up analysis looked at what participants said when asked to explain manipulated outcomes. Their 2006 *Consciousness and Cognition* paper explored language in those introspective reports. The existence of an articulate reason did not guarantee access to the unaltered choice.

**Source:** Johansson, Hall, Sikström and Olsson, “How something can be said about telling more than we can know,” *Consciousness and Cognition* (2006), DOI [10.1016/j.concog.2006.09.004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.concog.2006.09.004); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17049881/).

An explanation, then, should be regarded as a form of evidence: useful but not self-authenticating. It can reveal how someone interprets an event. It can disclose values and attention. It may also be shaped by what the person is told happened, by the wording of a question, or by the artifact placed in their hands. The correct response is not contempt for testimony. It is an interest in where testimony comes from and what else can check it.

## Puységur, patients and the tempting certainty of a record

Final Vestige's [Puységur Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) present an unusually revealing eighteenth-century attempt to transform extraordinary experiences into recorded evidence. Puységur used named subjects, predictions, witnesses, written statements, repeated procedures and notarial acts in arguing for animal magnetism. The archive also preserves failures, warnings and conflicts of consent.

The 2005 and 2010 studies do not experimentally explain Puységur's cases. The distance between an eighteenth-century treatment setting and a modern covert-choice manipulation is too great to pretend otherwise. Their connection is methodological.

In the historical files, a statement made in an altered or dependent state could be treated as proof that an operator's theory was correct. The person reporting the experience, the person setting the conditions, and the person interpreting the evidence were not always independent. A signed statement was an authentic piece of paper; it was not a controlled test of the proposed magnetic mechanism.

The modern choice-blindness experiments make a related caution concrete. An apparently first-person report can be shaped by the outcome the investigator makes available. If testimony is important, so are the circumstances of collection, the alternative hypotheses, the original selection, and whether dissent or failure was allowed to survive the telling.

Puységur himself sometimes recorded discomfort about operator power. The Final Vestige archive cites his warning that steering thought-responsive subjects was an abuse of that power. That historical admission does not prove the presence or absence of magnetism. It shows why the chain from report to interpretation must be examined rather than merely decorated with signatures.

To read these cases as either modern hypnosis science or obvious nonsense is to skip the best historical question: how did a group of witnesses decide what counted as evidence, and who controlled the account?

## The consumer's version of the question

A sales page can present the same kind of interpretive challenge without performing a literal switch.

Imagine reading about a twelve-minute audio routine that promises to transform how you focus. The page offers a vivid account of the life you could have if your mind were more orderly. Later, you explain why the offer appealed to you: “I chose it because it helps me concentrate.” But what has actually been established? The seller may have described a product and asserted a benefit. Those are different claims.

Choice-blindness research does not show that such an audio program is ineffective. It also cannot serve as evidence that the product works. Nor does it establish that a particular ad rewrote your preferences. The practical lesson is to preserve the boundary between **a story that makes an offer feel plausible** and **evidence that validates an outcome**.

The official Zen12 page describes a multi-level package of MP3 audio sessions and accompanying guides. It also advertises claims concerning the intensity of meditation benefits, attention, stress and the mechanism of brainwave entrainment. These efficacy claims are not established by the choice-blindness studies. An independent review would need product-specific, appropriately designed evidence to state the claims as fact.

For an ethically minded reader, useful buying questions are concrete. What exactly will arrive? Is this a download, an instructional course, or both? Can the seller document the benefits for this actual product? Are the refund terms precise and applicable to the intended checkout? What happens if the paid version is no more useful than quiet time without a recording?

These questions are not a refusal to buy. They are a way to make the choice legible *before* a purchase creates a story to defend it.

## A twelve-minute choice-audit you can perform for free

This exercise is an editorial demonstration, **not** a reproduction of Johansson's controlled experiments and **not** a diagnostic test or therapy.

**Minutes 0–2: Name two options.** Choose an ordinary, reversible decision: which of two short essays to read, which nonessential household task to do first, or which of two songs to queue later. Avoid medical, legal, financial or safety decisions. Write the options exactly as they are, before deciding.

**Minutes 2–4: Make your choice privately.** Record “I choose A” or “I choose B” and a brief reason. If possible, note the decisive attribute rather than a long defense. Do not revise this entry later.

**Minutes 4–6: Write what could change your mind.** What evidence would cause you to choose differently? This is the crucial safeguard. You are specifying a standard before the result is known.

**Minutes 6–9: Revisit the alternative.** Read or consider the option you did not pick, without concealing or manipulating either choice. Write its strongest legitimate advantage. If that changes your preference, say so; a changed mind is not a failure.

**Minutes 9–12: Compare the records.** Look at the original choice, its reason, your revision standard and your final judgment. Identify one piece of information that emerged after the first selection. The exercise tests whether *you can preserve a decision trail*, not whether you are immune to a laboratory illusion.

There is no need to trick a friend or conceal an item to make the lesson work. The ethical value is in keeping a trace of what was actually chosen and why, before a polished explanation takes its place.

## Five things the evidence does not say

First, choice blindness is not permanent blindness to contradiction. The studies report both detected and undetected manipulated outcomes, and performance depends on experimental conditions.

Second, it is not proof of mind control. A manipulated experimental outcome is not evidence that the participant's underlying preference was erased or implanted.

Third, the findings do not justify deceiving consumers to test a sales pitch. The scientific design came with a specific experimental context; an affiliate publisher does not acquire permission to switch offers or conceal material terms.

Fourth, a person can have good reasons even when a specific report is mistaken. The relevant question is whether a reason predicts a choice under independently checkable conditions, not whether the speaker sounds confident.

Fifth, none of these papers establish product-specific cognitive, clinical, therapeutic, financial or meditation effects for Zen12, Hypnosis Live, Brainwave Shots or another consumer product. The research and the sales copy belong in different columns of any honest evidence table.

## A better last question

The least interesting version of this story is that people are gullible. The more interesting version is that evidence can change between an event and our account of it — and a fluent explanation does not always reveal the change.

This matters in archives, where statements survive longer than the conditions that produced them. It matters in testimony, where a question can carry an assumption. And it matters on a purchase page, where an inviting story can make the prospect of a benefit feel like the evidence of one.

The solution is not to demand perfect self-knowledge. It is to keep the record: the original selection, the exact claim, the source that supports it, the competing explanation and the outcome that could prove us wrong. That is as useful for a modern audio product as it is for a magnetic memoir written two centuries ago.
