# The Hand That Wasn't Yours
*An investigation into the rubber-hand illusion, the limits of testimony, and a scientific argument about suggestion.*

**Final Vestige Studios | Mind & Perception | Editorial draft, not published | November 2, 2026**

Imagine a table with a false hand resting on its surface. Your own hand is out of view. A researcher brushes the visible fingers of the imitation and your hidden fingers at roughly the same moments. You watch the object. You feel the contact. The timing lines up. For some people, the uncanny consequence is a report that the false hand somehow feels like part of their own body.

Nothing supernatural happened. The object didn't grow nerves. Yet the testimony matters. It points to an awkward fact about the most intimate fact we possess: even the sense that a body part is *mine* can be susceptible to experimental manipulation. And then comes a second, more difficult question. How much of that testimony can be attributed to the timing of touch—and how much might depend on the question the researcher asked?

This is the rubber-hand illusion. Its history is also a rare opportunity to watch scientists publicly argue about whether they have properly controlled for suggestion.

## The small correspondence that opened a very large question

In February 1998, Matthew Botvinick and Jonathan Cohen published a brief scientific correspondence in *Nature* called “Rubber hands ‘feel’ touch that eyes see.” Their account brought vision, felt touch, and knowledge of limb position—*proprioception*—into a single provocative experiment [1]. The study was not a claim that a prosthetic object became someone's hand. It was a test of how the brain might assemble bodily self-identification from cues that usually agree.

Think of these cues as witnesses whose stories ordinarily match. You can see your fingers resting beside a coffee cup. You can feel the pressure of the surface. Without looking you can estimate where your hand is. These channels are not identical. They are different kinds of evidence, gathered by different sensory systems. Life usually spares us the task of deciding which witness to trust.

The rubber-hand setup creates an unusual conflict. The eye sees a hand-shaped object in a plausible position. Touch arrives in time with the visible contact. But proprioception still carries information about the real hand concealed nearby. The experimental question becomes what people report when different sources of information disagree.

The famous version of the experiment involves synchronized brushing. But timing is not decorative detail; it is the experimental variable. When the brush strokes are out of step, the apparent relationship between what is seen and what is felt changes. A proper investigation therefore needs conditions that differ in the timing or congruence of stimulation—not just a spectacular anecdote from someone who says the rubber hand felt real.

There is no universal guarantee that a participant will report the illusion. Even among people who do, experiences and ratings differ. A polished demonstration video can make the effect look inevitable. That confidence belongs to the editing, not to the data.

## Two measures, two stories

Researchers have commonly separated two outcomes. One is *subjective ownership*: agreement with statements about the visible fake hand feeling as though it belongs to the participant. The other is *proprioceptive drift*: a shift in where someone estimates their own hidden hand to be.

The two measures are interesting precisely because neither is a perfect lie detector for consciousness. A questionnaire records what a person says they felt. A location estimate records behavior under a particular task. They may travel together without being interchangeable, and they can be influenced by different features of the setup.

In 2005, Manos Tsakiris and Patrick Haggard revisited the illusion in a series of experiments. They examined how the timing of touch and the expected anatomical form of the hand affected the result [2]. Their conclusion was careful: matching visual and tactile signals was an important part of producing the illusion, but it was not the whole explanation. The body's established representation also mattered.

Later, Tsakiris and colleagues tested a continuum of objects, including a wooden block and hand-like forms [3]. Their reported findings suggested that synchronized touch alone does not confer a sense of ownership on just any object. This is why the physical resemblance of the fake hand should not be edited out of the story. A compelling illusion is not a magic trick with an interchangeable prop.

The experiment provides a lesson about scientific measurement. A reported inner experience cannot be weighed like a metal sample. That does not make the experience fake. It means the researcher needs independent predictions, honest controls, and humility about the inferences a rating scale will bear.

## The objection from inside the laboratory

In 2020, Peter Lush published a paper with an arresting title: “Demand Characteristics Confound the Rubber Hand Illusion” [4]. *Demand characteristics* are aspects of an experiment that may signal what responses are expected. A participant may sincerely experience something influenced by expectation, or may report in a way shaped by the setup. These are not identical explanations.

Lush asked what people expected to feel after being told about the classic procedure. In his quasi-experiment, participants read about and watched a demonstration of the rubber-hand arrangement, then predicted how people would respond to the usual illusion and control statements. The pattern of their expectancies resembled published patterns of reported illusion ratings. He argued that the traditional comparison questions had not been adequately validated as controls for this kind of suggestion.

That argument has teeth. If investigators ask, in effect, whether a false hand felt like your own, the question could itself be a clue. A participant who has seen an introductory video already knows the phenomenon is supposed to be strange. When research relies on an inherently subjective report, expectation cannot be dismissed simply because the question sounds clinical.

But there is a crucial distinction: showing that expectations can predict a pattern of answers does **not** by itself prove that synchronous visual and tactile stimulation has no effect. Both processes could contribute. The paper challenged the adequacy of controls; its strongest possible interpretation—that the illusion might be wholly a suggestion effect—became the next target of debate.

## A disputed explanation, not a vanished phenomenon

In 2022, Mel Slater and Henrik Ehrsson reanalyzed data underlying the debate [5]. They argued that when participants were divided by hypnotisability score, synchronized stimulation still produced greater ownership ratings and proprioceptive drift than asynchronous stimulation at each level of the score. Their model comparisons favored the multisensory condition over expectations or hypnotisability as the dominant contributor in those data.

That is an important counterargument, not a license to announce that expectations are irrelevant. Slater and Ehrsson acknowledged that hypnotisability was associated with subjective ratings. Their point was that such an association did not eliminate the within-person contrast between synchronized and unsynchronized conditions.

Also in 2022, Rémi Thériault, Mathieu Landry and Amir Raz studied top-down attention and task instructions in the rubber-hand illusion [6]. They found that instructions could modulate embodiment ratings, while cognitive load affected a different, affective dimension of the experience. The work reminds us that *multisensory processing* and *top-down influences* need not be mutually exclusive rivals.

The evidence is not a sporting event with one winner. Lush raised questions about expectancy and conventional controls. Slater and Ehrsson offered a reanalysis emphasizing sensory congruence. Other experiments point to body form and instruction. The most defensible summary is that bodily ownership reports depend on more than one input and that researchers still debate the contribution of each.

As readers, we can do something more valuable than selecting the most dramatic claim. We can inspect the experiment that would distinguish competing predictions. Would an outcome remain different when synchronization changed but instructions did not? Would the same pattern appear in a new sample? Are ratings and location estimates both reported? Does the paper discuss people for whom the illusion failed to occur? Which result was specified before the researchers saw the data?

These are ordinary questions. Their power comes from asking them before the story becomes a certainty.

## The historical echo: Puységur's files

Final Vestige's [Puységur Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) follow an eighteenth-century project that treated patient utterances, predictions, witnesses, signed statements, and repeated procedures as evidence. The published archive identifies 153 active master-source rows and 16 case dossiers and explicitly separates what the historical record documents from what it establishes.

The temptation is to link those dossiers to the rubber hand with a triumphant sentence: *science has finally proved that suggestion can make anything real.* But that would be a category error. Puységur's magnetic somnambulism occurred in a different social setting, with different interventions and claims. The rubber-hand experiments do not validate magnetic cures or clairvoyant speech. Neither does the presence of social influence in a modern experiment tell us which eighteenth-century testimony was sincere.

What connects the records is a discipline: treating testimony as evidence *of an experience or claim*, not automatically as proof of its proposed mechanism. The experience deserves to be described carefully. The causal theory must earn its place separately.

The same discipline runs through the [Seven Day Focus Fieldbook](https://finalvestige.com/focus/). There is value in noticing where attention goes, how a ritual feels, and what changes when a routine becomes regular. Such observations can be personally useful without establishing clinical outcomes or providing a shortcut to the findings of a controlled study.

## A free experiment in questions, not in mind control

You do not need a rubber hand, another person's touch, or a dramatic trick to appreciate the problem. Here is a safer exercise in how **wording** can guide an interpretation. It illustrates method, not the rubber-hand illusion itself.

Write down two neutral descriptions of a fictional observation: “A person reports that a model hand felt familiar while their own hand was hidden” and “A person reports that the model hand felt like theirs while their own hand was hidden.” Then ask yourself what each wording encourages you to infer. Does one sound like mere resemblance and the other like ownership? What physical measurement would you need before claiming a change in the person's knowledge of where their real hand was?

Now rewrite the claim a third way: “In a specified setup, some participants reported ownership-related sensations, and investigators compared responses across stimulation conditions.” This is less entertaining. It is also closer to the distinction between observation and cause.

If you encounter a live demonstration, do not assume you must feel the illusion. You can decline participation. No one should pressure another person into tactile demonstrations, and no such exercise is a treatment or a test of someone's mental health.

## Why this matters outside the laboratory

The problem of sensory ownership is intimate, but the reasoning travels. In an accident investigation, a person can honestly remember a detail without correctly identifying its source. In a historical archive, several witnesses can repeat a claim without independently confirming the proposed explanation. In personal development marketing, a moving description of a mental state is not equivalent to an independently measured product effect.

None of this says experiences are meaningless. In the rubber-hand studies, what someone perceives is exactly what scientists want to understand. It says we should resist collapsing three statements into one: “I felt it”; “the experiment made it happen”; “therefore the advertised mechanism is proven.”

A quiet form of rigor allows us to hold all three apart. That rigor is the point of an evidence archive. It is also what keeps curiosity alive after the spectacle ends.
