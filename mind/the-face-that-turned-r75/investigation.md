# The Face That Turned — and the Expression That Returned
*Final Vestige Studios · Mind & Perception · unpublished research manuscript · November 2, 2026*

Imagine looking at a portrait upside down. The eyes are where eyes should be, the mouth in its ordinary place, and the face belongs to a recognizable human being. Something about it seems familiar even when the forehead points toward the floor. Now turn the photograph upright. It looks monstrous.

The image has not changed. The information reaching your eyes contains exactly the same altered features. What has changed is the orientation of the whole face—and, with it, the ease with which certain relationships become noticeable.

In 1980, the psychologist Peter Thompson published a two-page paper in *Perception* titled **“Margaret Thatcher: a new illusion.”** The British prime minister's portrait supplied its memorable subject. Thompson inverted the eyes and mouth *within* the portrait and then turned the entire image upside down. The grotesque effect could be surprisingly unobtrusive when inverted and conspicuous once the portrait was rotated upright.

It was an ingenious visual joke, but it was also a real research instrument. The question was not whether photographs can deceive us. It was how a face becomes a face in perception: what do we detect feature by feature, and what do we read in the relationships between those features?

## Two turns, not one

There are two manipulations, and they should never be confused. One changes the orientation of the eyes and mouth relative to the surrounding face. The other turns the whole image through half a revolution. An ordinary inverted photograph is not itself the Thatcher illusion. Neither is a face with its mouth distorted while everything remains upright.

The comparison matters because it keeps the alteration constant while changing the viewing condition. The same eyes, the same mouth, the same arrangement of pixels can support a different judgment when the whole picture is upside down.

That is more interesting than declaring that our eyes cannot be trusted. Sensation, recognition, and decision are different parts of an experiment. An observer may detect something unusual yet fail to identify exactly which part changed. Another may recognize the face but misjudge its expression. A third may learn the trick and spot it immediately. These outcomes would not be interchangeable measures.

Thompson's original publication introduced the phenomenon. It did not settle every explanation later attached to it.

## Parts, wholes, and the danger of an easy explanation

A common summary says that we process faces as wholes and that turning a face upside down disables that processing. The summary captures something useful, but it is too neat.

In 2003, Luc Boutsen and Glyn Humphreys reported four experiments separating comparisons of isolated facial features from comparisons of whole faces. They varied whether faces belonged to the same person and what kind of matching task participants had to perform. In this way, an observer could not always solve the task by searching for one obvious mark.

Their findings implicated what researchers call *configural processing*: sensitivity to relationships among features. Inversion disrupted the use of certain local relationships, while “Thatcherization” interfered with the configural information conveyed by upright features. But the strength of the effects depended on the task and available information.

That qualification is important. A demonstration can show that one type of judgment becomes harder without proving that an entire system of perception has shut down. The eyes are still visible. The participant is not suddenly unable to see mouths. What has changed is the information that is most useful or accessible for the question being asked.

Other research has challenged the idea that a single whole-face explanation is sufficient. Work on orientation-sensitive facial features, including studies that isolate the eye or mouth region, suggests that feature-level encoding can contribute. The right question is therefore not “Which magic switch went off?” but which cues explain which observations under which experimental conditions.

## Rotate the witness

In 2007, Janek Lobmaier and Fred Mast asked an unusually clever question: What if it is not the picture that turns, but the person looking at it?

Their participants viewed normal and altered faces while tilted at different angles on a three-dimensional turntable. This separated two reference frames that are ordinarily aligned: the orientation of the image on the retina and the body's orientation relative to gravity.

Why does this matter? When we say that a face is upside down, we might mean upside down on a screen, upside down relative to the observer's head, or upside down in the world. Most everyday observations cannot distinguish those possibilities.

The investigators tested face judgments while varying picture orientation and body tilt. Their results suggested that retinal orientation was the main reference frame for the effect, while a particular head-down tilt also revealed an influence of gravity or bodily orientation.

This was not a general finding that gravity changes everyone's face recognition in every circumstance. It was a measured result within a particular apparatus and task. The experimental advance was to separate explanations that seem indistinguishable when a subject sits upright in front of an ordinary screen.

The best research often proceeds this way. A phenomenon begins as an image that seems impossible. Then an experimenter asks how to make two rival explanations predict different outcomes.

## Try a cautious version yourself

Draw two simple faces on a piece of paper. Make the outlines identical. For the second, reverse the eyes and mouth inside the oval. First compare the faces upright, then rotate the page through 180 degrees and compare them again. Take notes on what you notice before telling yourself what the result should be.

If you want a second observer, show both drawings without explaining the transformation and ask what seems different. Do not turn the question into a test of their intelligence.

**This is an illustration, not a standardized reproduction of Thompson's experiment.** Minimal drawings may fail to produce the published effect; expectations, time on task, rendering quality and familiarity can matter. A single person's answer cannot estimate an effect size. If the difference is obvious in both orientations, report that honestly. Demonstrations that must succeed are advertising, not good inquiry.

The free exercise has value whether or not you experience a striking illusion. It asks you to distinguish the stimulus, the condition, the observer's report, and the explanation offered afterward.

## When an honest witness becomes an unreliable theory

This is where the illusion belongs within Final Vestige's larger investigation of evidence.

The site's published **Puységur's Animal Magnetism Files** examines unusual historical testimony recorded around the development of mesmerism. Those records are not experiments on faces. The relevant bridge is methodological, not mystical.

An historical account may establish that a witness described an extraordinary state. It does not, by itself, prove the proposed magnetic mechanism. Likewise, a viewer may sincerely report that an inverted face looked ordinary. That report tells us something about perception under a given condition; it cannot establish any sweeping account of hidden mental forces without further evidence.

The distinction honors the witness rather than dismissing them. It allows experiences to be real as experiences while asking independent questions about their causes.

History is full of cases in which people understood the same event through competing explanations. Science is full of instruments designed to distinguish between those explanations. Their shared discipline is to protect observations from the stories we most want to attach to them.

## What the upside-down face cannot promise

A commercial presentation may use attention illusions as shorthand for a claim that a subconscious mind can be effortlessly rewritten. Another may imply that a visual surprise demonstrates brainwave control, therapeutic benefit, or the power to achieve a financial outcome by listening to an audio recording.

**None of that follows from the Thatcher illusion.** The studies examined here concern face perception and particular discrimination tasks. They do not test meditation apps, hypnosis recordings, brainwave products, health treatments or wealth programs.

Even the statement “the brain ignores upside-down features” needs care. The research does not show universal ignorance or an absence of retinal information. It shows that perception and judgments depend on orientation, feature relationships, the task, and sometimes the reference frame.

There is no need to inflate the result. The actual phenomenon is sufficiently remarkable.

The picture turns. The pixels do not change. An expression suddenly returns.

The useful conclusion is neither “seeing is false” nor “the subconscious can be controlled.” It is this: **seeing is an operation performed under conditions, and those conditions matter.**
