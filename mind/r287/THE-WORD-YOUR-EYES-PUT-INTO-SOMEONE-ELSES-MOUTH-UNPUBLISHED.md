# The Word Your Eyes Put Into Someone Else's Mouth
**Final Vestige Studios · Mind & Perception Archive · ORIGINAL UNPUBLISHED INVESTIGATION · 10 November 2026**

*An audio recording says one syllable. A face appears to say another. Some listeners report a third. The surprise is not that perception can be fooled; it is how much a familiar demonstration can—and cannot—tell us.*

## The voice on the film did not say that

Imagine a short recording of a person repeatedly saying “ba.” There is no hidden sentence, no subliminal instruction, and no clever editing of the sound. You listen to the audio alone, and “ba” is what you hear. Now watch the person's mouth move in a different pattern while exactly that same soundtrack plays. A surprisingly common response is “da.” The word was not recorded. The discrepancy was built between what the microphone captured and what the eyes supplied.

In December 1976, psychologists Harry McGurk and John MacDonald published a brief report called [“Hearing lips and seeing voices”](https://www.nature.com/articles/264746a0). Their demonstration paired an acoustic /ba/ with a visual /ga/. Adults reported a fused /da/ sound. With the audiovisual pairing reversed, reports frequently combined elements rather than producing the same fusion. When the audio played without the mismatched video—or when the film was unaltered—responses were accurate. The crucial observation was not that people could be persuaded to *say* something different. Their reported auditory experience itself could change.

The paper did not invent the concept of visual information influencing speech. People have long used lip movement to understand a speaker in a noisy room. What it made hard to ignore was a peculiar consequence: information from the eyes could contribute to the sound one believed one had heard. The experimental film was deliberately unnatural. In life, speakers generally make sounds that correspond to their mouths. That ordinary match is why audiovisual speech can be useful; the experiment made the inputs disagree to reveal an underlying process. [Original report](https://doi.org/10.1038/264746a0).

## Three answers, two signals, one caution

It is tempting to announce that “the eyes overrule the ears.” That slogan is memorable, but it smooths out the phenomenon. The classic display involves at least three distinguishable categories: the acoustic sound, the visible articulation, and the resulting report. A listener may hear the acoustic syllable; may report the visual syllable; may experience a fused syllable; or may describe a combination. The frequencies of those reports depend on the person, the talker, the specific recording, and the choices the experimenter offers.

This makes the McGurk effect useful for explaining why a witness's certainty is not the same thing as a pure record of one sensory input. It does **not** prove that every witness is mistaken, that people's minds can be remotely controlled, or that a single viewing demonstrates someone is particularly suggestible. Conflicting information from two senses is a specific laboratory manipulation. The lesson is narrower and stronger: seeing a source can change what we report hearing from it.

It is also worth noticing what the original experiment did *not* do. It did not put headphones on a participant and measure a week of productivity. It did not compare a meditation recording with a control program. It did not test an affiliate product. An audiovisual speech illusion cannot verify a claim about hours of focus after a fifteen-minute audio session.

## Why a viral demonstration does not measure you

The modern McGurk clip circulates as a repeatable shock: close your eyes, open them, listen again. The clip can be delightful. It is not a medical test or a standardized cognitive score.

Researchers have measured tremendous variability. In [a 2018 study by Brown and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30418995/), some participants rarely reported fusion while others did so frequently. More fine-grained visual speech-reading skill accounted for some variation, but measured attentional control, processing speed, working-memory capacity and auditory perceptual gradiency did not explain the remaining differences in that study. The investigators stressed that susceptibility to an audiovisual illusion should not be treated as a simple diagnostic of those abilities.

A 2014/2015 research program by Magnotti and Beauchamp illustrated another complication: if two laboratories use different faces or different recordings, they may be measuring different distributions of responses. In a model fitted to data from 165 people viewing as many as fourteen stimuli, the researchers separated audiovisual disparity from individual encoding differences. A single popular clip cannot substitute for a design that varies the stimuli. [Noisy encoding of disparity model](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25245268/).

Another study tracked susceptibility to a range of McGurk stimuli over time. Despite wide variation across people and clips, the average frequency changed little at the one-year retest, and individual results were strongly correlated between tests (reported r = 0.91). Even that reliability is specific to the method and sample, not a claim that a person's brain has a fixed permanent type. The same study found that forcing participants to pick among limited response options produced an estimated eighteen-percentage-point higher rate of the effect than allowing open responses. [Variability and stability](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25802068/).

Here is the practical implication for a short-form video: two viewers can both be paying attention and report different syllables. The difference does not certify which viewer is more perceptive. If the video then sells an “upgrade” to the one who heard the unexpected syllable, it has turned a research finding into a commercial claim without the necessary bridge of evidence.

## The more revealing problem: what a study chooses to count

“Percentage affected” sounds like a neutral number. But before taking that number seriously, ask how it was made. Did the experimenter count a fused “da” response only? Did they count anything other than the acoustic /ba/? Were participants given three buttons or asked to describe what they heard? Was the same speaker used throughout? Did the research team report all groups, including inconclusive comparisons?

These questions matter beyond laboratory tidiness. A paper on published comparisons between participant groups found that selective publication of statistically significant McGurk differences can exaggerate apparently large group contrasts. In simulations and empirical analyses, one example showed a true ten-percentage-point difference being reported as thirty-one points in a typical published significant sample. That illustrates the bias mechanism rather than a universal correction factor. [Published estimates of group differences are inflated](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30231054/).

An important 2018 review by Alsius, Paré and Munhall warned that the mechanisms involved in incongruent McGurk stimuli may not map straightforwardly onto normal, matching audiovisual speech. This is a problem of generalization, not grounds for discarding the effect. The experiment shows something striking about perception under conflict; it does not settle every question about everyday conversation. [“Forty Years After Hearing Lips and Seeing Voices”](https://doi.org/10.1163/22134808-00002565).

The best historical experiments have a similar trait: they answer a question smaller than the legend later attached to them. They reveal a mechanism; the public story tries to make it an entire theory of the mind.

## When the archive meets the senses

Final Vestige's [Puységur Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) concerns a different phenomenon entirely: the historical claims, observations and interpretations surrounding animal magnetism. The [Mesmerism investigation](https://finalvestige.com/2026/11/02/mesmerism/) examines an eighteenth-century commission's attempts to separate effects of suggestion and expectation from assertions about a magnetic fluid.

McGurk's audiovisual manipulation and the 1784 inquiry do not prove one another. They share a discipline: identify what observers were shown, what they were told, what they reported and what can be independently checked. In the audiovisual case, the independent soundtrack can be replayed. In the historical magnetism case, the surviving accounts, procedures and contested claims can be compared. In neither case does a surprising response automatically substantiate the grand explanation offered around it.

The archive's purpose is not to turn suggestion into the explanation of everything. There are neural, perceptual, social and experimental questions to distinguish. One should not replace a claim about invisible magnetism with a claim about universal mind-control simply because modern neuroscience sounds more sophisticated.

## A demonstration that does not pretend to be an experiment

You can explore the *logic* of multisensory evidence without borrowing a copyrighted McGurk film or inventing a “brain test.” Here is a paper-and-voice exercise you can do without recording anyone or asking them to perform an unusual task.

Write the syllable “ba” on a piece of paper, then cover it. Say “ba” at an ordinary volume, and write down what you heard yourself say. Next uncover the written syllable while you repeat it; notice how little conflict there is when sight and sound agree. Finally, read the original 1976 study's description of how the researchers deliberately made speech sound and lip movement disagree.

**What this does not do:** it does not reproduce McGurk's visual manipulation, induce the illusion, determine susceptibility, or test your focus. A real replication would require carefully prepared and rights-cleared audiovisual stimuli, controlled presentation, recorded responses and a suitable comparison condition. This exercise simply makes the difference between agreement and experimentally created conflict explicit.

If you encounter a demonstration online, you can perform a more informative media-literacy check. Record in your notes (not in a tracking form) the clip's source, the actual audio syllable if disclosed, the exact visual syllable, whether the presenter offered a forced-choice response, and whether the demonstration claims more than its source supports. The clip's popularity and a viewer's reaction are not substitutes for those facts.

## Why this belongs beside a discussion of commercial focus audio

There is a legitimate reason to seek an audio routine. Some readers want a clear duration, a soothing structure or an alternative to silent practice. A structured library can be convenient without being scientifically extraordinary. But the influence of *visual* speech on an *auditory* report is no evidence that commercial audio has the claimed effect on concentration, stress, wealth, health, brainwaves or the duration of meditation.

The distinction is especially important when marketing combines science vocabulary with a promise of effortless improvement. Inspire3 identifies Zen12, Hypnosis Live and other products as its own offerings. Zen12's standard public sales presentation describes twelve-minute sessions and makes an hour-equivalence claim. The seller source URL is recorded in the separate unpublished claim-evidence register. The seller is the source for the *description*; it is not independent product-specific proof of that outcome. Final Vestige has not evaluated the audio firsthand, conducted a controlled comparison, verified an affiliate agreement or authenticated a paid order.

For a reader seeking a noncommercial first step, the existing [free Seven Day Focus Fieldbook](https://finalvestige.com/focus/) provides a twelve-minute starting routine, worksheets and a purchase-claims checklist. It uses the existing Dispatch subscriber list. It is not a clinically tested treatment, and its general research references do not certify the fieldbook itself.

The order of operations is important: **understand the experiment, check the evidence, try a free and measurable practice, and consider a purchase only if a documented feature solves a real need.** No product sale is required to make a surprising perception finding worthwhile.

## A note for the next person who shares the illusion

The sound in the original demonstration has not changed. The visual track has. In some people, the reported experience changes anyway. That small discrepancy is a better ending than an extravagant promise. It shows why evidence can be unsettling: two sincere observers can describe different impressions of the same recording.

The proper response is not to distrust every experience. It is to keep the original record available for comparison. A soundtrack, a film, a transcript and the exact experimental method give us a way to ask a question that personal certainty cannot answer alone.
