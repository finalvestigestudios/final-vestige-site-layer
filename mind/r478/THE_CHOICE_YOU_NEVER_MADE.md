# The Choice You Never Made: Why a Switched Answer Can Feel Like Your Own

*Final Vestige Studios · Mind & Perception Archive · Investigation draft · 18 November 2026 · NOT PUBLISHED*

**Source note:** The findings below concern specific experiments, not a diagnosis of ordinary people, a method for manipulating someone, or evidence that a commercial audio program can change a mind. A successful demonstration of a mismatch does not identify its psychological mechanism.

## The card that came back wrong

Imagine being handed two photographs and asked which face you find more attractive. It is an ordinary choice, the sort for which we can usually offer a reason. You point. The experimenter hands over the chosen card. Then comes a request: explain your decision.

Here is the concealed fact: the card has been exchanged. You are now holding the face you *didn't* choose.

What happens next is more interesting than a magic trick. In Petter Johansson, Lars Hall, Sverker Sikström, and Andreas Olsson's 2005 experiment, a number of participants did not immediately call out the mismatch. They offered explanations for the face presented to them. The paper called this pattern **choice blindness**: a failure, in the experimental interaction, to identify a discrepancy between an intended selection and the reported outcome.

That is the unsettling part. The investigator did not merely ask participants to remember a face. Participants had to explain a choice they had just made, while a carefully timed substitution changed what appeared to have been chosen. The distinction matters. An explanation can sound introspective and sincere without accurately recording the causal path to the answer.

But in 2025 another research team asked a question that every reader of the original deserves to hear: What if some people *noticed* the mismatch but did not tell the experimenter?

This investigation follows that question through supermarket shelves, altered questionnaires, eye measurements, and finally the eighteenth-century archive of a man who thought he had discovered a new force of nature. Our subject is not that people have no convictions. It is how quickly a confident story can outpace the observations supporting it.

**Original study:** Johansson et al., *Science* 310, 116–119 (7 October 2005), https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1111709 ; abstract indexed at https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16210542/ .

## What the researchers actually changed

The 2005 task used paired photographs of women's faces. A participant pointed to a preferred face. In specially arranged trials the experimenter used a sleight-of-hand card substitution to give the participant the unchosen face instead. Asked to justify the selection, some described features of the face now in front of them as though it were their own choice.

The original paper's scope is narrower than the popular summary, "people don't know what they want." In the setup, the alternatives were faces; the choice was an attractiveness preference; and the switch followed the decision almost immediately. The social setting placed an experimenter in charge of presenting the answer. A participant could fail to remember, trust the experimenter, rethink the choice, decline to challenge the apparent mistake, or genuinely not notice. Those possibilities produce similar outward behavior, but they are not the same explanation.

The study demonstrates an experimentally produced mismatch and particular observed responses. It does not establish that all preferences are imaginary, that memories can be rewritten on command, or that persuasion has a single universal mechanism.

A useful reading rule follows: separate the fact of a manipulated trial, the act of reporting it, and a theoretical claim about the private mental process behind that report. Research often measures what a person says; the person's moment-by-moment awareness may require additional methods.

## A jar of jam at a supermarket

Five years later, the researchers carried a related setup out of the laboratory. Lars Hall and colleagues invited shoppers to sample two jams or two teas at a local supermarket. Participants chose a favorite and were invited to sample the chosen product again and explain their preference. During the handoff, the researchers secretly reversed the sample.

The 2010 paper reports that no more than a third of manipulated trials were detected in the study overall. Even large taste or odor differences did not always guarantee detection. This was not a laboratory face-selection trick transplanted unchanged: the researchers had moved into taste and smell, with people consuming ordinary foods in a public shopping setting.

That ecological detail makes the study memorable. The participant may say they like a particular jam because it tastes more refreshing, while the product in the sample cup has been switched. Yet even here, the finding does not license a universal promise that customers can be made to buy anything. Deciding which jam tastes better in a brief encounter differs from choosing a costly service, consenting to a treatment, or evaluating a claim against written documentation.

**Source:** Hall et al. (2010), *Cognition*, "Magic at the marketplace: Choice blindness for the taste of jam and the smell of tea," https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20637455/ . Consult the paper's exact task and detection definitions before turning its results into a numerical slogan.

## When the switched answer concerns morality

A subsequent experiment replaced pictures and food with a more consequential-seeming object: a person's own stated opinion. In 2012, Hall, Johansson and Strandberg used a self-transforming paper survey. A physical alteration caused participants to encounter a reversal of a previous moral attitude, apparently under their own recorded response. Researchers then asked them to discuss the statement.

The study's frequently quoted figure is that **69% of participants failed to detect at least one of two changes**. This is a participant-level result for that experimental procedure, not an estimate of how often ordinary people abandon moral principles. Some reversals were noticed. Some were not. The authors reported participants arguing for the opposite position of the one originally selected.

To understand the difference between an experimental result and a societal prediction, keep three questions separate. First: did the participant recognize the altered text? Second: did the participant challenge it in conversation? Third: did a lasting preference change follow? A short discussion in a staged survey does not independently answer the third question.

Political and moral survey research raises difficult questions about the meaning of a stated attitude. We might express a viewpoint before we have considered the reasons, update it under new evidence, or object to a misleading survey question. A study of covert reversal can tell us something about report and explanation; it cannot by itself tell us which participants possess settled convictions.

**Source:** Hall, Johansson & Strandberg (2012), *PLOS ONE* 7:e45457, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3446893/ ; critical philosophical discussion: https://doi.org/10.1007/s11229-019-02414-3 .

## The 2025 challenge: silence is not necessarily blindness

The next turn in the history matters because it resists an irresistible headline. In a paper published **4 December 2025**, Pablo R. Grassi and colleagues reported experiments using computerized choice-blindness tasks, pupil measurements, behavioral responses and post-experiment questioning. They found evidence that participants could detect manipulated outcomes without reporting them during the task.

The researchers did not simply declare that all earlier experiments were wrong. Rather, they showed why an operational definition based mainly on overt correction may confound several experiences: failing to notice, suspecting an error but remaining quiet, assuming that a button was pressed incorrectly, accepting a changed alternative, or being reluctant to accuse an experimenter of deception.

Pupillometry is not a magical window into belief. Changes in pupil response can reflect surprise or processing demands, and interpretation depends on the experimental context. The contribution of the 2025 work is a convergent argument: behaviors and later answers can reveal awareness that an on-the-spot verbal report misses.

This raises a difficult possibility for everyone who uses the phrase choice blindness. How much of the effect is perceptual or mnemonic failure, and how much is a decision *not to report* the mismatch? There is no reason to assume that the balance is identical for a playing card, a cup of tea, a moral statement and a financial form.

The newer work itself has boundaries. It used particular computerized tasks. It should not be generalized automatically to every earlier in-person sleight-of-hand experiment. Researchers disagree about which mechanisms dominate. That unresolved disagreement is exactly why a responsible documentary should show this counterevidence, rather than stopping at the original spectacular result.

**Source:** Grassi et al. (2025), *Frontiers in Psychology* 16:1598254, https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1598254 ; full methods and dataset link: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12713319/ . Publication date 4 December 2025.

## What about consequential decisions?

Another test applied the framework to pensions. In a 100-participant financial-choice experiment reported in *Judgment and Decision Making*, no more than 37.2% of manipulated trials were detected over the tested conditions. But the authors also reported that financial sophistication, reaction time and decision complexity predicted detection, and that participants' subsequent explanations did not fit a single story of wholesale preference change.

That is important because a pension-choice task comes closer to consequential consumer reasoning than a face photograph does. It still does not establish that real retirement plans or payments were switched in ordinary life, or that financial advisers can use this effect to induce purchases. It is an experiment whose assumptions, participant pool and incentives have to travel with its headline.

The same caution should govern affiliate and marketing copy. A sales page can make a bold proposition seem intuitive. The fact that a visitor can imagine an explanation for wanting a product is not verification that the product delivers its promised outcome.

**Source:** "Choice blindness in financial decision making," *Judgment and Decision Making* (2023), https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/judgment-and-decision-making/article/choice-blindness-in-financial-decision-making/74173A560D9294AE9A8D51FAF3A70FA6 .

## The older paper trail: Puységur's willing witnesses

An eighteenth-century chapter of Final Vestige's archive offers a different, older version of the same problem: how does testimony acquire the authority of proof?

Armand-Marie-Jacques de Chastenet, Marquis de Puységur, kept records of what he considered animal magnetism. His sources include altered-state testimony, witness statements, predictions, failed predictions and warnings about an operator's influence. The surviving archive is fascinating not because modern choice-blindness research proves magnetic somnambulism—it does not—but because both situations force us to ask what a witness's explanation means.

A person can sincerely report something and still misidentify the cause. An operator's expectation can shape the question. An institutional setting can encourage agreement or inhibit challenge. A signed statement establishes that someone made a statement; it does not prove the extraordinary mechanism behind that statement.

Final Vestige's published Puységur dossier does what myth retellings often refuse: it preserves the failed cure, the failed prediction and the operator's own concern about compliance. It also documents episodes in which vulnerable subjects' requests were overridden, a serious ethical issue independent of whether the alleged magnetism existed.

It would be a mistake to collapse these episodes into a claim that everyone was "hypnotized." The historical terms refer to a distinct doctrine and source setting. The scientific studies described earlier do not retroactively validate animal magnetism, its cures, its forces or its ethical assumptions.

## A free twelve-minute source-audit exercise

This is not a test of hypnotizability, a medical practice, or a proven training method. It is a small way to apply an evidence habit to a routine choice.

**Minutes 0–2 — Write the choice first.** Choose between two mundane, low-stakes options—for example two books to read next. Before someone else's recommendation appears, record which one you prefer and why. Keep your note private.

**Minutes 2–4 — Record uncertainty.** Write one reason for the alternative. Mark which details you know and which you assume. Your goal is not to prove you were right.

**Minutes 4–7 — Read a persuasive description.** Examine an ordinary description of the option you did not choose. Look for subjective adjectives presented as measurable outcomes. Do not buy anything as part of the exercise.

**Minutes 7–9 — Compare without rewriting history.** Do you still prefer the same option? If you changed your mind, say so. Changing your mind with new information is not a failure or a choice-blindness result.

**Minutes 9–11 — Trace the claim.** Identify the maker of one strong statement, the source used to support it, and the critical detail that would have to be measured to justify it.

**Minute 11–12 — Preserve the difference.** Save your initial choice next to your current one. Write one sentence distinguishing memory, inference and observation.

There is no score, no commercial checkout, no collection of personal responses and no claim that completing the exercise improves cognition. It can simply help you notice which part of a convincing explanation is actually evidence.

## When the explanation arrives too smoothly

Choice blindness is sometimes introduced as a proof that human beings never know what they believe. The studies support a more precise, more useful proposition: in certain tasks, an experimentally switched result may be accepted or left unchallenged, and participants may produce reasons for the result they are shown. A newer research program shows that outward silence need not mean inward ignorance.

The lesson is neither that our minds are hopeless nor that every persuasive demonstration is a fraud. It is that an explanation, a preference and the evidence of an event are three different things. The strongest investigators preserve that separation, especially when the story would be more exciting without it.

A person describing the wrong photograph may be reasoning sincerely. A scientist may document a genuine and replicable phenomenon yet overstate one interpretation. A historian may collect a shelf of signed statements without establishing the extraordinary force they were meant to prove. A merchant may describe a product's audio format accurately while promoting effects that require much stronger evidence.

That last distinction belongs in the open: this article is independent editorial research, **not** an endorsement of Zen12, Hypnosis Live, Brainwave Shots, self-hypnosis services or any other commercial product. Choice-blindness findings do not validate those offerings. Any separate tools guide should identify the seller, verify the exact product and terms, and disclose compensation beside an authorized link—not smuggle a purchase recommendation into the scientific conclusion.

**Bottom line:** Ask not only, "Can I explain my answer?" Ask, "What did I actually choose, what changed after that, what did I notice, and what would prove the mechanism?" A compelling story is where an investigation begins.
