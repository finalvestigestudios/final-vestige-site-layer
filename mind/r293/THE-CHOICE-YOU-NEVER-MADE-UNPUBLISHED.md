# The Choice You Never Made

*Final Vestige Studios · Mind & Perception Archive · Original investigation · UNPUBLISHED, editorial review required · 11 November 2026*

**Deck:** A researcher gives a volunteer the picture they did **not** select. Sometimes the volunteer explains why it was the right choice anyway. The unsettling discovery is real; what it proves—and what it does not—is the story.

**Evidence classification:** Laboratory observation, peer-reviewed experiments, historical analogy, and original reader exercise are identified separately. There are no product endorsements in this investigation.

## The photograph on the table

Imagine sitting across from an experimenter with two portrait photographs. Your assignment is uncomplicated: point to the face you find more attractive. There is no correct answer to worry about. You point. The researcher passes your selected photograph to you and asks you to explain your preference.

Except something went wrong with the handoff. The face now in your hand is the one you rejected.

This is a paraphrased reconstruction of the procedure described by Petter Johansson, Lars Hall, Sverker Sikström and Andreas Olsson in a 2005 paper in *Science*—not a transcript of an individual participant's experience, and not a recreation of copyrighted experimental photographs. The researchers made the switch covertly. Some participants did not detect it and went on to offer reasons for what they believed was their earlier choice. They called the phenomenon **choice blindness**. [1]

It is tempting to read that result as proof that people have no idea what they want. The actual finding is narrower, more interesting and much more useful: under certain conditions, people can fail to notice a conflict between an action they just performed and the outcome they are shown. Some can then provide an explanation for the outcome as though it followed from their own decision.

A preference was expressed. An outcome was substituted. A confident explanation followed. The experiment deliberately broke the chain connecting those three events.

## A switch made with ordinary hands

The first study used pairs of female faces, presented in the context of attractiveness judgments. After participants chose, the experimenter used a concealed maneuver to present the nonselected face as the chosen one. The participant could be asked to describe the reason for the supposed choice. The paper's central contribution was not merely that people could overlook a visual difference. It was that the mismatch affected what they reported about their own decision. [1]

Three separate events matter. **Choice** is what the person indicated. **Presentation** is the photograph the researcher handed back. **Explanation** is what the participant later said. In an unmanipulated trial these refer to the same picture. In a manipulated trial they do not. A person can truthfully report the face they now see while incorrectly describing it as the face originally selected.

That last distinction is indispensable. The study does not license calling participants dishonest. An invented explanation is not necessarily a deliberate lie. The subject may believe the premise supplied by the experimenter. Researchers can observe the discrepancy between action and report; they cannot read a person's internal experience directly.

Nor should this be confused with simple change blindness. The change-blindness experiments in Final Vestige's published investigation involve a visual scene changing between views. Here a behavioral record—*your own decision*—is what no longer matches the evidence presented to you. The two phenomena can coexist, but they are not interchangeable. See our published [Change Blindness investigation](https://finalvestige.com/2026/11/02/change-blindness/). [6]

## When the explanation feels like evidence

Suppose someone tells you, “I picked this picture because the expression looks warm.” The statement sounds like first-person evidence. It may describe a genuine preference after seeing that photograph. But it may tell us little about the original choice if the person was covertly shown the other image.

That is the intellectual trap at the heart of the experiment. We often treat an explanation of behavior as a window onto the cause of behavior. Sometimes it is; sometimes it is a story assembled around information received afterward.

Johansson and colleagues subsequently examined participants' verbal reports through linguistic analysis in a 2006 paper on introspection. That work developed the questions raised by the initial experiment, but a linguistic description of accounts does not independently establish the participant's internal causal process. [2]

The experiment also depends on the researcher's ability to conceal a switch and ask for reasons in a social setting. Attention, memory, uncertainty, stimulus similarity and conversational expectations may all affect whether a mismatch is detected. The original design was a specific laboratory procedure, not a universal susceptibility test.

## From preferences to identification

The idea becomes more consequential when the decision being reported is not a preference between portraits, but an eyewitness identification.

In a 2013 field study, Anna Sagana, Melanie Sauerland and Harald Merckelbach recruited **71 pedestrians**. Two researchers first spoke with the participants while posing as tourists. Later, pedestrians viewed photographic lineups and identified the people they believed they had encountered. For one identification the researchers substituted an unselected photograph before asking participants to justify the decision. The published abstract reports **40.8%** blindness to this manipulation, with detection varying according to the similarity between the chosen and substituted faces. [3]

The qualification about similarity is not a minor footnote. It means the effect is shaped by the task and the material. A highly similar replacement and an unmistakably different replacement should not automatically be assumed to produce the same detection rate.

These participants were taking part in a staged field experiment—not in actual criminal proceedings. The result raises an important question about how a record of identification could be presented to a witness. It does **not** establish a real-world rate of mistaken conviction or claim that 40.8% of all eyewitnesses accept falsified identifications.

A later series of **five experiments**, published in 2014, explored this boundary more carefully. For evaluative choices, reported concurrent blindness was **27%**, with retrospective blindness **21%**. For absolute identification decisions, immediate concurrent blindness ranged from **32% to 35%**, but retrospective blindness was only **0% to 6%** in several conditions. With a 48-hour delay, concurrent and retrospective blindness rose to **68%** and **39%** respectively. These are distinct conditions and denominators: they cannot be averaged into an “eyewitnesses are blind X percent of the time” statistic. [4]

If a dramatic psychology video quotes one percentage without the decision type, interval and detection measure, it has probably simplified away the most consequential part of the finding.

## The second experiment: what happens to memory?

A report that accepts a swapped outcome is not necessarily a permanently rewritten memory. This difference matters because “choice blindness” is frequently recast as “scientists can implant any belief.”

Lotta Stille, Emelie Norin and Sverker Sikström investigated how choice-blindness manipulations might interact with later recollection in a 2017 *PLOS ONE* study. Their paper draws a distinction between failing to detect a manipulated choice and later remembering misleading information as though it were part of the original experience. These are connected questions requiring separate measurements. [5]

The authors also reviewed earlier identification experiments with sharply mixed results. One mock-crime procedure produced little or no choice blindness under some conditions; later choices did not reliably follow the swap. That makes a stronger story than a single universal figure: a manipulated decision can sometimes pass unnoticed, yet the opportunity to detect it depends heavily on what kind of decision was made, how it was presented, and when someone was asked.

The useful conclusion is not that every explanation is false. It is that first-person confidence, however sincerely expressed, does not by itself prove that a displayed outcome matches the earlier act.

## The historical problem: did the witness see the cause?

In 1784, investigators working with Benjamin Franklin examined claims associated with animal magnetism. In the following years the Marquis de Puységur documented episodes described as magnetic somnambulism. These were not choice-blindness experiments and should not be retroactively treated as such.

But there is a shared question about **inference**: what can a witness legitimately conclude from an experience, especially when expectations and suggestions shape the way that experience is described?

A statement such as “I felt something” documents a report. A stronger statement such as “the magnetic fluid caused it” requires independent evidence about cause. Similarly, in choice-blindness research, “I prefer this photograph” is different from “this is the photograph I originally selected.” One is a present report; the other is a claim about a past action that can be checked against the experimenter's records.

Final Vestige's [Puységur Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) place historical documents and interpretations side by side. The archive lists 153 source rows and 16 case dossiers; those figures describe the editorial source collection, not validation of eighteenth-century therapeutic claims. [7] The comparison here is methodological, not a claim that Mesmerism was an early discovery of choice blindness.

## An original three-minute exercise: separate the choice from the reason

This is a self-observation exercise written for Final Vestige, **not** a recreation of a deception experiment, a validated diagnostic tool, or a treatment.

On paper, write two ordinary alternatives you could genuinely choose between: two books already on your shelf, two walking routes, or two recipes you know how to make. Before choosing, state one concrete criterion: “the option with fewer steps,” for instance. Make the selection, write it down and date it. Then, in a different line, write the reason you think mattered most.

Return to the page after a few minutes. Ask whether the recorded choice actually meets the criterion. If you have changed your mind, say so. Do not silently rewrite the earlier decision. This is not designed to make you experience choice blindness. Its point is to keep three records separate: what you planned to value, what you selected, and how you later explain it.

Try it privately, without tricking somebody else. Covertly switching another person's belongings or decisions is not required for useful reflection. The published studies were research designs with their own procedures and ethical obligations.

One way to apply this in daily work is to write down a next action before starting: *I will read the first two pages and stop when I can summarize them.* The [free Seven Day Focus Fieldbook](https://finalvestige.com/focus/) already offers structured practice and a record of what you intended to do. It is a free educational resource distributed through Final Vestige's existing Dispatch signup, not an experimental test of choice blindness. [8]

## A seller's invitation is not a controlled trial

Why does this belong in the same archive as meditation and self-hypnosis?

Because the literature on choice blindness warns against a familiar leap from an interesting observation to a confident account of its causes. A commercial audio recording can give someone a pleasant structured period of listening. Whether that recording reliably improves focus, “reprograms” a mind or produces a defined number of hours of benefit is another proposition entirely.

Inspire3's official Zen12 page describes a twelve-level set of 48 MP3 recordings and associated guides. Hypnosis Live markets a *Total Focus* session as a 33-minute, 47 MB download. Brainwave Shots sells *Focus Shot* in 15- and 30-minute formats. These are seller descriptions of product packages, not independent efficacy studies. The sellers also make stronger claims about concentration and brainwave effects; those claims should be evaluated separately rather than quietly adopted by the editorial archive. [9–12]

No study discussed in this article tested those particular commercial recordings. The 2005 choice-blindness experiments supply exactly **zero** direct evidence of their effectiveness. Nor should an ordinary user be told that taking a twelve-minute break is equivalent to an hour of meditation because a sales page says so.

Final Vestige is developing an **independent, unpublished buying guide** comparing a free timer or fieldbook with the value of a structured paid audio library. It is not a published offer, we have not used or tested the product, and no affiliate purchasing link is enabled. Commercial recommendations, if ever approved, will be separated from the research conclusions and accompanied by a clear earning-link disclosure.

For the moment, the reader does not have to buy anything to benefit from the most important instruction this science offers: preserve the original decision before trusting the story you tell about it later.

## What we know, and where the record stops

**Observed under experimental conditions:** Some participants failed to detect manipulated outcomes of their own decisions and sometimes supplied reasons for those outcomes. The 2013 and 2014 identification studies provide evidence of important task, similarity and timing effects. [1,3,4]

**Interpretation:** Explanations of past choices are not automatically reliable records of the initial process. The effect is not a diagnosis, proof that all choices are illusory, or evidence that another person can control everyone's preferences.

**Unknown here:** The prevalence of choice blindness among all ordinary purchasing decisions; individual susceptibility; whether a particular reader would notice the switch; and whether any named meditation or hypnosis product has an effect on choice blindness. None was tested.

This is where the research ends. Anything a seller wants to add must begin a new, separately sourced argument.
