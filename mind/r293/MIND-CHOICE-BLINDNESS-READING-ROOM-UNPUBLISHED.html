The choice you didn't make

In a 2005 study, volunteers could be shown the photograph they had rejected and then explain why they had supposedly chosen it. The paradox is not a license to call every memory false; it is a reason to preserve the original record.

03 · Practice & reflection

Keep the first decision visible

Before starting one small task, write the exact action you intend to take. Return twelve minutes later and compare your record with what you actually did. This is an original everyday exercise, not a laboratory choice-blindness experiment or clinical treatment.

Should you pay for a structured audio routine?

Zen12's seller describes a course of twelve levels and 48 MP3s. The useful commercial question is whether a ready-made library will suit your preferences—not whether anyone has proved that twelve minutes replaces an hour of meditation.

A demonstration without a trick

Write down two options. Decide in advance which property matters to you—time, cost, complexity, or something else ordinary. Choose. Only then explain the choice.

Do those three records agree? If not, you can change your mind openly. The exercise does not claim to reproduce Johansson and colleagues' experimental manipulation. It simply creates a record before the memory of the decision is overwritten by the account of it.

Research boundary

The 2014 eyewitness experiments reported different detection rates for evaluative versus absolute decisions, and when the manipulated feedback occurred immediately versus after 48 hours. There is no honest universal “everyone is suggestible” percentage.
