# The Choice That Changed After You Made It
## Why a switched photograph and an altered spoonful of jam matter more than another promise to “reprogram your brain”

**Editorial status:** original evidence review; source-controlled; unpublished. **No affiliate recommendation or product-use testimonial.** Prepared 2026-11-15 for Final Vestige Studios' Mind & Perception Archive.

Imagine choosing between two photographs. You point to the face you prefer. An experimenter returns the photograph, asks why you chose it, and you explain the decision with an apparently good reason. There is only one problem: the photograph you have been handed is not the one you selected.

This is not a story about people who cannot recognize faces. It is a more unsettling question about how a person relates an action to the explanation they give afterward. In a 2005 experiment, Petter Johansson, Lars Hall, Sverker Sikström and Andreas Olsson used a sleight-of-hand method to exchange selected face photographs on some trials. The researchers reported that participants often did not flag the mismatch and could provide reasons for the substituted outcome. The scientific paper introduced the term **choice blindness**. [1]

That experiment does not mean all decisions are fabricated, that every memory is unreliable, or that the researchers proved free will is an illusion. It shows something narrower and more testable: under certain experimental conditions, a mismatch between what someone intended and what they were shown afterward was not always detected. Explanations supplied after the fact are not guaranteed to be accurate recordings of the decision process. [1]

The distinction matters to a publication whose central promise is to make the evidence itself the experience. Historical archives contain statements by people who were certain of what happened. A trustworthy investigator asks not just whether the witness is sincere but what evidence could check the witness's explanation.

### A laboratory trick with a serious question

The face experiment used a deliberate manipulation. Its design turned a simple choice into a test of monitoring: could a participant keep track of the selected option when presented with the purported outcome? Researchers recorded detection and elicited verbal explanations. The reported failures were not produced by secretly measuring private thoughts; they were an observable gap between the action, the manipulated feedback, and the participant's response. [1]

There are limits to that inference. A participant's noticing may depend on timing, the similarity of images, the social situation, task demands and how the discrepancy is presented. Not detecting a swap in a particular trial is not the same as never noticing a consequential change in ordinary life. The experiment also does not establish that a persuasive advertisement can change anyone's mind at will.

What the study makes visible is a methodological failure point: the person who narrates the decision is not an independent recording instrument. If we want to know exactly what someone picked, a contemporaneous record of the pick is better evidence than a confident explanation elicited afterward.

### The same question followed the investigators into a supermarket

In 2010, Hall, Johansson, Tärning, Sikström and Deutgen moved the method beyond face photographs. At a supermarket tasting station, shoppers sampled different varieties of jam and tea and chose what they preferred. Researchers then switched the content of the purportedly selected sample and asked participants to taste or smell again and explain their choice. Across manipulated trials, no more than about one-third of switches were detected, according to the paper's abstract. Even conspicuous contrasts could go unnoticed. [2]

This replication across a very different sensory setting matters more than a vivid anecdote about one fooled person. The object of judgment changed from a visual social stimulus to flavor and smell. The common element was the gap between expressed preference and experimentally altered feedback.

But notice what the experiment **did not** prove. It did not show that shoppers secretly preferred the other jam, that preference could be permanently rewritten, or that marketing claims are more effective when presented as neuroscience. An unnoticed swap reveals a limit in immediate self-monitoring; it is not evidence that any specific paid audio program can give you better focus or stronger judgment.

### An original two-column exercise — no trick required

You can investigate this problem without deceiving anyone. Set aside twelve minutes with a notebook or the existing free [Seven Day Focus Fieldbook](https://finalvestige.com/focus/). Before you evaluate two written options — for example, two alternative ways to organize tomorrow's research — write down which option you favor and **one concrete reason you expect to matter**. Date the note. For the next four minutes, read competing evidence or list practical tradeoffs. Then make the choice and write a fresh explanation without looking at the original. Finally compare the records.

Ask three questions: Did the decision change? Did the stated reason change? What piece of evidence, if any, accounts for the change? Differences do not indicate a psychological deficit and are not a score of suggestibility. Updating a judgment after new information may be a strength. This exercise is an **original recordkeeping demonstration**, not a validated choice-blindness diagnostic and not a reproduction of Johansson's deception paradigm.

The useful habit is preserving the original intention beside the later explanation. It makes revision visible, including revision for sound reasons. This is exactly what archival research tries to do with dated notebooks, marginal annotations and later recollections.

### The Puységur bridge: testimony is a source, not a verdict

Final Vestige's published [Puységur's Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) studies an eighteenth-century landscape of unusual behavior, magnetic somnambulism, medical claims, obedience and disputed testimony. [3] The records are important because they document what people reported and how observers interpreted it. They do not, by their survival, authenticate an alleged magnetic force.

Choice blindness and animal magnetism are **not the same phenomenon**, and a 2005 study cannot retroactively diagnose historical participants. The connection is editorial and methodological. Both demand separation of three layers: a record of what happened or was said; an explanation supplied by observers or participants; and evidence that would independently test the explanation. Without that separation, confidence can quietly become a substitute for proof.

It is possible to respect participants' sincerity and still refuse an unsupported causal claim. That same standard must apply when a modern salesperson calls an audio recording a proven route to calmer emotions, enhanced concentration, neurological entrainment or medical improvement.

### From demonstration to commerce: the unsupported bridge

The verified Inspire3 public catalog points readers toward several different offerings: Zen12 meditation audio, Hypnosis Live recordings and Brainwave Shots audio products. Their seller pages describe product format and benefits, but they are **sales materials**, not independent efficacy trials. [4–7]

The Zen12 seller describes twelve levels and forty-eight downloadable MP3s; its main sales material promotes short listening sessions. The specific assertion that twelve minutes produces an hour's meditation benefits is a **seller claim**, not a fact adopted by this investigation. The product's advertised components do not, by themselves, demonstrate a measurable improvement in choice accuracy, sustained attention or clinical symptoms. [4,5]

Hypnosis Live's **Total Focus** is described on its current page as one 33-minute recording, 47 MB, written and recorded by J. A. Amos. The same sales page contains strong assertions about concentration and reprogramming the mind. Final Vestige has not independently tested the recording, and evidence about hypnosis generally must not be substituted for proof about this file. [6]

Brainwave Shots' **Focus Shot** sales page describes 15- and 30-minute versions, additional sound variants and a seller-promised return policy. The seller describes a technology of auditory brainwave stimulation. Its separate FAQ describes isochronic tones. Binaural-beat studies cannot simply be repurposed as trials of this exact product or exact modality. [7,8]

And there is an additional live-commercial uncertainty: at the time of this review, the Total Focus product page displayed a current price of **$24.44** and a crossed-out **$29.95**. Previous FV notes cited a different observed price. Likewise, Zen12 has distinct standard and coupon-oriented seller routes. These observations demonstrate **price-path instability**, not an authorized Final Vestige price or guaranteed discount. Do not put a stale price into reader-facing CTA copy. [5,6,9]

### What a rigorous product guide owes the reader

A buyer's guide should answer four different questions separately. What is included in the purchase? What does the seller claim the recording does? What independent product-specific evidence exists? What are the actual eligibility, checkout, refund and access terms for the reader's proposed purchase path? An absence of evidence for the third question should be printed as an absence, not tucked beneath upbeat claims.

A second distinction is between reviewing an offer and endorsing it. We can explain an audio format without recommending purchase. We can describe the publisher's advertised money-back terms without claiming their fulfillment has been tested. A commission relationship, when authorized, must be disclosed close to the specific earning link and cannot create a covert incentive to change an investigative conclusion. [10,11]

The FTC advises clear disclosure of material endorsement relationships, and requires scientifically substantiated, nonmisleading health-related benefit claims. A disclosure cannot cure an unsupported substantive claim. In this workstream, **no medical, wealth, neurological, or performance guarantee is acceptable**, and no member of the studio claims to have used these recordings. [10,11]

### The smallest honest next step

If you came here because a short video made you curious about your own attention, the practical choice is not between believing a sales pitch and rejecting all practices. Start with a documented self-observation and a primary source. Read the original experiment. Keep an intention record. Use the existing free Focus Fieldbook if it helps you sustain the habit. You can inspect an independent buyer's guide later without being required to subscribe.

The proposed paid next step, **Zen12**, is explicitly **unavailable as an affiliate recommendation** until Final Vestige's publisher acceptance, exact product and channel permissions, issued earning URL, applicable checkout/refund conditions, and reporting terms are verified. This hold protects the reader and makes any future commercial result auditable. No outbound-click count can substitute for a confirmed sale; neither can an intention to buy.

**Editorial conclusion:** An explanation given with confidence may still be incomplete. That is a reason to improve our records, not to buy certainty in a downloadable file.
