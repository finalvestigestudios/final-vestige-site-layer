The choice you might explain even after it changes

Researchers in 2005 asked people to choose between photographed faces. On some trials, the displayed outcome was quietly switched. Participants frequently did not identify the discrepancy and sometimes explained the substituted choice. A 2010 follow-up extended the method to jam and tea in a supermarket. These controlled findings demonstrate limits of immediate decision monitoring; they do not establish that meditation audio improves it.

Read the 2005 original publication record and 2010 marketplace research, not a third-party testimonial about the product.

A free twelve-minute recordkeeping test

Write down two options and which one you prefer, with one specific reason. Spend four minutes considering the tradeoffs. Write the final decision and its reason without reading the first note. Then compare the notes. A changed reason is not a diagnosis, a suggestibility score, or a reproduction of the deception experiment. It is a transparent way to see how your reasoning was recorded.

Evidence, limits and independence

Choice blindness is an experimentally demonstrated phenomenon, not a trial of Zen12. Modern audio-meditation interventions vary widely; evidence from other training programs cannot be transferred to this particular recording. Historical testimony in Puységur's Animal Magnetism Files is treated as source material rather than proof of magnetic or hypnotic efficacy.
