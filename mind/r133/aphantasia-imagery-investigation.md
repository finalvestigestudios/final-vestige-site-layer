# The Beach You Cannot Picture

*What happens when a meditation voice asks you to see something that your mind does not show you?*

Imagine a narrator telling a room of people to close their eyes. "Picture a beach. See the waves. Notice the color of the sky." To some listeners, a bright internal scene forms almost at once. Others know what the words mean, remember beaches, can describe one accurately, and yet experience no picture at all. There is no inner postcard. They have not failed the instruction. They may have a form of mental-imagery variation now called **aphantasia**.

That difference matters in a world of audio programs, visualization advice, hypnotic scripts, and meditation instructions that assume everyone's internal experience resembles the narrator's. A particularly persuasive sales story can promise to teach almost anybody to "see" a better future. But what exactly is a mental image, and why should a buyer believe that an audio recording will produce one?

The history is more surprising than the advertisement.

## An old observation, a new word

In the nineteenth century, Francis Galton asked people to describe the apparent vividness of images summoned in memory. Their responses varied sharply. Some people spoke of clear mental scenes; others reported something closer to abstract knowledge. That contrast endured without a stable modern label. The word *aphantasia* emerged in a 2015 paper by neurologist Adam Zeman, Michaela Dewar, and Sergio Della Sala, following clinical observations of people who reported little or no voluntary visual imagery. Their brief report did not establish a universal diagnostic test, a single cause, or a treatment. It made a neglected difference legible to research. [1]

The vocabulary is useful, but categories are not destiny. Visual imagery is not identical to imagination. Someone can reason spatially, invent an argument, plan a trip, or describe a friend's face without necessarily seeing an internal photograph. Conversely, vivid imagery does not establish superior judgment or immunity to suggestion. Researchers now study a range running from imagery that is hard to detect to strikingly vivid imagery, sometimes called *hyperphantasia*. A 2024 review by Zeman emphasizes that different imagery modes and subtypes remain incompletely understood. [2]

These distinctions should make us wary of the phrase "everyone can visualize if they try hard enough." A paid course may teach a person to attend to sound, language, or bodily sensation, but that does not prove it can change the vividness of their visual imagery.

## The pupil that did not follow an imagined sun

One of the most revealing experiments did not rely exclusively on what participants said about their minds. In a 2022 *eLife* study, Lachlan Kay and colleagues measured pupil responses when participants looked at and later imagined bright or dark shapes. In participants able to generate imagery, the pupil response associated with imagining brightness tracked reported imagery vividness and objective indices of imagery strength. A group of 18 participants with aphantasia showed ordinary pupil responses when **seeing** bright and dark material, and changes associated with cognitive effort, but no significant evidence of the corresponding **imagery-induced** pupil response. [3]

Pause over that distinction. Their eyes responded to actual light; their mental task did not produce the same group pattern. The researchers interpreted this as physiological support for an imagery difference that cannot simply be dismissed as a failure of vocabulary or effort. Yet it was a specific study with a selected sample and tasks, not a medical diagnostic tool to reproduce in a living room.

The finding also teaches a broader lesson about marketing science: demonstrating one cognitive mechanism does not validate an unrelated commercial claim. A listening track that mentions "brainwaves," for example, does not inherit every finding about imagery, attention, or meditation.

## No mental image does not mean no mental life

Aphantasia can affect more than the familiar instruction to "picture a beach." A 2020 study by Dawes and colleagues found that respondents with aphantasia, compared with people reporting imagery, also described differences in other sensory imagery, autobiographical recollection, imagined futures, and dreams. These were group-level self-reported patterns, not proof that all people with aphantasia lack rich memories or dreams. The same paper found no apparent deficit in spatial abilities. [4]

That last observation is important. A person may know the layout of a childhood home, turn directions in the mind, or recall where an object belongs without having an image that resembles seeing. Aphantasia does not mean the person has no imagination, no creativity, no attention, or no ability to learn mindfulness.

The story is still developing. A 2024 article by Raquel Krempel and Merlin Monzel argues that voluntary-versus-involuntary imagery is not a tidy boundary; dreams and afterimages require separate investigation rather than sweeping assumptions. [5] A different 2024 review noted that imagery vividness differs by person and sensory modality and that subtypes likely exist. [2] The cautious conclusion is not "everyone can learn to visualize." It is "people experience internal representation differently, and simplistic universal instructions can miss some of them."

## When imagining a frightening story becomes measurable

A separate 2021 study examined physiological responses to frightening written scenarios. Wicken, Keogh and Pearson reported that a group with verified aphantasia showed reduced skin-conductance responses while reading and imagining frightening stories compared with control participants, although responses to frightening **pictures** did not show the same simple absence. [6]

It would be a mistake to turn this into "aphantasia prevents anxiety" or "people with aphantasia have no fear." It does not. The study isolated a particular task and physiological response. Its value here is narrower: a narrator's imaginary scene can have different bodily consequences for different listeners. That should shape our expectations of recorded guided imagery. Silence and failure to picture may be normal experiences, not evidence of inadequate commitment.

## A history of suggestion, not a proof of miracles

Final Vestige's [Puységur Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) document the complicated history of suggestion and interpretation. Historical reports of magnetism, trance, memory, and healing show what observers **claimed** in their period. They do not establish that an invisible fluid existed or that all trance phenomena behaved as their proponents believed.

The comparison with contemporary audio matters. Nineteenth-century observers sometimes took a subject's unusual response as proof of the theory they already favored. Modern sellers can make a related mistake when a subjective reaction to a recording becomes proof that every advertised mechanism must be real. A person who feels calmer after listening has described an experience; their report alone does not reveal which element—quiet, time away, expectation, sound, or specific technique—caused it.

Neither skepticism nor enthusiasm requires ridiculing the individual. An experience can be meaningful without proving the sales explanation.

## A free experiment that works without pictures

Try the following once, without treating it as a test of your brain. Set aside sixty seconds in a comfortable setting where you do not need to drive or supervise a task requiring attention.

First, read these words: **beach, tide, cool air**. Notice what arrives. Maybe a picture, maybe language, maybe the idea of a familiar place, maybe nothing particular. Do not grade the answer.

Now, for fifteen seconds, notice three actual sounds in the room or outside it. Notice whether they are continuous or intermittent. Next, attend to the physical contact of a hand on a table or your feet on the floor. Finish by describing in ordinary words where your attention went.

This demonstration makes no therapeutic claim. It is not a diagnostic screen for aphantasia. It merely illustrates that **attention can have different anchors**: visual images, words, sound, touch, or the physical environment. People who dislike visual imagery exercises may find nonvisual instructions easier to follow; that is a reasonable personal preference, not a scientifically verified treatment recommendation.

## The buyer's question nobody should skip

If a paid meditation or hypnosis audio describes lush imaginary scenes, the buyer should ask: *Does it offer a nonvisual way to participate?* The answer is product-specific. A seller's statement about "relaxation" or "rewiring" is not evidence that a particular recording has been tested in participants with aphantasia.

A useful purchasing checklist starts with format, sample accessibility, session length, instructions, actual refund terms, data-handling practices, and the availability of plain alternatives. If a product uses binaural audio, distinguish the real perceptual phenomenon from unverified assertions about mental health, memory, or universal brainwave control. If it asks for a beach, remember that no beach has to appear for a person to attend to sound.

## What we know—and what we do not

Research supports the existence of substantial variation in mental imagery, including aphantasia, and provides experimentally observable differences in some settings. It does **not** show that people without visual imagery cannot meditate or benefit from attention exercises. Nor does it establish that a commercial audio program can cure aphantasia, guarantee a mental image, replace clinical care, or deliver the same result to everyone.

The beach that never appeared may be the most useful image of all: a reminder that one person's perfectly natural internal world is not the standard against which every mind must be measured.
