# The Choice That Came Back Wearing Another Face

**Final Vestige Studios · Mind & Perception · UNPUBLISHED RESEARCH DRAFT · 11 November 2026**

*An experiment in which the answer changed—but the explanation kept going.*

Imagine being given two photographs. Someone asks which face you find more attractive. There is nothing complicated about the choice. You point to one. Moments later, the experimenter shows you what appears to be the photograph you selected and asks you to explain why.

You begin with a perfectly ordinary answer. The expression seems warmer. The eyes are striking. The face looks familiar, or not familiar enough to matter. You can give reasons for a preference that you remember making.

Except that the photograph in front of you is not the one you chose.

This is not a hypothetical mind-reading trick. A research team in Sweden devised a controlled way to switch the outcome of a decision without announcing the switch. Their question was narrower, and ultimately more unsettling, than whether people can be fooled: **when our choice and its apparent outcome disagree, will we always notice? And if we do not, what happens to the story we tell about our decision?**

The findings became known as **choice blindness**. They do not prove that our preferences are imaginary, that no one knows what they want, or that advertisers can permanently install any belief they choose. They instead expose a specific vulnerability in the evidence we sometimes use to explain ourselves.

## The envelope in the experiment

In 2005, Petter Johansson, Lars Hall, Sverker Sikström and Andreas Olsson published a brief report in *Science*: “Failure to Detect Mismatches Between Intention and Outcome in a Simple Decision Task.” Participants viewed pairs of photographs depicting female faces and chose which they found more attractive. The experimenters sometimes covertly changed the result presented to the participant. When asked to explain their chosen face, some people did not detect that the face now being discussed was the other one.

The principle looks simple in retrospect: a person makes a choice; the researcher manipulates the apparent output; the participant is asked to account for the output as though it belonged to them. The outcome is no longer a transparent record of the intention.

The researchers named the effect choice blindness. The *Science* abstract reports that participants sometimes failed to detect quite obvious mismatches and nevertheless generated descriptions of why they preferred the option they were shown. It is important to retain the experimental conditions: a brief choice involving photographs, an apparently authoritative handoff from the experimenter, and a concealed manipulation. The study was not an open-ended test of major life decisions.

A research report can invite a dramatic retelling. The stronger version—“humans never know why they make decisions”—is not the conclusion licensed by this experiment. Some mismatches were discovered; some were not. The manipulation mattered. So did the procedure, the immediate verbal explanation, and the distinction between a person's actual initial choice and the apparent result.

## Why the explanation matters as much as the switch

A switch in a photograph is a perceptual event. An explanation is a second event, and it can make the first one look more secure than it was.

When someone offers specific reasons for liking a face, a listener may assume the speaker has inspected that precise image and traced the preference back to the original decision. The language itself becomes convincing evidence: the detail sounds like memory. But under the manipulated condition, the explanation can concern an outcome the participant did not choose.

That mismatch creates a research problem about introspection. People have partial access to the motives behind their decisions, not an infallible transcript of their own thought processes. When circumstances supply a plausible answer, we may construct a plausible explanation. The researchers used the term **confabulatory introspection** to describe this aspect of their work. Here it refers to explanations offered without awareness of the switch—not to a clinical diagnosis, deliberate dishonesty, or evidence that every reason a person gives is fabricated.

There is a difference between saying, “This explanation did not correspond to the observed choice,” and saying, “All explanations of personal preference are meaningless.” The first is a testable statement in a specific task. The second skips over controls, variation between participants and the many occasions when people accurately identify a choice and explain it.

The experiment is valuable precisely because it allows the records to disagree. An initial selection and a subsequent account can both be documented. Neither needs to be discarded simply because the other is persuasive.

## The bridge from change blindness

Visitors to Final Vestige may already know the idea of [change blindness](https://finalvestige.com/change-blindness/): important visible differences can go unnoticed when attention, timing or scene continuity interfere with detection. Choice blindness borrows that puzzle but adds another layer.

In ordinary change blindness, the observer misses a change in the world. In choice blindness, the missed change can be presented as the observer's **own decision**. A report of what happened becomes a report of what *I did*. That is a different relationship between perception, attribution and personal identity.

A 2008 follow-up by Johansson, Hall and Sikström, published in *Japanese Psychological Research*, explored choices among both abstract patterns and faces. The article's abstract describes switches during the decision procedure and later memory testing, in which participants could remember the manipulated option as their own. This adds a reason to be careful about retrospective accounts: the question is not limited to a momentary failure to see a different picture.

But the scope still matters. Experimental memory performance under a designed deception does not make a quantitative prediction about the reliability of memories in court, about hypnosis, or about everyone's decisions outside the laboratory. Researchers can identify a mechanism worth investigating without having proved that it generalizes unchanged into every setting.

**Follow-up:** Johansson, Hall & Sikström (2008), “From Change Blindness to Choice Blindness,” *Japanese Psychological Research* 51(2), 142–155, [DOI 10.2117/psysoc.2008.142](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/psysoc/51/2/51_2_142/_article). This account uses the public publisher abstract and bibliographic record; it does not present unpublished trial details as verified.

## A later experiment makes the stakes clearer—and more limited

The Lund Choice Blindness Lab has continued studying how manipulated feedback and retrospective reasoning interact. Its publication list contains work on preferences, decision outcomes, voices, and political attitudes. This body of work is relevant, but it should not be turned into one sweeping statistic.

For example, Strandberg and colleagues' 2018 article addresses false beliefs and confabulation in political attitudes. Its title indicates the possibility of lasting effects under studied conditions, not universal susceptibility to permanent persuasion. Later work also examines **correction** of manipulated responses, which matters because some individuals detect or challenge the change. A useful archive records both susceptibility and resistance.

This is an especially important limit for a publication that investigates extraordinary claims. A well-designed demonstration can establish that a particular account is vulnerable to manipulation; it cannot, by itself, tell us who is lying, who is suggestible, or how every historical witness should be classified.

**Research directory:** [Lund University Choice Blindness Lab publications](https://www.lucs.lu.se/research/choice-blindness-lab/publications/). The later studies are cited as topics and bibliographic leads here, not summarized as though the full methods and results of each had been independently audited.

## An eighteenth-century echo, without a false equivalence

This is where the story intersects with [Puységur's Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/), a very different kind of evidence.

In the eighteenth century, Armand-Marie-Jacques de Chastenet, Marquis de Puységur, recorded speech, procedures, predictions, apparent recoveries and resistance among people involved in his magnetic-somnambulism system. His record is remarkably rich. It preserves not only extraordinary claims but failed predictions, refusal, contradictory testimony, and warnings about an operator's influence.

The relationship with choice blindness is **methodological, not mechanistic**. Johansson and colleagues were not testing animal magnetism, and Puységur was not conducting a modern randomized study of choice blindness. Both archives, however, force a disciplined question: when a person reports something under another person's framing or authority, what independent record can distinguish the experience, the interpretation and the procedure that produced the account?

Puységur's dossier includes consent problems. One patient asked to be left alone while the operator continued forcefully; other accounts present conflicts between wishes expressed in different states. These are historically sourced questions about power, not occasions for us to repeat intrusive or coercive exercises.

The modern experiment helps explain why the authority behind a question matters. It does not certify historical cure stories, prove hypnotic mind control, or establish that those historical subjects suffered a particular cognitive effect.

That distinction is foundational to Final Vestige. **Provenance is not proof of the claimed mechanism.** A document may faithfully record what someone said while the explanation attached to that speech remains unverified.

## Try a cleaner version: a choice audit without tricking anyone

You do not need concealed swaps to learn something useful from this research. Try a nondeceptive five-minute choice audit, alone.

Choose between two ordinary, low-stakes options—perhaps the order of two errands, two photographs you already have permission to use, or whether to begin a task in silence or with ambient sound. Before acting, write a sentence naming your selection and two reasons. Date or time-stamp it in a private notebook.

Later, *without editing the first entry*, write down how you remember making the choice and what seems important about it now. Compare the two records. Were the reasons the same? Did circumstances change? Was a detail missing? There is no “blindness score” and no predetermined lesson. A difference can come from learning, changing preferences, imperfect notes or ordinary reconsideration. It is not evidence that anyone manipulated you.

This exercise is deliberately not a replication of Johansson and colleagues' deception experiment. Its value is practical: it teaches a separation between **what was recorded then** and **what feels persuasive now**. That habit is useful for examining archives, commercial claims and your own decisions.

## What this does—and does not—say about meditation audio

A reader interested in concentration may reach this article through an attentional-blindness video and wonder whether a guided recording could fix the problem. It is a tempting bridge: here is a strange feature of the mind; there is a product promising to help the mind.

It is also the exact place where a responsible publication must slow its inference.

The 2005 choice-blindness experiment did not test Zen12, Hypnosis Live, Brainwave Shots, meditation, audio entrainment, hypnosis treatment or any branded intervention. Its results cannot serve as product-specific substantiation. A merchant may describe the format of an audio course, its tracks or its intended audience. Product performance requires a different kind of independent evidence.

For readers comparing structured meditation audio, the relevant buyer questions are mundane and useful. What is included? How long does each session last? Is a recording guided or simply music? Is there a free sample? Can it be used offline? What happens after purchase? Are the actual checkout terms clear and stable? What would a reasonable no-cost alternative look like?

## The record worth keeping

The most important artifact from the experiment is not the swapped photograph. It is the disagreement between two records: what the participant chose and what the participant later confidently explained.

That is also why choice blindness belongs in a historical investigation library. Sources can tell the truth about what a witness said and still leave open whether the witness understood the event in the way later readers assume. A vivid explanation deserves attention. It does not deserve automatic promotion to proof.

The experiment asks us to preserve the choice, preserve the changed output, preserve the explanation—and keep each on its own line of the ledger.
