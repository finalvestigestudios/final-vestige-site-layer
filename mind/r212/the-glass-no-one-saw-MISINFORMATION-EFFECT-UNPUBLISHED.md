# The Glass No One Saw: How a Question Changes the Witness

*There was no broken glass in the film. A week later, some people reported seeing it.*

That is the detail that has kept a psychology experiment alive for more than half a century. Not the cars. Not their speed. A small object that was absent from the screen and present in a later answer.

In 1974, Elizabeth Loftus and John Palmer published two experiments under an unassuming title: “Reconstruction of automobile destruction: An example of the interaction between language and memory.” Participants watched traffic-accident films and answered questions. The investigators changed one word of a question about speed. Later, in the second experiment, some participants were more likely to report broken glass that had never appeared in the footage.

The difference was not a hypnotist, a strange substance or elaborate apparatus. It was an ordinary verb. The observer had seen an event, then encountered language describing that event. The later answer was no longer independent of the question.

This is a good place to begin an archive of unreliable witnesses, because a recorded image offers a rare advantage. There is a reference record. We can compare what was shown with what was reported.

But the experiment has also become one of those scientific stories that gets bigger every time it is told. A careful reading has to retain both its force and its limits. A mistaken answer is not proof that the entire original memory was physically erased. Nor is a demonstration of suggestibility evidence that a commercial recording can improve focus, heal illness or permanently reprogram a brain.

The broken glass was not there. What else do we actually know?

## A word inserted after an event

The first experiment used short films of car accidents. After watching, participants answered questions about what they had seen. The crucial question asked them to estimate the speed of two vehicles when they made contact, but the wording differed by group. The list of verbs included *smashed*, *collided*, *bumped*, *hit* and *contacted*.

Those are not merely five synonyms in the context of a crash. They suggest different intensities. We hear *smashed* and picture something violent. *Contacted* can sound scarcely more consequential than a touch.

The original full paper documents forty-five students, nine assigned to each verb, and seven films of traffic accidents. The questionnaires first asked participants to give their own account of what they had seen, and *then* asked specific questions. Mean reported speeds ranged from 40.8 mph for *smashed* to 31.8 mph for *contacted*, with *hit* at 34.0 mph. The change in wording influenced subsequent estimates; the study did not prove that every observer's stored image changed. These are published group means, not a reanalysis of individual records. [1974 primary full text](https://bpb-us-e2.wpmucdn.com/faculty.sites.uci.edu/dist/c/571/files/2024/08/LoftusPalmer_ReconstructionAuto_JVLVB1974.pdf).

The crucial experimental variation came **after** an initial free account of the scene: participants were then directed toward a speed estimate by a question with a verb carrying a suggestion about the collision's severity. The unprompted account and the later manipulated question should never be conflated.

There are several ways that could matter. The word may alter how the event is later represented. It may also bias the estimate in the moment: someone might infer that a crash described as *smashed* was probably fast, whether or not their visual recollection changed. The response alone does not settle which mechanism occurred.

That distinction is not pedantry. It is the difference between documenting an effect on an answer and claiming to know precisely what happened inside memory.

**The source:** Loftus and Palmer (1974), *Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior*, 13(5), 585–589. [Original journal abstract and DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-5371(74)80011-3); [ERIC bibliographic record](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ112718). The linked original paper gives the methods and tabulated group results; no raw participant-level dataset was inspected.

## The return visit

The second experiment is the one with the glass.

After seeing a traffic-accident film, participants were asked a question about the speed of the vehicles. One version used the word *smashed*, another *hit*, and a comparison group did not receive that speed question. A week later, a question asked whether the participant had seen broken glass.

The film contained none.

The original full text reports a **separate sample of 150 students**, fifty per condition. Participants first described the accident in their own words and then received the speed question (*smashed*, *hit*, or no speed question). One week later, without seeing the film again, **16 of 50** in the *smashed* group, **7 of 50** in the *hit* group and **6 of 50** controls answered yes when asked about glass that was absent. Those are **32%, 14% and 12%**, respectively, in this study, not universal rates of mistaken memory. The first experiment's speed data must not be interchanged with these later reports.

A difference of that kind matters precisely because the later question seems mundane. Nobody was told to imagine shards on a road for a week. The misleading ingredient was embedded in language that looked like a normal follow-up question.

Yet two cautions belong alongside the result. First, the later measure was a yes-or-no report. It does not grant researchers direct access to a stored visual image. Second, asking whether glass was present can itself convey a suggestion. The experiment shows a difference between experimental conditions, not an ability to identify which participant experienced what internal mental change.

If we say simply that the question *rewrote the brain*, we have replaced careful evidence with our own dramatic metaphor.

The appropriate conclusion is sharper and more modest: wording supplied after an event can influence what people subsequently report about that event.

## A stop sign becomes another sign

Four years later, Loftus, David G. Miller and Helen J. Burns approached the problem using sequences of slides rather than accident films. Their 1978 paper examined the integration of verbal information into visual memory.

The researchers showed slide sequences depicting an automobile-pedestrian accident. The [complete original paper](https://bpb-us-e2.wpmucdn.com/faculty.sites.uci.edu/dist/c/571/files/2024/08/LoftusMillerBurns_StopYield_JEP-HLM78.pdf) reports **1,242 participants across a pilot and five experiments**, not 1,242 in each condition. The **pilot (N=129)** used yes/no recognition; **Experiment I (N=195)** used a forced-choice picture test. In that experiment, participants who received consistent post-event information chose the correct picture 75% of the time, compared with 41% after misleading information. Outcomes and procedures varied across experiments; not every experiment used every post-event information condition or both response formats. The [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/621467/) summarizes the combined finding that misinformation reduced recognition accuracy across the tested procedures.

The stop-sign-versus-yield-sign example associated with this research is memorable for a reason. A road sign is a discrete object. A witness can be shown one sign, then later questioned in terms that imply the other. If misleading information makes the wrong alternative more likely to be chosen under these conditions, an observed recognition response is not simply an untouched copy of the original event. These experiments did **not** manipulate the amount of effort participants invested in remembering.

But that does not license the conclusion that every false recognition report results from permanent destruction of the original memory. Questions of competing traces, source confusion, retrieval and decision bias have remained research questions.

The 1978 study also reminds us that the effect is not a magical property of one notorious word. Different stimulus sequences, misleading details and recognition procedures can be used to study whether information presented *after* an event contaminates a later report.

This matters for history as much as psychology. Records usually reach us in sequence. A first statement is transcribed. A witness reads another account. Someone asks a question in the language of a theory already circulating. A second statement appears, and later historians compare them. The second account may contain new knowledge, but it may also carry language from the intervening conversation.

The archive has to ask not only *what did this witness say?* but also *what had this witness been told before saying it?*

## The distinction between seeing and hearing about seeing

Consider three separate records that often get flattened into one narrative.

The first is the sensory event: what the film or slide actually depicted. The second is the intervening language: a question, description or suggestion introduced after the event. The third is the test answer: what the participant later says they recall.

In a laboratory, researchers can preserve all three, and in a well-designed comparison can vary the second while holding the first reasonably constant.

Outside a laboratory, the first record may be incomplete or missing. Nobody filmed a private conversation, an eighteenth-century consultation or an unremarkable incident on a road. Historians then have testimony about testimony. The possibility of suggestion is relevant, but it cannot automatically prove a witness was manipulated or mistaken.

This is where an evidence-first institution must resist the temptation to treat cognitive science as a universal solvent. There is a known effect under some conditions. It is not a warrant to declare every witness unreliable or every historical report false.

Nor can it adjudicate a particular legal case without facts about how interviews occurred, how records were preserved and what other evidence survives.

A sound editorial method is more work than the slogan “memory is unreliable.” It means building a chronology of exposure and asking how strongly each claim is tied to an independent record.

## What thirty years of work added

In a 2005 review, Elizabeth Loftus looked back on decades of research under the title “Planting misinformation in the human mind.” The [2005 review](https://doi.org/10.1101/lm.94705) defines the misinformation effect as reduced memory performance after exposure to misleading post-event information. It also notes that researchers have studied conditions that increase or reduce vulnerability, the limitations of warnings, and competing explanations of what happens to an original memory.

That retrospective is useful because it keeps us from turning a classic demonstration into a law without exceptions. Participants do not always accept a misleading suggestion. Warnings sometimes help, though not without qualifications. An observed recognition error does not by itself decide the status of every earlier trace.

The work belongs to an ongoing scientific argument, not a single final story about human nature.

This is also why a free online “memory test” should be honest about what it does. Many short demonstrations are entertaining, but they differ from controlled experiments in who participates, what they have been shown before, how prompts are timed and whether anyone is blinded to the comparison.

A reader who misses an object in a game has not received a diagnosis. A reader who notices it has not proven immunity from suggestion.

## A much older archive of suggestion

Final Vestige's [Puységur Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) preserve another kind of problem: the record of extraordinary claims in an era that lacked modern controlled psychology.

In eighteenth-century accounts of magnetic sleep, a patient's statements, a practitioner's interpretations and witnesses' descriptions frequently sit together. We can examine the text, the context and the role of the operator. We cannot use Loftus and Palmer's twentieth-century car-crash films to retroactively diagnose the state of mind of someone in the 1780s.

That would be another form of overreach.

There is a legitimate connection: accounts involving suggestion require careful separation between reported experiences and independent substantiation. The historical observer might be sincere and still influenced by the form of a question. Equally, the mere presence of a suggestive setting does not prove that any particular described event was fabricated.

The [Mesmerism investigation](https://finalvestige.com/2026/11/02/mesmerism/) discusses Franklin's 1784 commission and the methods used to separate expectations from claims about magnetic fluid. Its experiments involved different questions from the misinformation studies, and neither provides an endorsement of present-day hypnosis products.

A useful comparison preserves the differences instead of manufacturing a continuous lineage.

## The commercial temptation to borrow the result

Modern audio marketers often use words such as *suggestion*, *subconscious*, *reprogramming*, *entrainment* and *focus*. The words can sound scientific because some are connected to real research fields.

That is not enough to prove a specific product works.

Inspire3's [official catalog](https://inspire3.com/) lists meditation, hypnosis and brainwave-audio brands. The [Zen12 Premium page](https://zen12.com/hnp) describes a structured digital library of twelve levels and 48 MP3 recordings, with four listening styles. It also asserts that a short session can deliver benefits equivalent to much longer advanced meditation.

Those are different kinds of statements. The number of files and listening styles are **seller-described product features**. The comparative benefit statement is a claim about results and needs independent, product-specific evidence. The car-crash studies, the slide experiments and the 2005 review provide **none**.

More importantly, evidence that a leading question can affect a later memory report does not prove that a recorded suggestion can produce a beneficial, durable change in attention or health. The experimental manipulation, outcomes and context are entirely different.

## Try a source audit, not a memory trick

Here is an original exercise that avoids deceiving the reader or pretending to recreate a psychology experiment.

Choose a publicly accessible historical photograph or a short documentary clip whose original catalog or transcript can be inspected. Before reading any commentary about it, make a short written note of five visible facts. Describe what is actually in the frame, not why you think the event happened.

Next, read one published description of that image. Highlight three kinds of language: directly observable details, interpretation and words implying cause or intention.

Finally, return to the image and compare your notes with both the object and the description. Which details could you verify without the caption? Which were merely plausible? Did the commentary make a particular interpretation seem obvious?

For another day, choose a new image rather than relying on a result you already know. Keep the original observation notes unchanged. The goal is to preserve an independent record against which later language can be checked.

This is **not a reproduction** of Loftus's experiments: there is no deception, random assignment, controlled intervention or meaningful population-level inference. It is a source-reading habit, not a cognitive diagnosis.

## The witness and the record

An investigator who finds a contradiction has choices. They can accuse a witness of lying. They can decide the witness was merely confused. Or they can preserve the contradiction while investigating what the witness saw, what question they were asked, what other accounts they had heard and what independent record survives.

The third path is slower, but it is where useful history begins.

The missing glass in the 1974 experiment is a reminder that an answer has a provenance. It comes after an event, but it also comes after a question. A later statement is never automatically a pristine replay of the first observation.

The stronger lesson is not that memory is worthless. We depend on it. Witnesses, diaries, letters and oral histories are indispensable, even when incomplete. The lesson is that a good archive preserves the conditions under which testimony was made, and never mistakes the confidence of a retelling for an original document.

There was no broken glass in the film.

There was, however, a record of the question. That second record is what made the finding possible.
