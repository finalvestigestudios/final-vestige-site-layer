# The Memory That Arrived After the Event

*Final Vestige Studios · Mind & Perception Archive · Original investigation · UNPUBLISHED · 11 November 2026*

**Editorial position:** This is an investigation of a particular memory experiment and its limitations—not a repeat of the portrait-swap account in the earlier unpublished choice-blindness manuscripts. It is a proposed **companion research note**, not an approved new URL. No product recommendation appears in this investigation.

## The answer that came back wrong

A volunteer watches a brief film. Afterward, they mark what they remember on a questionnaire. The researcher asks about the answers; the volunteer explains some of them. Later, they answer a second set of memory questions.

It is a sequence that could be mistaken for a routine research procedure. But in the experiment at the center of this investigation, some of the answers returned to the volunteer had been altered. What looked like the participant’s own record was not always what that participant had written.

The researchers wanted to learn something quite specific. If a person did not detect a change to their earlier answer, and then explained the changed answer as their own, might their *later reported memory* shift toward it? In other words: could a manipulated report become a source of misleading information precisely because the person believed the report was theirs?

The paper, by Lotta Stille, Emelie Norin and Sverker Sikström, was published in *PLOS ONE* on **March 8, 2017**. Its title—*Self-delivered misinformation*—is a compact description of the experimental trick. The manipulation was delivered by a researcher, but its appearance was that of the participant’s own earlier answer. [1]

We should resist the obvious headline, “Scientists rewrite memory.” Memory is not a document that can be edited by replacing a line of text; neither the experiment nor its outcome warrants that metaphor without considerable qualification. What the researchers measured was changed *reporting* on later questions about an event. That is important enough without pretending to have direct access to someone’s private recollection.

## The sticker and the second questionnaire

Participants watched a short film featuring a sequence of events, then marked agreement or disagreement with statements about what they had witnessed. The statements appeared on paper. Some of the markings were changed using **reattachable stickers**, allowing a participant’s apparent original response to be moved or replaced before the researcher asked them to justify it. A later questionnaire asked about the remembered events. The design brought together two previously distinct research traditions. [1]

**Choice blindness** refers to failing to detect a discrepancy between a decision and the outcome presented as that decision. Its best-known demonstration involved choosing between portrait photographs, only to receive the nonselected portrait and sometimes explain that substituted portrait as one’s preferred choice. [2]

The **misinformation effect** concerns memory reports after exposure to misleading information. Elizabeth Loftus and John Palmer’s 1974 experiments, for example, examined how wording in questions about a filmed traffic accident affected participants’ subsequent answers. Their design did not use the same manipulation as Stille and colleagues, and the findings should not be treated as interchangeable. [3]

Stille and colleagues combined these ideas. Instead of someone telling a witness an incorrect fact, the researcher supplied an altered version of the witness’s *own* apparent response. That difference matters. We often inspect a stranger’s claim for error; we may give a familiar-looking record of our own answer a different level of trust.

The researchers gave participants room to notice and correct the manipulation. Some did. They did not simply count every altered answer as a case of choice blindness. A person who recognizes that a mark has moved and corrects it is behaving differently from one who accepts the manipulated result.

## What the numbers actually say

The published study reports **161 manipulated answers**. Of those, **61% were corrected**, while approximately **39% produced choice blindness** at the item level. Separately, **72% of participants** were choice blind on at least one of their three manipulated items. Those two percentages describe different denominators: one concerns altered *answers*, the other concerns *people who missed at least one alteration*. They cannot be swapped in a headline. [1]

The later memory questionnaire produced the more consequential finding. For statements where choice blindness had occurred, **70% of answers differed** from the first questionnaire. Of those, **68 percentage points** changed in the direction of the manipulation and 2 points changed independently of it. For statements that had not been manipulated, **19% differed**. These figures come from the paper’s specific comparison method and item exclusions; they are not percentages of all human memories that can be altered. [1]

The abstract reports that a majority of choice-blind participants subsequently changed their reported recollection in line with the supplied alteration, compared with a small minority of controls. This is the important positive finding. Leaving it out while citing the paper merely as a cautionary reference would distort the study in the opposite direction.

There is also a subtlety worth holding onto: in the authors’ coding, a change could include movement between categorical answers such as “no,” “don’t know,” and “yes.” This was a questionnaire outcome, not a neuroscience scan proving a durable new autobiographical memory. Specific event claims and the measurement rules deserve more attention than a spectacular generalization.

## The uncomfortable limit of a compelling result

The experiment is persuasive because the investigators controlled a record that participants thought was their own. But the design does not establish that the same effect occurs whenever someone receives false information. The study involved a particular film, statement format, paper manipulation, interviewer exchange and subsequent memory questionnaire. It also involved experimental demand and social interaction, where people may be motivated to make their account coherent.

The researchers explicitly discussed limitations. Some prompts were ambiguous; two items were excluded from the primary analysis because comparing their later free-text answers with earlier statements was too interpretive. The paper reports that including them produced a similar result with a somewhat smaller effect size. The authors also considered whether participants were reluctant to admit inconsistencies and how the manipulation size affected detection. [1]

Most important, **earlier studies had mixed results**. Work on eyewitness identification had shown that manipulated choices could be missed, but studies using different mock-crime and questionnaire setups sometimes found very limited or no choice blindness. The 2017 study’s contribution is not an end to that disagreement; it is a carefully designed instance where a later shift in reported event memory was observed. [1,4,5]

The right question is not “Can the mind be hacked?” It is: which records were changed, which people detected the change, what was asked afterward, and how was the outcome measured?

## Three versions of the same statement

Imagine a museum catalog that records a witness as saying that a carriage was red. The witness’s original note, however, says brown. Later, the witness explains why the carriage was red. A historian looking only at the catalog and the explanation might conclude that red had always been the witness’s recollection.

This is an **invented archival analogy**, not an episode in the 2017 experiment or an actual museum document.

We can distinguish at least three things: the event that occurred, the first report about it, and a later report influenced by an intervening record. In ordinary archive work, those are separate evidentiary objects. A responsible investigator keeps them separate even when they happen to agree.

That is one reason this research belongs in Final Vestige’s archive. Historical mysteries are often told as if a witness statement were a camera. It is closer to a dated document: useful, situated, sometimes mistaken, and open to independent checking.

The [Puységur Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) reconstruct what late-eighteenth-century observers claimed about magnetic somnambulism, how they recorded the performances and what evidence might establish their proposed causes. The collection’s **153 source rows and 16 case dossiers** describe its research inventory; they do not validate claims of magnetic treatment. [6]

We cannot retroactively diagnose Puységur’s subjects with choice blindness. The historical people did not participate in Stille’s experiment. The useful parallel is methodological: a reported experience is not automatically proof of a theory explaining it, and a later account should not silently overwrite the earlier record.

## How this differs from the portrait experiment

Our previous unpublished choice-blindness research traces a concealed portrait switch, a marketplace taste test, eyewitness identification and later explanations of choices. The **new evidentiary contribution here** is the paper’s distinctive combination of a manipulated *record about a witnessed event* and a subsequent *memory test*.

A participant who says why they picked the wrong portrait has described a substituted choice. A participant who later answers a question about a filmed event in the direction of an altered answer has supplied a changed memory report. The latter involves an additional step. The two outcomes are related, but one does not automatically imply the other.

The earlier manuscripts, *The Choice You Didn’t Make* (R259) and *The Choice You Never Made* (R293), overlap enough that a public editor should not publish them both under separate near-identical URLs. This piece is intended as a study-specific companion or integrated research section after a human editor selects one canonical choice-blindness explainer. It does not authorize a third redundant URL.

## A non-deceptive two-column record test

Here is a free exercise for thinking about **evidence**, not for testing whether someone is suggestible. It does not change anyone’s memories.

Write down a small observation: the arrangement of books on a shelf, the position of a chair or the order of three objects on a desk. Date it. On a new line, record your confidence in that observation. Then check the scene again, making a separate note of what has changed, if anything. Never erase the first observation.

Make a simple two-column table: *first record* on the left, *later observation* on the right. If the descriptions conflict, label the conflict. You do not have to explain it away. The exercise demonstrates a sound documentation habit, not a validated intervention for memory, concentration or wellbeing.

This is the same distinction Final Vestige asks readers to practice when reading an extraordinary archival claim: separate what somebody wrote from what the underlying record establishes.

## The marketing leap that the research does not allow

It would be easy to turn these findings into a product pitch: if the mind can accept a changed answer, perhaps an audio recording can “reprogram” beliefs, concentration, health or financial success. **That conclusion does not follow.**

The 2017 researchers did not evaluate Zen12, Hypnosis Live, Brainwave Shots or any retail meditation recording. Their participants did not buy a guided audio course as the experimental treatment. A finding about manipulated questionnaires cannot serve as product-specific evidence of an audio program’s efficacy.

FTC guidance explains that advertisers must substantiate not only literal claims but also the implications that reasonable readers take from an advertisement. Product-adjacent science that makes a reader *infer* an unsupported benefit is still an advertising concern. [7,8]

A buyer might reasonably choose guided audio for convenience, preference or a routine. Those are product-format and personal-choice questions, not scientific demonstrations that a recording can replicate a controlled laboratory effect.

The [free Seven Day Focus Fieldbook](https://finalvestige.com/focus/) offers a twelve-minute starting routine and a 30-day log for people who want to observe their own habits without buying an audio program. Its presence here is an existing free editorial resource, not a medical recommendation or a duplicate email capture.

## The claim that survives

The most revealing part of this experiment is not that people can be fooled. It is that researchers were able to separate an earlier recorded answer from a later explanation and then measure what participants reported remembering.

A manipulated record gained apparent authority because it looked like the participant’s own. Sometimes the participants detected the substitution; sometimes they did not. Under the 2017 study conditions, later answers often moved toward the undetected manipulation. Both the positive result and its boundaries deserve to remain in the story.

An archive protects its first draft because a convincing second draft may not tell you what was originally there.
