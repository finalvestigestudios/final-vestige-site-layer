# Can a Sound Make You Concentrate? The Evidence Behind Brainwave Audio

**Final Vestige Studios · Mind & Perception · Original investigation · Draft / unpublished · 13 November 2026**

**Standfirst.** An audio file might make a work session more pleasant. That is not the same as showing that a frequency puts the brain into a promised state—or that it makes someone more productive. Three sets of research results show why this distinction matters.

## The promise arrives before the experiment

Imagine a person with a half-finished application, fourteen browser tabs and a task they have postponed all week. They want a way to begin. A product page promises that a short audio recording can switch on exceptional concentration. Another promises that a twelve-minute recording is comparable to much longer meditation. Neither promise is ordinary entertainment language: both invite the reader to expect a measurable change in mental performance.

The attractive part is not necessarily the technology. It is the possibility of removing a decision. Put on headphones, press play, and the difficult first step becomes someone else's responsibility. This is exactly why the evidence deserves to be inspected with more care than the sales headline.

We are not reviewing a device we have tested. We have not listened to the paid products, measured a purchaser's attention, checked a seller's private trial dossier or verified an affiliate account. We are comparing what the sellers actually describe with research on nearby phenomena—and showing where those two records fail to meet.

## First, what is a binaural beat?

In the binaural-beat paradigm, two slightly different pure tones are delivered separately to the ears, and listeners can perceive a rhythmic beat corresponding to their difference. That phenomenon is real enough to study. The disputed leap is that a perceived rhythm reliably forces neural oscillations into the corresponding frequency, and that this in turn produces a specific mental benefit.

Those are three different questions: Can a person perceive the beat? Does a brain recording show a replicable frequency-following effect under particular conditions? Does the exposure produce a useful behavioral change outside the laboratory? A result at one level does not prove the next.

There is another complication: *brainwave audio* is a marketing umbrella, not a single standardized stimulus. Binaural beats, monaural beats, pulsed tones, nature recordings, guided instructions and other audio arrangements need not work in the same way. It is a category error to borrow evidence for a binaural-beat experiment and present it as evidence that every product mentioning brainwave sounds works.

## A positive meta-analysis and a stubborn contradiction

A 2022 meta-analysis by Garcia-Argibay and colleagues examined fifteen studies of binaural beats on memory and attention, assembling 31 effect sizes. It reported an overall statistically significant medium-sized pooled effect, Hedges' g approximately 0.40. That finding is worth reading—not quietly ignoring because it complicates a skeptical story. But even its abstract reports conflicting results, especially for frequency-specific memory and attention tasks. The included studies were not trials of the exact retail recordings on this page. [1]

In 2023 Ingendoh, Posny and Heine asked a narrower physiological question: do binaural beats entrain measured electrical activity in the brain? Their PLOS ONE systematic review included fourteen EEG studies. Five reported findings consistent with the entrainment hypothesis, eight reported contrary findings, and one was mixed. Experimental methods, frequencies, EEG measures and analyses varied enough to limit direct comparison. This does not prove that no beat could ever affect an EEG signal. It does mean that a categorical claim of reliable entrainment is stronger than that reviewed evidence can carry. [2]

A separate 2023 experiment examined 58 healthy adults performing an attention-network task while exposed to 40-Hz binaural beats or a control condition. The authors reported no significant improvements in reaction times, errors, the tested attention networks or self-rated anxiety under their procedures. It is a bounded null finding, not a verdict on every audio product and not necessarily a test of other beat frequencies. Its practical value is that it directly asks for a behavior, not just an interesting signal from an EEG. [3]

Then a 2024 systematic review of 31 studies considered music, ambient sounds, noise and binaural beats in relation to autonomic arousal and cognitive performance. The authors found mixed or insufficient evidence to reach firm conclusions about the proposed arousal–performance mechanism. Many studies measured autonomic responses and cognitive outcomes separately, making causal stories especially vulnerable to overstatement. [4]

Here is the crucial distinction: **mixed research is not the same as evidence for every benefit, and a negative trial is not evidence that every listener will dislike an audio file**.

## What the actual product pages say—and do not establish

The public Inspire3 website groups Zen12, Hypnosis Live and Brainwave Shots under its self-development brands. [5] They should not be folded into a single imaginary product.

**Zen12** sells a structured library of twelve-minute audio sessions in multiple listening styles. On the official seller page checked on 13 November 2026, Beginner described the first six levels and 24 MP3s, Premium all twelve levels and 48 MP3s, and Advanced the last six levels and 24 MP3s. Premium was advertised at $87.99, but an advertisement is not proof of an identical checkout total, applicable taxes, availability or the customer's final payment. The site also represented its audio as delivering an hour's meditation benefits in twelve minutes. No product-specific trial sufficient to substantiate that equivalence was available in the materials independently reviewed for this investigation. [6]

**Hypnosis Live: Total Focus** is a seller-described 33-minute, 47-MB guided hypnosis MP3 recorded by J. A. Amos. The page shows a $17.95 sale price and describes a fourteen-day refund policy. Its claims about exceptional concentration and brain reprogramming are advertising claims, not outcomes that our review has verified for the recording. The product is different in form and intention from the multilevel Zen12 library. [7]

**Brainwave Shots: Focus Shot** is advertised as a package containing fifteen- and thirty-minute sessions, additional brainwave-only versions and a quick-start video. Its official page describes a protocol alternating between 12 and 18 Hz, with ambient mountain sounds and brown noise in the regular mix. The page says headphones are not required. That fact alone is an important warning against assuming Focus Shot is the same stimulus as the two-ear binaural beats used in the studies above. Its claimed four-hours-to-all-day effect on focus is a seller promise, not a demonstrated duration we can attribute to the product. A $17.99 seller-page price and one-year guarantee were displayed; neither substitutes for verified checkout and binding refund terms. [8]

The relevant buying question is therefore not "Is brainwave science proven?" It is: "Which precise audio program, which claimed outcome, which test, in which people, with what comparison, actually supports this purchase decision?"

## The historical control: a tree that never needed to work

In 1784 a commission including Benjamin Franklin investigated the practice of animal magnetism. A young subject was led blindfolded among trees. One tree had been supposedly magnetized; the child reported escalating effects at other trees and experienced a crisis before reaching the designated one. The commissioners treated the mismatch as evidence against the proposed invisible magnetic fluid. The original report and Final Vestige's sourced account distinguish what the commissioners observed from their inference about imagination and expectation. [9]

That is not an eighteenth-century controlled trial of audio, and it would be intellectually lazy to say a modern MP3 is just Mesmer's tree. But the research question travels well: if a person is told a procedure should do something, how will we distinguish their expectation, the surrounding ritual, the passage of time and the actual proposed mechanism?

Puységur's later archive exposes a further problem with relying only on a strong paper trail. His 1785 sequel contains cases, witnesses, signed acts, contradictions and notes about operator authority. Those documents help us reconstruct the history. They do not turn animal magnetism into established physiology. The archive is especially careful about patients' consent and pressure from investigators. [10]

A company can document that an audio product has been downloaded a million times, that users report enjoying it, or that someone felt more focused while listening. None of those facts, on its own, isolates a causal mechanism. The quality of the measurement matters more than the drama of the story.

## A fair free demonstration, without a magic score

Before purchasing an audio to help you start tasks, try an inexpensive baseline that measures an outcome you care about rather than a mystical state.

Choose a task small enough to make visible progress in twelve minutes: draft the first paragraph of a letter, classify ten receipts, or outline a difficult reading. Write down one tangible output target and the time you intend to start. Silence alerts, set a twelve-minute timer, and begin without an audio intervention. When time ends, record what you actually completed, how often you switched tasks and whether you would willingly repeat this approach tomorrow.

Repeat on a comparable task on another day if you want a rough personal baseline. Do not mistake one better afternoon for a causal experiment: sleep, task difficulty, motivation and setting may all differ.

If you later buy or otherwise obtain an audio you are authorized to use, compare what you produce and how tolerable the process feels. Keep the conditions as similar as you reasonably can. You could enjoy the audio yet gain no measurable output; you could finish more because it masks an irritating background noise rather than "entrains" any brainwave. Neither result is a diagnosis or a scientific trial.

## What would change our evaluation?

Product-specific research with a preregistered protocol; a credible control condition matched for expectations and audio pleasantness; sample sizes that support a meaningful difference; openly defined attention and task-completion outcomes; adverse-event reporting where relevant; and replication by researchers without a direct commercial interest would improve the evidential picture.

For an individual buyer, the immediate criteria are less grand: clear audio contents, truthful seller identity, stable transaction price, accessible sample, purchase and refund conditions, a straightforward way to stop unwanted messages, and whether the exercise actually suits them. A relaxation recording can be a valuable personal choice without proving a dramatic neural mechanism.

One caution about safety: 'completely safe' is too strong to repeat as an independent statement. People with sound sensitivities, unpleasant reactions or relevant health concerns should make personal choices with appropriate advice and should stop an exercise that makes them uncomfortable. Audio is not a replacement for medical assessment or treatment.
