The experiment that made a hand feel like somebody else's

In 1998, Matthew Botvinick and Jonathan Cohen described an experiment in which a researcher's brushes touched a concealed actual hand and a visible rubber hand in synchrony. Some participants reported an impression of ownership of the model. Later research debated how best to measure that ownership and whether expectation influences some responses. A fascinating observation, but neither the procedure nor the debate tested Zen12 or any commercial recording.

The historically documented Puységur magnetism archive poses a related question of evidence: witness testimony is a valuable record of what was said and observed, but does not itself prove a proposed force. Modern studies require their own measurements and controls. Neither archive nor illusion may be used as a shortcut to claim an audio product works.

A genuinely free twelve-minute baseline

Use a clock of your choice. Minutes 0–2: choose one small task and write what starting means. Minutes 2–8: do the task, making a simple mark if distracted. Minutes 8–10: note actual output rather than how 'reprogrammed' you felt. Minutes 10–12: identify other plausible reasons for your result, such as noise, task interest or fatigue. This is a personal observation practice, not a validated treatment, brainwave measurement or promise of better productivity.
