# The Gorilla in the Scan: Why Looking Is Not the Same as Noticing
### An unexpected figure in a medical image reveals the narrowness of attention—and the danger of treating a vivid impression as a complete record.

**Final Vestige Studios · Mind & Perception · Original evidence investigation · UNPUBLISHED draft · 9 November 2026**

There was a gorilla in the scan. It was not hidden in a maze of pixels, disguised as a malignant lesion, or tucked into the corner of an obscure research figure. The researchers deliberately inserted the unmistakable shape into a computed-tomography image that trained radiologists were examining for lung nodules. The report's roughly forty-eight-fold comparison refers to the **volume of the rectangular box enclosing the inserted gorilla** (more than 7,400 mm³) relative to the average nodule volume (153 mm³)—not its height, pixel area, or clinical abnormality. The paper spells out the denominator on page 1849. [1] Yet most of the experts did not report seeing it. [1]

This is the sort of experiment that becomes instantly memorable—and instantly distorted. A headline about professionals “missing a gorilla” can make them sound incompetent, as though the experiment were a competition anyone paying attention ought to win. It was not. The clinicians had been given a legitimate specialist task. They were scanning for nodules. The unexpected image was a deliberately conspicuous object that did not belong to the category they had been instructed to find.

That distinction changes what the experiment means. The failure was not necessarily poor vision, carelessness, or inadequate professional training. It was a demonstration that **what we look at and what we consciously register can be different things**. It also raises an unsettling question for anyone who studies historical evidence: when a witness says they saw the whole scene, what actually reached awareness?

## One task, one astonishing addition

In 2013, Trafton Drew, Melissa L.-H. Võ and Jeremy Wolfe published *The Invisible Gorilla Strikes Again* in *Psychological Science*. Their study asked whether inattentional blindness—previously demonstrated with unfamiliar visual tasks—would appear among experts examining images in their own specialty. Twenty-four radiologists performed a familiar lung-nodule search. The researchers included a gorilla in the final case and tracked gaze. Twenty of the 24 (about 83 percent) did not report the gorilla when questioned about the case. Of those 20, **12 had fixated its location while the figure was visible**. All 24 recognized the gorilla when shown the relevant still image afterward. These are task-level findings, not a diagnosis of why any individual missed it. [1] [1]

That percentage describes **this sample, this image arrangement, and this task**. It is not an estimate that 83 percent of radiologists would miss all abnormalities in actual clinical practice. The gorilla was not a malignancy, and researchers were not measuring patient outcomes or setting a standard for diagnostic competence. A controlled unusual-object test can reveal a perceptual limitation without quantifying how often a physician fails a medical obligation.

The eye-tracking detail is more surprising than the headline. A gaze trace puts the eyes in the neighborhood of an object. It does not certify that the object entered conscious awareness. Fixation, recognition and later report are different measures. From the experiment alone we cannot assign a single mechanism to each individual's failure: perhaps the visual information was not selected for further processing; perhaps it did not consolidate into an accessible report; perhaps expectations about the task mattered in a way not individually measured. The paper's observed behavior is stronger evidence than a tidy story about what “the brain refused to see.”

This result also invites a moral error: ridicule. People are not defective for having selective attention. The human visual system does not maintain an exhaustive inventory of everything entering the eye. It continually extracts information relevant to what an observer is doing. A specialist may be especially practiced at one kind of selection. That does not make an expert immune to an unexpected object outside the designated search.

## The earlier gorilla was moving

The medical scan was a follow-up to an experiment reported by Daniel Simons and Christopher Chabris in 1999. Their paper, *Gorillas in Our Midst*, examined sustained inattentional blindness while people monitored a dynamic scene. Participants devoted attention to one part of a passing-task sequence, while an unexpected event appeared. The published abstract emphasizes two factors affecting detection: how similar the unexpected object was to the attended objects and how demanding the monitoring task was. It reports that spatial proximity alone did not account for noticeability. [2]

A popular shorthand says that “half of people miss the gorilla.” That may describe an often-cited condition or familiar demonstration, but it is an unsafe universal rule. Results depend on what viewers are asked to count, which team or elements they follow, the unusual object and the display. This draft intentionally does not extend one remembered percentage to all versions of the experiment. For exact condition-level counts, the full original methods and results—not a viral recap—must be checked.

Why does task demand matter? Imagine trying to tally precise passes or movements while events cross paths on a screen. One activity asks for continuous, selective measurement. An unexpected figure asks for broad scene monitoring. A person can genuinely exert effort on the first task and still not report the second. The experiment's unsettling feature is not that observers were asleep. It is that active, goal-directed observation does not promise an exhaustive record.

For Final Vestige, this is where a scientific result becomes an investigative rule: **the absence of an observed event from a witness's account is not always evidence that the event did not happen**. But the reverse error matters too. Inattentional blindness does not entitle anyone to invent unseen events or dismiss every contradiction as a failure of attention. A missing observation is a question to investigate, not a license to supply the answer.

## When knowing the trick becomes another trap

By 2010, many viewers knew the gorilla demonstration. Simons studied whether familiarity with that kind of surprise helped people identify other unexpected events in a similar scene. In a new video, the gorilla was not the only change: a curtain changed color, and a player departed. Observers familiar with the gorilla demonstration reliably spotted the familiar surprising object. Yet familiarity did not establish an advantage in detecting the **other** surprises. In the full paper, 4 of 23 familiar viewers (17%) noticed either other change, versus 12 of 41 unfamiliar viewers (29%). **That between-group difference was not statistically significant.** The direction of the sample difference is not evidence that knowing the gorilla demonstration makes perception worse. [3] [3]

That result matters because a common response to any illusion is simply, “Now I know; next time I will look harder.” Awareness of one expected surprise is not the same thing as a general safeguard against all surprises. A viewer who searches for the gorilla may still miss the changing curtain.

The finding should not be inflated into a universal claim that expertise never helps, warning always hurts, or attention training is ineffective. The study tested a specific form of prior familiarity in a particular demonstration, not every instructional intervention or real-world observation task. Its value is narrower and more interesting: recognizing a famous example does not abolish the underlying selection problem.

## Different failures, different experiments

Final Vestige already has a published investigation of **change blindness**: one classic flicker experiment makes an alteration to a scene difficult to detect when a blank interval interrupts the visible transition; a separate real-world experiment substitutes one conversational partner for another as a door passes between them. That is not the same test as the unexpected gorilla. [4]

Change blindness asks whether a change between views is detected. Inattentional blindness asks whether an unexpected object or event is reported while an observer is occupied with a different task. Both challenge confidence in visual completeness, but treating them as interchangeable would obscure the actual methods. The existing change-blindness demonstration uses its own original artwork and explicitly identifies differences from the lab conditions. A future gorilla companion should likewise avoid borrowing copyrighted research footage or representing a custom graphic as a standardized experiment. **Editorial collision:** the earlier unpublished R227 manuscript, *The Man in the Suit Who Was Never Seen*, already covers the 1999 passing game, 2010 familiarity sequel and 2013 radiology scan. Neither R227 nor this R239 manuscript has canonical publication approval. The editor should choose one, reconcile any unique evidence and suppress the other; two competing gorilla articles would dilute both search intent and research provenance.

Nor is “looking without seeing” a medical diagnosis. Neither the old passing-task studies nor the radiologist study supplies a clinical screen for a reader's attention, memory, neurodivergence, or mental health. Someone who notices every unusual object in a short online test has not proved that their perception is complete. Someone who misses one has not failed an assessment.

## What the experiment does—and does not—tell a buyer

Attention is a compelling subject for commercial audio programs. Some products promise deep focus, automatic meditation, or the ability to direct mental performance by listening to engineered sounds. Those claims can sound more plausible when placed next to research showing that attention has limits. But the studies described here did not evaluate Zen12, Brainwave Shots, Hypnosis Live, or any specific consumer recording. They used visual search, unexpected objects and task reports. **The existence of inattentional blindness cannot substantiate a product's promise to correct it.**

There is a legitimate, more modest buying question: might someone appreciate a structured audio routine, a guided recording or an assortment of sounds? Possibly. Preference and convenience are real purchasing criteria. They are not proof of superior clinical outcomes, better career prospects, or an “hour of meditation in twelve minutes.” Final Vestige's separate independent buyer guide must identify what the seller actually supplies and which claims remain marketing assertions; a purchase recommendation cannot inherit a scientist's authority merely because both subjects involve the mind. [5][6]

The distinction is vital to commercial trust. If a source establishes a product's length, format or included files, it supports a description of what is sold. If a source measures performance in an independent experiment on a different task or a different intervention, it does **not** become a product-specific effectiveness trial. If the source is the merchant, its guarantee must be labeled a seller promise until actual checkout and refund conditions are independently checked.

## A free exercise in the difference between seeing and recording

This is an observation exercise, not an attempt to replicate the gorilla experiment and not an assessment of cognitive ability.

Choose a normal photograph that you have permission to use: perhaps your own image of a tabletop, a bookshelf or a public square. For twenty seconds, write down all the objects that belong to one category—for example, everything with a rectangular shape. Then put the photograph away and make a second list of objects from a different category: items made of fabric, or objects located above waist height. Reopen the photograph and check both lists.

You may find that one kind of detail dominated your first inventory. You may also find that your second inventory includes things you had not written down, despite your sincere impression that you had carefully inspected the picture. Alternatively, you may notice no difference. **Neither outcome is evidence that the clinical or laboratory research has been reproduced.**

The useful habit is the method: state the task, record what you actually noticed, return to the evidence and do not retroactively treat later information as something you noticed at the time. That habit has a place in historical research, engineering investigations and everyday self-observation. It is available without buying an audio library.

## The unreliable witness is not a liar

The temptation after a startling psychological experiment is to choose between two theatrical conclusions: human perception is fundamentally worthless, or the experiment is a clever trick with no practical implication. The evidence permits neither extreme.

People successfully observe, identify and act on a remarkable amount of information. They also select it. Under task-specific conditions, an unexpected item may fail to reach reportable awareness—even when a gaze trace crosses its location. Familiarity with one surprise may guide us toward that surprise while leaving others unseen. Those are meaningful observations from controlled research, with clear boundaries.

A careful investigator does not treat every witness as unreliable in the same way. Instead, they ask what the witness was looking for, what conditions shaped the observation, and what independent records might corroborate or complicate it. An expert report deserves serious attention; it does not become exhaustive merely because the observer is an expert. The scientific studies do not grant permission to disregard experts, only a reason to remain specific about the task they were asked to perform.

The gorilla did not make the image incomprehensible. It exposed a difference between seeing, selecting and saying what was seen. That difference is where the investigation begins.
