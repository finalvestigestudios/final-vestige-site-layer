# The Frequency That Was Supposed to Fix Your Attention
## Isochronic tones, binaural beats, and the missing step between a signal and a result
*Final Vestige Studios | Mind & Perception | independent investigation and buying guide | draft only | 15 November 2026*

A number appears on a product page: **12–18 hertz**. It looks satisfyingly precise. Beside it is a promise about getting focused. A person who knows that electrical rhythms can be measured in the brain might connect the two: if attention has a rhythm, perhaps a recording at a chosen frequency can put the listener in that state. The seductive part is the leap. The number is real enough to print; the outcome requires an entirely different kind of proof.

Final Vestige has been following claims that acquired authority by sounding like instruments. In the [Puységur Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/), eighteenth-century witnesses signed statements, operators repeated demonstrations, and predictions were committed to paper. That strengthened the documentation of what people reported. It did not establish that a magnetic fluid caused the reported experience. A frequency, too, can be accurately specified without proving the explanation placed beside it.

The present case is an unusually instructive product comparison because **Brainwave Shots' own FAQ says its system uses isochronic tones**, whereas much of the widely shared modern literature involves **binaural beats**. Those phrases describe different kinds of auditory stimulation. Quoting a binaural-beat experiment as though it tested the Focus Shot recording would quietly swap the intervention under review. The mistake matters even if the literature on binaural beats were unanimous. It is not.

### First, the exact product

On the official [Focus Shot sales page](https://brainwaveshots.com/focus-shot), Inspire3's Brainwave Shots brand describes two lengths: 15 and 30 minutes, plus brainwave-only versions and supporting materials. The page says its 30-minute protocol alternates between **12 and 18 Hz**, described there as low-mid beta, alongside ambient mountain sounds and brown noise in the standard mix. The seller's statement is a description of its own advertised format, not an independently authenticated inspection of the actual MP3 waveform.

The [official FAQ](https://brainwaveshots.com/faq) identifies the company's approach as *isochronic tones* and says headphones are not required. It also claims that a session can shift someone's state, possibly for hours. The page displays an advertised one-year refund policy, with restrictions on the number of refunds and on some multi-item orders. These are seller statements. We have not bought, decoded, acoustically measured, or used the file; neither efficacy nor refund performance has been personally established.

An isochronic tone usually refers to a sound pulsed rhythmically on and off at a set rate. Binaural beats instead arise when slightly different tones are delivered separately to the left and right ears, resulting in a perceived beat related to their difference. A pure binaural-beat setup therefore requires distinct signals at each ear; that is why its experimental protocol cannot simply stand in for a speaker-compatible pulsed sound. Other auditory methods exist, including monaural beats and amplitude-modulated sound. The seller says Focus Shot is isochronic, but the full engineering specification of the delivered MP3 remains independently unmeasured.

The business implication is simple: **a study of binaural beats may help frame questions about auditory stimulation, but it does not establish the effect of Focus Shot's claimed isochronic format**. We must not silently translate one into the other.

### A result that seems to support the story

The strongest challenge to knee-jerk skepticism is that relevant research does contain positive results. Garcia-Argibay, Santed, and Reales pooled **22 binaural-beat studies** in a 2019 meta-analysis and reported an overall medium effect, **Hedges' g = 0.45**, across cognition, anxiety, and pain outcomes. The researchers also analyzed exposure timing and frequency as moderators. Readers deserve to know that result rather than being told no sound study has ever found an effect. [Study record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30073406/).

But an aggregate effect across varied endpoints is not a guarantee about an individual task, and a binaural-beat synthesis is not a trial of a particular marketed isochronic soundtrack. It also cannot tell us whether the observed effect on any given task is caused by frequency entrainment, improved arousal, masked distractions, expectation, or some combination. A result can be statistically interesting while the mechanism remains unresolved.

A 2022 published study by Robison and colleagues tested **16-Hz binaural beats** during sustained-attention tasks. In one experiment participants were faster than controls, but that effect was modest and the Bayes-factor model comparisons were not decisive about including the effect. The study is useful precisely because it limits the conclusion: a laboratory manipulation showed a possible attention change under particular conditions. It did not test an isochronic recording, the Focus Shot package, its 12–18-Hz switching protocol, or a general promise of all-day focus. [Study record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33978805/).

There is also a 2021 study that compared **40-Hz binaural and monaural beats** with pink noise in a small sample of first-year psychology students. Its attentional-task findings differed by stimulus, while the EEG analysis did **not confirm the proposed entrainment mechanism**. Because 40 Hz is not the seller's stated 12–18-Hz protocol, and monaural beats are not automatically equivalent to isochronic tones, it is another piece of contextual evidence, not a product validation. [Study record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34245340/).

A fair review holds both propositions at once: auditory stimulation can have measurable effects in some settings; the effects are not simple enough to license every branded promise.

### The review that makes the promise harder

Ingendoh, Posny, and Heine examined **14 EEG studies of binaural beats** in a 2023 systematic review. **Five** supported an entrainment interpretation, **eight** contradicted it, and **one** had mixed findings. The authors emphasized substantial differences in methods, stimulus delivery, design, and EEG analysis. This is a review of binaural-beat oscillatory activity, not of the isochronic Focus Shot track. Even a confident finding about brain activity would still need an additional, appropriately controlled test to show that a listener works better. [Systematic review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37205669/).

Another 2024 review examined **31 studies** of music, ambient sounds, noise, white noise, and binaural beats in relation to autonomic measures and cognitive performance. Its authors found the evidence mixed or insufficient to draw general conclusions about arousal and performance, in part because physiology and task outcomes were often evaluated separately. This is a more useful caution than a universal negative: auditory environments differ, people differ, and the measured outcomes differ. [Review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38458383/).

There are at least three gates between a specified tone and the seller's desired headline. First, is the sound in the purchased file actually encoded as advertised? That needs independent signal inspection. Second, does exposure reliably change the intended neural signal under plausible listening conditions? That needs appropriate measurements and a valid control. Third, does that measured change produce a meaningful, reproducible improvement in concentration compared with ordinary music, neutral sound, and silence? That needs behavioral outcomes, adequate samples, prespecified analyses, and replication. A successful first gate is not a substitute for the second or third.

The temptation is to compress these gates into an arrow: frequency → brainwave → productivity. Science rarely gives that arrow for free.

### A historical warning from Puységur

The [Puységur archive](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) contains an instructive source distinction. A signed statement about what a subject said and when can be genuine. The causal theory developed from those statements can still be mistaken. The archive preserves a failed prediction, contradictory wishes, operator pressure, and concerns about compliance. A better instrument for documenting a report does not automatically give the operator a better causal explanation.

In modern audio marketing, a technical frequency label can play the role of a notarial signature: reassuring, exact, and easy to point at. It may authenticate what the seller says it encoded. It does not independently authenticate what the audio does to a listener. Final Vestige's evidence rule is therefore not anti-sound or anti-meditation. It is anti-shortcut.

### A twelve-minute test that doesn't pretend to be a trial

Rather than buy an effect, first define what the effect would mean. Choose a safe, ordinary task requiring concentration: proofreading a page, sorting citations, reviewing notes, or organizing a desk. Write one outcome before starting—such as corrected errors, paragraph count, or pages carefully checked. Start a twelve-minute timer. Note how many times you interrupted yourself and whether you completed the specified work.

On another comparable day, repeat the task with a familiar non-commercial ambient sound or with a different sound condition, at a comfortable level. Keep the environment, workload, timing, and notes as similar as you reasonably can. Do not infer neural entrainment from comfort or from a productive afternoon. Repetition helps expose preference and distraction patterns, but a tiny unblinded personal comparison is **not** a randomized experiment and cannot demonstrate a product's causal benefit.

If a sound makes you uncomfortable, stop. The seller itself cautions against some uses and warns people with epilepsy not to use its programs; see the [Brainwave Shots FAQ](https://brainwaveshots.com/faq) for full wording and consult an appropriate clinician about individual medical concerns. No recording should be used while driving or when alertness is essential.

### The responsible purchasing question

The right question is not, "Is 16 Hz a real frequency?" Of course it is. Nor is the right question, "Has anyone ever found an auditory-stimulation effect?" Yes. The honest question is: **has this exact audio file, in this exact format, been independently compared against sensible controls for the particular outcome being promised?**

Our review has not identified such a validation for the exact Focus Shot, Zen12, or Total Focus files. That is a limit on the currently examined evidence, not a claim that the recordings cannot be enjoyable or helpful to any individual. People can reasonably value structure, pleasant sound, guidance, or convenience without treating those preferences as a clinical or cognitive guarantee.

If the business someday gains verified permission to refer readers, the recommendation must still make that separation visible. **Commercial approval would permit a correctly disclosed link; it would not upgrade the quality of the scientific evidence.** That distinction is the entire reason to maintain an independent Mind & Perception archive.
