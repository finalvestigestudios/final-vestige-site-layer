# The Label That Changed the Trance
## If a recording is called “hypnosis,” what changes before the recording even begins?

**Final Vestige Studios | Mind & Perception / History of Suggestion | Unpublished research manuscript | 16 November 2026**

*Editorial status: source-controlled draft, not fact-checked for publication or approved for CMS. No product recommendation in this investigation.*

A person sits in a research room and receives a fairly ordinary instruction: listen to a recording, then describe how deeply hypnotized you felt. But before the recording begins, there is another instruction—one the listener might barely notice. The researchers give the procedure a name.

In one condition, they describe an induction as hypnosis. In another, they introduce a different procedure as a non-hypnotic control. The names sound like labels for what is about to happen. In fact, the names are also part of what is being tested.

This is the elegant trap at the center of a 2025 experiment by Zoltan Kekecs and colleagues. The researchers were not trying to prove that hypnosis is fake, nor to establish that any recording has magical power. They were asking whether the *framing* of an experience and the *procedure* itself contribute differently to what people report. The experiment matters to anyone navigating the growing market for recordings that promise to reprogram the mind. It also speaks directly to a much older historical argument: when observers describe a striking experience, what exactly have they established?

### An experiment with the labels deliberately crossed

The study, published online on **7 November 2025** in *Psychophysiology*, enrolled hypnosis-naive participants in a randomized, balanced-placebo-style design. After exclusions, **46 people** were included in the analysis. Each heard four different six-minute induction recordings. Two were conventional inductions, using relaxation or confusion; two were unconventional procedures, including white noise and an embedded induction. Two were introduced as hypnotic procedures and two as non-hypnotic controls. Crucially, the labels were crossed with the types of recording, and their ordering was randomized.

Before each trial participants rated how deeply hypnotized they *expected* to feel. After the recording and a period of quiet rest, they rated how deeply hypnotized they *had felt*. Researchers also collected EEG data, but this was not a trial of any commercial meditation or hypnosis product.

Those details matter. A conventional induction introduced as a control is a different comparison from a conventional induction introduced as hypnosis. White noise introduced as hypnosis lets researchers ask a harder question: how much of the reported experience reflects the supplied interpretive frame, rather than the special structure of a conventional technique?

**Read the original methods and limits:** Kekecs et al., 2025, *Psychophysiology*, DOI [10.1111/psyp.70183](https://doi.org/10.1111/psyp.70183); [open research materials](https://osf.io/prscg/files/osfstorage).

### The result was not merely a laboratory curiosity

The word “hypnosis” changed expectations substantially. Averaged across induction types, a hypnosis label increased expected hypnotic depth by about **3.41 points** on a 0–10 scale relative to a control description. Reported hypnotic depth was also influenced by the label. The authors emphasize a qualification: the words themselves do not necessarily produce the experience; they operate through beliefs, expectations and context. Their results cannot be reduced to the slogan that “hypnosis is only suggestion.”

There was a second surprise. White noise could produce reports of hypnotic depth comparable to some conventional procedures, whereas one unconventional procedure did not perform equivalently. In the authors' discussion, these differences complicate *both* simple expectancy explanations and simple special-process accounts. If everything were just a label, every well-framed procedure would perform alike. If only conventional induction had unique power, ordinary white noise would be hard to explain. The actual pattern is messier.

A more complete account could involve expectation evolving during a session, attention shifting, an individual's hypnotic responsiveness, social context and characteristics of the stimulus. The authors present these as theoretical possibilities to investigate, not a final explanation.

There are severe generalization limits. The analyzed sample was **46**, most were young adults, hypnosis-naive and favorably disposed toward hypnosis, and the design used short laboratory inductions. It measured subjective hypnotic depth and electrophysiological correlates, not sustained work productivity, lasting behavior change, recovery from trauma, memory accuracy, prosperity or the benefits of specific retail recordings. A buyer should never see this study presented as proof that a particular merchant's MP3s work.

### When a name becomes part of the evidence

The difference between an *event* and its *interpretation* has occupied Final Vestige before. In 1784, the Marquis de Puységur described strange encounters with subjects he believed were responding to animal magnetism. His memoirs and subsequent documents accumulated witness accounts, statements, predictions, failures, and attempts at verification. Those are genuine historical materials about what observers said, did, recorded and believed. They do not independently verify the magnetic fluid theory that organized their accounts.

The published [Puységur Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) distinguish the survival of a source from the survival of its author's explanation. A reported phenomenon does not dissolve simply because one causal explanation fails; equally, the report does not authenticate the proposed mechanism.

One could imagine a subject in 1784 being told that a session involved an invisible magnetic influence and another being told that the same behavior was a form of rest. Such a counterfactual is **not** a description of an experiment Puységur actually performed. It is a way of recognizing the modern question that historical researchers could not settle with testimony alone: did the explanatory frame help shape what witnesses attended to and reported?

That distinction must remain clear. A modern randomized laboratory experiment does not travel backward in time to adjudicate every historical anecdote, and historical testimony is not contemporary evidence of a retail product's performance.

### Why “hypnosis is fake” is also the wrong headline

A useful skepticism tests specific claims rather than replacing them with an equally sweeping denial. A 2024 systematic review by Zahedi, Lynn and Sommer surveyed competing theories of hypnotic suggestion. It describes documented changes in reported perception, action, cognition and agency in experimental research, while emphasizing that there is no single settled explanation of how suggestions work. Different accounts illuminate different parts of the phenomenon.

A separate 2025 investigation into attention found, in a modestly sized experiment, that relaxation-based induction and an emotional-numbing suggestion could modulate emotional attentional bias among highly suggestible participants. That is an interesting result about the contribution of different components of a hypnotic context, not proof that any off-the-shelf recording changes attention for a typical purchaser.

Clinical hypnosis literature also cannot simply be transplanted into retail advertising. A study of hypnotic responses in a controlled environment, or evidence about a specific clinical intervention in a particular population, is not a transferable certificate for another method, audio track, dose or promised outcome. The FTC's health-products guidance emphasizes substantiation adequate for the *claim consumers actually take away*.

One especially risky promise would be that a recording recovers forgotten memories accurately. Hypnosis may alter confidence or subjective vividness without ensuring veracity. Another is that brainwave stimulation produces a medical result without product-specific clinical evidence. Replacing these with vague hints and testimonials would not resolve the problem: an implied benefit is still an advertising claim.

### An original twelve-minute observation, without a trance promise

You need no headphones, special soundtrack, personal disclosure or purchases. This is a self-observation exercise—not a hypnosis-induction study, diagnostic test or claim that you can reproduce the 2025 experiment.

**Minutes 0–2: Choose the frame.** On paper, write two possible descriptions of your next few minutes: “a short focused rest” and “a short exercise in noticing expectations.” Notice whether either phrase sets up an expectation. Keep the page private.

**Minutes 2–5: Sit or stand comfortably.** Let your gaze settle on a neutral, stationary point. Notice three sounds, two physical sensations and one thought. You may keep your eyes open and stop at any time. Do not attempt this while driving or operating equipment.

**Minutes 5–8: Change only the name.** Quietly describe the activity to yourself using the other phrase. Observe whether it changes what you *expect* to feel, even if the activity itself has not changed. Do not force a result. Sometimes nothing changes.

**Minutes 8–10: Separate report from explanation.** Write one sentence about what you noticed and a different sentence about what might explain it. Label the second as a hypothesis. Distinguish “I felt calmer” from “the wording caused my calm.”

**Minutes 10–12: Apply the consumer test.** Find an advertisement or promotional phrase for a meditation or hypnosis audio and ask: what does it say will happen, to whom, how quickly, and what study of that *specific product* supports the promise? Avoid adding a claimed result of your own.

This exercise cannot tell you whether you are hypnotizable, prove whether an audio works, or establish a scientific causal relationship. Its purpose is to practice the habit that makes the evidence more useful than a headline: separate a subjective observation, the proposed reason for it, and the evidence necessary to decide.

### The archival discipline that survives the experiment

Puységur's subjects, the twentieth-century laboratory, and contemporary retailers occupy very different contexts. But the same mistake threatens all three: a dramatic personal account becomes a sweeping explanation before the verification work has been done. A carefully designed study can isolate a label from a procedure, but even that investigation leaves difficult questions unanswered.

The more useful conclusion is not that words control our minds. It is that descriptions can shape expectations, which may shape reports of experience. The difference between what we experience and why we think it happened is where serious investigation begins.
