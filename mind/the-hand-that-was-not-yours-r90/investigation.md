# The Hand That Was Not Yours
*An evidence investigation into borrowed bodies, synchronized signals, and the uncomfortable power of expectation.*

**Editorial state: UNPUBLISHED.** This is an independent science-and-history investigation, not a product recommendation. No visitor-facing page or experiment is live.

A hand lies on a table. It has five fingers, a wrist, and the familiar shape of something that could belong to a person. But it does not. The person seated beside it has a real hand hidden behind a screen. An experimenter touches both hands in view of the participant's attention: the artificial one can be seen, the real one can be felt.

Now comes the strange part. For some participants, the artificial hand begins to seem like *their* hand.

In February 1998, Matthew Botvinick and Jonathan Cohen published a one-page report in *Nature* with a deceptively plain title: “Rubber hands ‘feel’ touch that eyes see.” Their work described a way to pull vision, touch, and knowledge of limb position into conflict. Its importance was not that an inanimate object had somehow acquired sensation. It was that the participant's experience of ownership could shift while the real anatomy remained unchanged. [1]

A century of investigations into suggestion had already asked how people come to experience what they expect. The rubber-hand experiment added a different kind of evidence: a precisely arranged disagreement among the senses. It also opened a dispute that is still scientifically productive. When someone tells an experimenter, “That felt like my hand,” how much is being changed by sensory integration, how much by expectation, and how do you tell?

## 1 | Two hands, one carefully arranged discrepancy

Imagine an overhead view of the original arrangement. To the left is the participant's real hand, hidden behind a barrier. Nearby, in a plausible anatomical position, a rubber hand lies where the participant can watch it. A person holding two brushes delivers touches to the hidden and visible hands. Timing is the experimental lever.

The synchronization matters because brains constantly reconcile information from different sources. Vision informs us where a hand appears; touch tells us that something contacted our skin; proprioception—information about the body's position—helps place the actual limb in space. Signals that coincide in time and space can be treated as evidence that they arise from the same object.

Botvinick and Cohen reported both questionnaire responses and a reaching measure in their short publication. The article's abstract identifies a three-way interaction among vision, touch, and proprioception. [1] This is the narrow, defensible foundation: under experimentally arranged conditions, some participants can report feelings of ownership toward an artificial limb, along with related changes in judgments of hand location. It is not evidence that ownership is entirely invented, that everyone experiences the illusion, or that suggestion by itself can command the body.

A viral retelling often skips the more demanding question: **what, precisely, is the control condition?**

If touches to the two hands are badly out of step, a participant may be less inclined to treat them as the same event. That makes asynchronous stroking a useful comparison. But if participants can guess which condition *ought* to be uncanny, ratings can reflect something besides the bodily process researchers hoped to isolate. The answer cannot simply be “ask people whether they felt it,” because that is the measure under dispute.

## 2 | A mechanical trick—or a prediction about causes?

In 2015, Majed Samad, Albert Jin Chung, and Ladan Shams offered a computational account of the phenomenon in *PLOS ONE*. Their research modeled body ownership as a problem of causal inference: does this visible hand and the information from my hidden limb plausibly come from one source? Their model predicted that visual and position information might sometimes be sufficient to trigger the illusion even without touch, while synchronized stroking could strengthen it. In two experiments, the authors found evidence consistent with those predictions. [2]

That result complicates the familiar explanation that the illusion is produced by synchronized brushes alone. It suggests that apparent anatomical plausibility and the brain's assessment of common causes may also matter.

A causal-inference account is not a mind-reading machine. It is a model tested against observations. It makes predictions about how changing sensory relations should change reported ownership. Other models can fit parts of the same data; no one study turns a theoretical account into universal law.

Even the outcome measures are not identical. A participant may report that a rubber hand feels like theirs, and independently estimate that the position of their hidden hand has shifted toward it. The second measure is often called *proprioceptive drift*. A difference in reported ownership is not automatically a difference in drift.

In a 2023 meta-analytic paper, Giorgia Tosi, Benedetta Mentesana, and Daniele Romano examined the relationship between subjective embodiment and proprioceptive drift because earlier research had produced both positive and null correlations. [3] Treating the two measures as interchangeable erases a real methodological issue. For Final Vestige, this is a familiar archival problem: two records that seem to refer to the same event can describe different aspects of it.

## 3 | The participant knows the script

There is another line of evidence, and it deserves room rather than a footnote. In 2020, Peter Lush published *Demand Characteristics Confound the Rubber Hand Illusion* in *Collabra: Psychology*. Participants in that study learned about experimental procedures by reading and watching demonstrations, then predicted the experiences that the procedures would provoke. Crucially, they were not undergoing the tactile illusion in the ordinary manner while giving those expectancy judgments.

Lush reported that predicted experience differed across synchronous and asynchronous conditions in ways that resembled differences in ordinary illusion reports. His argument was direct: the standard comparison between simultaneous and unsynchronized touch might not adequately control for what participants think the experiment is supposed to show. That possibility should be treated seriously. [4]

But it should also be described accurately. An expectancy demonstration **does not establish that all genuine rubber-hand experiences are merely compliance, imagination, or deception**. It challenges whether a widely used control isolates the proposed sensory mechanism. It asks researchers to improve the comparison conditions and to distinguish experiences, interpretations, and reporting incentives.

A 2024 replication and extension revisited these expectancy effects. The researchers reported a replication in Japanese samples and identified an important influence of condition order: what a participant had already seen shaped what they anticipated next. They recommended better-matched controls and more attention to ordering effects. They also acknowledged limits on generalization beyond the populations studied. [5]

This is an unusual scientific story because both lines of inquiry may illuminate the same thing. Sensory information can change the perceived location or ownership of a limb; expectations can shape reports, and perhaps subjective experience itself. Disentangling these possibilities requires experimental design, not a declaration that one camp has won.

## 4 | Puységur and the problem of the witness

The name Armand-Marie-Jacques de Chastenet, Marquis de Puységur, belongs to an earlier and very different research landscape. His eighteenth-century accounts of animal magnetism are part of a historical record in which observers described extraordinary states of responsiveness and suggestion. Final Vestige's published *Puységur's Animal Magnetism Files* examines what those records can—and cannot—establish.

It would be historically careless to treat Puységur's subjects as participants in modern multisensory neuroscience. It would be scientifically careless to infer from the rubber-hand illusion that animal magnetism worked. Those are separate claims, separated by time, procedures, concepts, and evidentiary standards.

What they share is an interpretive problem. A witness can describe an experience sincerely and still be mistaken about why it happened. An investigator can observe a striking response and still lack a reliable comparison. A participant's expectation can become part of the conditions producing the report.

The appropriate response is neither to dismiss every report nor to promote it into a supernatural fact. It is to ask which observations are independently checkable, what alternative causes have been considered, and what the experiment would predict if the favorite explanation were wrong.

That is the actual bridge between historical investigation and modern cognitive research: not “the ancients knew neuroscience,” but the recurring human difficulty of separating **what was experienced** from **what caused it**.

## 5 | What a rubber hand cannot prove

The illusion is sometimes folded into sweeping claims that identity is a fiction or that a person can rewrite bodily reality by believing hard enough. The experiments do not support those claims. They show that some dimensions of experienced ownership are malleable under particular laboratory conditions. They leave many questions about mechanism, individual differences, and measurement.

Nor is the experiment an argument for commercial meditation, hypnosis, binaural audio, subliminal messages, or “rewiring” products. A rubber hand says nothing about the efficacy of Zen12—or any other specific recording. Connecting them as if one validates the other would be exactly the mistake this archive exists to resist.

What, then, is the evidence? There is an original laboratory report [1]. There is a predictive causal-inference model and experimental testing [2]. There are attempts to compare different embodiment measures [3]. And there are expectancy and replication studies challenging the sufficiency of familiar controls [4–5]. They are related, not interchangeable.

A photograph of the apparatus would show one arrangement. A participant's statement records an experience. A chart of responses documents measured reports. A theory explains why those measurements changed. Each is a different layer of the story.

## 6 | A no-purchase field exercise: your eyes make an assumption

You do not need to imitate the original experiment to learn its central lesson. This exercise measures no ownership illusion and is not a medical or scientific test. Its purpose is to practice separating a visible cue from the conclusion you draw from it.

On paper, make three columns: **What I saw**, **What I inferred**, **What else could explain it?** Think of a recent ordinary moment when two events happened close together—for instance, a notification appeared just as you thought of someone, or a door moved as a person entered a room.

Write a literal account in the first column, avoiding words such as “because,” “made,” or “intended.” In the second, write your instant explanation. In the third, list at least one competing explanation and one observation that could distinguish them.

That small division between record and explanation is the fieldbook habit. It is also the habit missing from a hundred overconfident retellings of perception illusions.

## 7 | The evidence, in plain view

The rubber-hand story has not ended with a single impressive demonstration. The original experiment inspired competing models and sharper questions about how to measure ownership without planting the answer in advance.

A claim about a body, a memory, or a strange historical occurrence can feel convincing. The task of an evidence archive is not to borrow that conviction. It is to test its foundation.

**The hand did not change owners. What changed was the evidence the mind was prepared to call its own.**
