# The Hand That Wasn't Yours: When Touch, Vision, and Suggestion Disagree
**Final Vestige Studios | Mind & Perception / Evidence & History | UNPUBLISHED EDITORIAL DRAFT | 10 November 2026**

*Proposed search focus: rubber hand illusion, is the rubber hand illusion hypnosis, hypnotic suggestion versus perception. Editorial status: sources checked; independent duplicate-title audit and founder approval pending. The article is not a product review.*

A hand rests on a table. You can see its fingers and knuckles, but it is not attached to you. Your own hand is concealed. A researcher brushes the hand in front of you while an unseen brush touches your real hand in the same rhythm. After a while, an ordinary sentence becomes an extraordinary report: *that fake hand feels like mine.*

The scene sounds like a demonstration of how easily we can be fooled. But that description gives away too much. It assumes that the participant is being deceived, that everyone experiences the same effect, and that we already understand why it happens. None of those assumptions follows automatically from the experiment.

This is the rubber hand illusion, an influential experiment about the brain's sense of the body's boundaries. It is also the center of a scientific disagreement with an unexpectedly historical question: when an experimental setup strongly suggests what someone might experience, how can we distinguish changes in perception from changes in expectation?

## The experiment behind the familiar video

In 1998, psychologists Matthew Botvinick and Jonathan Cohen described an illusion in which touch appeared to be attributed to a foreign hand. Their short *Nature* correspondence called attention to a three-way relationship between vision, tactile sensation, and proprioception—the internal information involved in sensing where body parts are located [1].

The usual demonstration supplies three kinds of information. The participant *sees* touch applied to a plausible hand. The participant *feels* a matching touch on an actual hand hidden from view. And proprioceptive signals still locate the actual hand elsewhere. The system has to resolve that disagreement.

When seen and felt touches are synchronized, some participants report a sense of ownership of the fake hand, or a shift in where they believe their real hand is located. Researchers call that latter measurement **proprioceptive drift**. These are related measures, not interchangeable ones: a questionnaire about ownership asks what a person experiences, while a location judgment quantifies a different aspect of the response. Neither, by itself, proves that a person's body representation has changed in every possible sense.

In the laboratory, an asynchronous condition often supplies a comparison: what happens if the visible and felt touches arrive at different times? Many experiments have shown stronger reports in synchronous conditions. But this contrast also creates a new question. A participant can notice the matching rhythm, and matching rhythm might itself suggest that the visible hand ought to belong to them.

An investigation that never asks about expectation can risk mistaking the strength of a report for certainty about its cause.

## Three statements that sound alike but are not

Watch how quickly a dramatic demonstration can turn into a claim:

1. **Observation:** some people report that a rubber hand feels like their own during synchronized stimulation.
2. **Interpretation:** the brain integrates information from sight, touch, and body position to generate a sense of ownership.
3. **Unwarranted leap:** a video, voice recording, or suggestion can therefore make people accept any new belief, habit, or command.

The original experiment supports investigating the first two propositions. It does not remotely establish the third. A laboratory manipulation of body ownership is not an experiment on commercial self-hypnosis, memory rewriting, guaranteed focus, or medical treatment.

If you have seen a rubber-hand demonstration on social media, notice which sentence its narrator uses. A good demonstration makes a phenomenon visible. A reliable investigation asks what the evidence can and cannot settle.

## Can an expectation produce the experience?

In September 2020, Peter Lush and colleagues reported a series of studies of a trait they called **phenomenological control**: the capacity to experience changes that fit imaginative suggestions or perceived task expectations. Their work also examined mirror-sensory experiences and the rubber hand illusion [2].

The paper included studies involving 156, 404, and 353 participants. The authors found relationships between measures of hypnotizability and several subjective rubber-hand measures. Their interpretation was that differences in how people respond to implicit suggestion could be influencing what researchers had taken to be primarily multisensory effects.

There was an important caveat *inside their own paper*. A preregistered attempt to manipulate what participants expected from synchronous versus asynchronous stimulation did not demonstrate the intended change in expectations. The authors therefore could not directly establish that the expectancy manipulation **caused** the rubber hand illusion. Some of the later evidence was correlational, and some of its analyses were not preregistered.

That matters. Correlation between hypnotizability and a rubber-hand measure does not mean that hypnosis explains the illusion, nor that a specific prerecorded suggestion will alter a person's sense of ownership.

In January 2022, Henrik Ehrsson and colleagues published a reanalysis of the 2020 data [3]. They argued that the relationship with hypnotic suggestibility disappeared when rubber-hand responses were quantified as the difference between synchronous and asynchronous conditions, consistent with the standard comparison used in the field. They emphasized that the existing evidence was more compatible with the multisensory interpretation than with a **specific** relationship to hypnotic suggestibility.

Lush and Anil Seth responded in the same journal [4]. Their reply continued to contest how suggestion-related response should be measured and interpreted. The disagreement did not vanish just because both sides cited the same dataset.

The dispute illustrates why research cannot be reduced to a clean percentage or a spectacular clip. The result depends partly on the quantity being measured. Does the experiment ask whether someone reports an experience after synchronized stroking? Does it subtract a control condition? Are measured expectations actually changed? How do investigators separate a real change in experience from a participant's interpretation of a questionnaire? Each choice can lead to a different answer.

**The responsible conclusion is narrower than either viral claim:** sensory integration clearly matters in body-ownership experiments; expectation and suggestion are legitimate possible contributors to subjective reporting; and the precise contribution of each remains a substantive methodological debate. This is not evidence that the illusion is entirely fake or entirely immune to expectation.

## What changed when gravity changed?

A 2022 open-access study by Bruno and colleagues investigated the rubber hand illusion in water immersion and parabolic flight settings [5]. The authors reported increased susceptibility under altered gravitational conditions and interpreted the findings as changes in the weighting of visual, proprioceptive, and vestibular information.

This is an illuminating complication. A purely verbal story about suggestion has difficulty standing alone when physical and sensory conditions vary. At the same time, findings in unusual settings do not allow us to conclude that all rubber-hand reports everywhere have a single explanation. The sample, protocol, and altered-gravity conditions place boundaries around what can be generalized.

The study is also a reminder that the body is not represented by sight and touch alone. Balance and orientation contribute information about how a body exists in the world.

A related 2022 study by Della Longa and colleagues examined how the intensity and qualities of touch could influence the illusion [6]. Differences in stimulation can change measured responses. Its use here is not to recommend painful stimulation: Final Vestige does **not** recommend replicating discomfort or threats in any at-home demonstration.

## A historical echo: Puységur's invisible explanation

In eighteenth-century France, Franz Anton Mesmer and Armand-Marie-Jacques de Chastenet, Marquis de Puységur, described striking experiences within the framework of animal magnetism. Their historical descriptions and the social conditions of their demonstrations are interesting sources for the history of suggestion. They are not independent confirmation that a magnetic fluid existed or that their proposed therapeutic mechanism worked.

Final Vestige's published [Puységur's Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) examines those records as history rather than a recipe for treatment. The relevant parallel is not that Puységur unknowingly discovered the rubber hand illusion. He did not. The parallel is an **evidence problem**: a striking subjective report can be sincere even when the proposed explanation is uncertain.

In a magnetism demonstration, the fact that someone reacted did not prove the supposed fluid. In a modern ownership experiment, the fact that someone describes a striking sensation does not automatically identify exactly which perceptual and expectational mechanisms produced it.

We should make two symmetrical mistakes difficult to commit. The first is dismissing an experience simply because the mechanism remains contested. The second is accepting a favored mechanism simply because the experience feels powerful.

This tension is precisely what makes both the historical archive and current experimental psychology worth examining.

## A demonstration you can do without manipulating anyone's senses

You do not need a prosthetic hand, brush, blindfold, startling threat, or staged 'hypnosis' demonstration to notice how interpretation enters an experiment. Try an **evidence-reading exercise** instead.

Read these three fictional statements one at a time:

> A. “The participant said the rubber hand felt like their own.”

> B. “After the procedure, the participant pointed closer to the rubber hand when locating their hidden hand.”

> C. “The participant believed the experiment would make them feel ownership of the rubber hand.”

Now identify the *measure* in each statement. A is a subjective report. B is a spatial-location judgment. C is a report of expectation. If the researcher recorded only A, could the result establish B? No. If A and C rose together, would C necessarily cause A? Also no. If B changed but A did not, should an article describe those outcomes as identical? Again no.

Finally ask: what would a rigorous control look like, and what might the participant expect in each version? This question does not produce a numerical illusion score. It trains the more valuable habit of distinguishing evidence, mechanism, and interpretation.

The exercise requires no audio, flashing images, bodily restraint, induced discomfort, or payment.

## Why the experiment still matters

The rubber hand illusion challenges the comfortable assumption that our sense of bodily ownership is a simple recording of what is physically attached to us. It shows why coherent experience can emerge from signals that do not perfectly agree.

The ensuing dispute does not make the original demonstration worthless. It makes the questions better. One group asks how the brain integrates different kinds of sensory evidence. Another asks how context and expectation can shape what observers experience and report. Both questions should be asked of the same experiments, with measures designed to distinguish their predictions.

A good investigation gives a reader a surprise and then a way to evaluate it. The surprise is the fake hand. The evaluation is to ask whether the measured result is ownership, localization, expectation, or some combination—and what the study actually tested.

**The most useful thing to take from the rubber hand illusion is not that minds can be controlled. It is that even an experience that feels unmistakably ours deserves careful explanation.**
