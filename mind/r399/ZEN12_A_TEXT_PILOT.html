The problem behind the promise

You sit down to do one task. A notification interrupts, then another, and an hour disappears. A short recording promises a simple solution. It's an appealing idea, and also a strong causal claim. Our Puységur source archive shows why even sincere eyewitness reports and notarized documents must not be confused with proof of a proposed mechanism. The same standard applies to commercial sound.

Try the free twelve-minute baseline

Write down one concrete task, your anticipated difficulty (1–5), and how many times you expect to switch away. Work for twelve minutes without paid audio. Record what you finished, interruptions, and difficulty. Repeat with a comparable task on another day if useful. This is personal reflection, not a blinded trial and not evidence that any recording changes the brain.

Scientific limit: The seller claims twelve minutes delivers the benefits of an hour of meditation, but studies of other meditation exercises and sound experiments don't validate these specific files. A 2021 controlled-intervention review found no significant pooled attention effect; a 2023 EEG review found inconsistent binaural-beat entrainment results. Other reviews report benefits in certain outcomes and settings. None of these studies directly tested Zen12 at its advertised comparison.

Five buyer objections worth taking seriously

  1. Why pay instead of using free audio? Format, organization and sound preference might matter, but are not proof of improved cognition.
  2. Does a frequency make my brain follow it? A number in a technical sheet is not an EEG result from the listener.
  3. How strong is the research? Ask for controlled product-specific trials, comparable listening dose and outcomes you care about.
  4. What about the refund? Verify the actual referred checkout terms, tax and eligibility. A seller promise is not a completed refund.
  5. Can I try something free first? Yes; the Fieldbook and the twelve-minute exercise stay available with no purchase.
