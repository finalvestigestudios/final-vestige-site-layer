# The Voice That Wasn't in the Recording

*You watch a mouth say one thing. Your ears receive another. A third sound seems to emerge.*

A film can preserve its soundtrack faithfully and still fail to preserve what the audience hears. This is not a trick about whether someone paid attention. Nor is it a story about a witness lying. It is a peculiar result of asking the human perceptual system to make sense of two incompatible signals that usually belong together.

In 1976 Harry McGurk and John MacDonald reported an experiment in *Nature* with a simple arrangement. They took footage of a speaker producing one syllable, combined it with sound from another, and showed the mismatch to observers. In one pairing, the lips articulated /ga/ while the soundtrack said /ba/. The listeners often reported something like /da/. The answer wasn't supplied by the soundtrack alone. It wasn't a verbatim report of the silent picture, either. It was the percept that emerged when the two were presented together. [1]

That makes the experiment particularly disquieting. We normally speak of seeing and hearing as if they were separate witnesses. One can corroborate the other. But the voices in our heads are not always independent testimony from ear and eye. What we call *what I heard* may already be an interpretation of what we saw.

A modern video promising to reveal the secret machinery of your mind may tell you the demonstration “works on everybody.” It doesn't. The scientific record is interesting precisely because it reports both the effect and the extraordinary variety of responses. There are people who hear the original soundtrack, people who report a fused syllable, and people whose answers change depending on the particular speaker, the lip movements or how the question is asked. [2][3]

And there is a larger boundary: an audiovisual illusion cannot show that a hypnosis download improves memory, that a brainwave soundtrack induces a clinically useful state, or that a twelve-minute meditation file recreates an hour of experienced practice. None of those conclusions was tested here.

## The problem was not the recording

McGurk and MacDonald were working at the University of Surrey, studying speech perception. A talking face offered a straightforward experimental question: when we listen to somebody speak, how much of our understanding comes from the sound and how much from the visible movement of the mouth?

It is easy to treat lip-reading as an emergency substitute for a damaged audio signal. We picture a noisy street or a silent movie and imagine that vision steps in when hearing fails. The 1976 experiment asked something more revealing. Would a visually incongruent mouth change what people *heard* even when the soundtrack was audible?

The researchers built mismatched audiovisual stimuli. A repeated /ba/ dubbed over lips saying /ga/ could prompt a reported /da/. The reverse pairing did not simply reverse the illusion. As the original abstract notes, some participants instead reported combination responses such as /bagba/ or /gaba/. That asymmetry is part of the finding, not a footnote to edit away. [1]

The controls were essential. When people heard the soundtrack without the conflicting picture, or watched an untreated recording, they reported the syllables differently. The researchers therefore had grounds to attribute the altered reports to the discordant pairing. They did **not** need to argue that ears were broken, that eyesight had hypnotized anyone, or that a mysterious hidden faculty had taken over.

The experiment had created a conflict in the evidence supplied to an ordinary speech-perception system.

John MacDonald revisited the work decades later, describing the development of the stimuli and the serendipitous nature of the discovery. That retrospective is valuable historical testimony by one of the original investigators, but it is not an additional independent replication. [4]

## Why /ba/ is not just a sound

Watch someone say /ba/ slowly. The lips come together before the voice reaches your ears. The closure is visible. It tells you something about the place in the mouth where the consonant is formed.

Now consider /ga/. The corresponding closure occurs farther back, where it is much less visible from outside the face. Both movements contribute to a stream of information about speech, but they are not interchangeable photographs of the same sound.

When researchers combine what would ordinarily be incompatible signals, a listener must interpret a joint event. Vision sometimes pulls the report away from the acoustic syllable. The famous /da/ answer is one possible result of that process. It is not a new sound physically added to the recording.

One can hear the same physical waveform while reporting different syllables under different visual conditions. This is why the experiment is better described as a change in *speech perception* than as proof that people are careless observers.

Words on a screen make this easy to misunderstand. Writing “audio /ba/ plus visual /ga/ equals perceived /da/” is a useful shorthand, not a mathematical law. If you play one online demonstration and hear /ba/ in both conditions, the demonstration has not failed a medical test and you have not failed a cognitive test. You have contributed one perfectly plausible response in a field with known variability.

The contrast also gives us a useful editorial discipline. A visible mouth can be the *context* that alters a response. That does not mean a consumer should believe every psychological claim framed as “your mind is already doing it unconsciously.”

## The replicability story is more complicated than a viral clip

By the time McGurk and MacDonald's paper had become a staple of psychology teaching, researchers had learned that there was no single percentage of people who would experience the illusion under every condition.

In a 2015 investigation, Basu Mallick and colleagues studied individual responses across different stimuli and tasks. Their first experiment included 165 English-speaking adults; forty returned for retesting about a year later. Depending on the individual, reported illusory perception across stimuli ranged from essentially never to essentially always. The frequency also differed markedly by particular stimulus. [2]

Their follow-up manipulation makes the point even sharper. When participants were forced to select from provided answers rather than allowed an open response, reports counted as McGurk perception increased by an estimated eighteen percentage points. That figure describes the difference under the study's response methods. It is **not** a universal increase in audiovisual integration and certainly not a percentage improvement any commercial recording can promise.

The researchers found that responses could be stable for many individuals across the year, even as the people in the sample differed sharply from one another. The obvious lesson is that an average is a poor substitute for understanding the spread.

A 2018 paper led by Valerie Brown examined traits associated with susceptibility. The researchers reported a relationship with the ability to obtain place-of-articulation information from visible speech. They did not find the same explanatory relationships with the measured attention control, working memory, processing speed or auditory gradiency tasks. The analysis was correlational; it did not establish that training lip-reading would cause a particular person's illusion rate to change. [3]

This is one of the reasons a short attention video must never become an informal diagnostic. A viewer who does not hear /da/ has not demonstrated weak concentration, poor imagination, or a damaged capacity to meditate. The task does not measure those things.

## A debate about what the test actually measures

A later review by researchers studying audiovisual speech warned against making the McGurk task stand in for every form of multisensory perception. Ordinary conversation is not a string of isolated syllables dubbed out of sync with another speaker's mouth. Under natural conditions, the audio and visual signals are generally compatible rather than deliberately contradictory. [5]

That distinction matters when the experimental effect becomes a broad claim about “how the brain works.” The mismatch test is an unusually revealing probe of certain processes. Its very unnaturalness may also limit its generalizability.

Different listeners can be good at using visible speech in everyday conversation while responding differently to the artificially mismatched syllables of a McGurk test. Findings in the literature question whether susceptibility to those special stimuli tracks real-world audiovisual speech performance closely enough to treat it as a general capacity score. [5]

There is no contradiction in saying that the phenomenon is real and that its use as a universal measuring instrument can be misleading. Good research often gives a dramatic observation while restricting the conclusion drawn from it.

Another set of experiments examined timing. Munhall and colleagues' 1996 work found that a strict frame-perfect match was not necessary for the effect; in their first experiment, the influence persisted with the sound lagging visual speech by substantial fractions of a second. [6] But that is not permission to claim any badly synchronized video demonstrates precisely the same phenomenon. Timing, stimulus quality and task instructions still matter.

If an original production is eventually commissioned for Final Vestige, the audio, lips, phonemes and consent of performers will all need controlled documentation. A narrator merely describing the effect over a rotating illustrated mouth would be an explanation, not an authentic experimental reproduction.

## What an outside recording can and cannot show

A visitor can explore an established audiovisual demonstration from Universitat Pompeu Fabra's Multisensory Research Group. Their public educational page asks viewers to compare a moving speaker's visible articulation with an audio-only condition. We can refer readers there, clearly identifying it as an **external** resource, without embedding or copying its film and without claiming we own its media. [7]

The exercise is simple. On the first pass, listen while watching. Record the syllable you think you heard before the video tells you what to expect. On a second pass, keep the sound and cover the picture or close your eyes. Write down the new answer. If a difference appears, compare the two reports without turning it into a diagnosis.

If no difference appears, that outcome belongs in your notes too. You have not disproved the paper. You also have not proven the entire population sees the world the same way you do.

A third pass can help identify a trap: once you have been told the “correct” answer, you may begin hearing, guessing or reporting with that expectation in mind. The repeat view is educational, not a blinded laboratory comparison. Its audio system, device delay, environment, prior knowledge and participant selection are uncontrolled.

The most useful result may be a question rather than a spectacular sound: what did the media actually present, and what did I report?

## A field note for people who don't want to watch a video

The observation can be understood on paper. Draw three columns: **what was recorded**, **what was visible**, and **what the observer reported**.

In the first, write: soundtrack contains /ba/. In the second: film depicts the lip movements for /ga/. In the third: some adults report /da/. The third column is not a faithful transcription of the first two. It is the dependent response that the investigators collected.

Then add a fourth column: **what this does not establish**. The answer should include at least four things. It does not establish that all observers experience the same sound. It does not establish the cause of every everyday listening error. It does not establish clinical impairment in someone with a different response. And it does not establish efficacy for a meditation, hypnosis or brainwave-audio product.

Finally, consider the converse: what would better evidence look like for a product claim? You would need a defined product, a population, a measurable outcome, a comparison group appropriate to the question, and evidence that the particular product caused the claimed result. A visual speech experiment supplies none of those.

This source-reading exercise is a miniature version of the work that belongs in a historical archive. The observation, the explanatory theory and the commercial promise occupy different columns. Keep them there.

## The tempting historical parallel

Final Vestige's published [Puységur Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) concern an altogether different setting: eighteenth-century accounts of magnetic sleep, the language of practitioners and witnesses, and the interpretation of reported experiences.

One can compare the problems of *testimony* without pretending to equate the experiments. In Puységur's world, the investigator often had to rely on testimony mediated by the person operating the procedure. In McGurk and MacDonald's laboratory, an audiovisual stimulus could be constructed, recorded and presented in controlled conditions.

The value of the comparison is methodological. A person may sincerely report an experience that needs to be understood through the circumstances in which it was produced. The fact of sincerity does not tell us the mechanism. Nor does the presence of suggestion permit us to diagnose a particular eighteenth-century participant. We need independent records and carefully delimited claims.

The published [Mesmerism investigation](https://finalvestige.com/2026/11/02/mesmerism/) looks at the 1784 commission associated with Franklin and the problem of suggestion and expectation. It is a separate historical investigation. An audiovisual speech illusion discovered nearly two centuries later does not retroactively validate mesmerism, disprove every reported trance, or endorse modern hypnosis recordings.

The bridge between the studies is not mysticism. It is the need to distinguish a compelling personal report from an independently established explanation.

## The thing we really heard

A recorded sound is an artifact. A reported sound is an observation made by a person. In most ordinary circumstances, the two are close enough that we never distinguish them. McGurk and MacDonald arranged a circumstance in which the distinction could no longer be ignored.

The enduring image is not of a brain fooled into hearing nonsense. It is of an observer doing what perception ordinarily does: constructing a coherent event out of the evidence arriving from several places. Sometimes the best guess the system can make is a syllable that appears nowhere in the soundtrack.

That is a sound reason to examine our testimony with care. It is not a reason to abandon trust in every observer, to sell a cure, or to claim that a three-second trick has unlocked the whole of consciousness.

The lips move. The loudspeaker plays. The listener says what was heard. An investigation starts when we refuse to treat those as the same piece of evidence.
