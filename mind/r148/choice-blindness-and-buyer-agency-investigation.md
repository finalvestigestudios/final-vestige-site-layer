# The Choice You Didn't Make: When Your Mind Explains Someone Else's Decision
**Mind & Perception Archive | Independent investigation | Unpublished draft, 5 November 2026**
*Research and historical interpretation only. Not an advertisement or a diagnostic test.*

## The substitution

Imagine two photographs placed on a table. You are asked to choose the more attractive face. You indicate one; the experimenter returns a photograph and asks you to explain your selection. Perhaps you say it was the smile. Perhaps you mention the eyes, or how the portrait appears more open.

But the photograph returned to you is not the one you chose.

There is a magician's move buried in the handoff. In a now-classic experimental paradigm, the experimenters covertly exchanged a selected face for the rejected alternative. Some participants described the substituted picture as though it were their own earlier choice. The striking part was not merely the missed switch; it was the orderly account that followed. A person might offer an explanation of an action whose represented outcome had been changed without their knowledge.

Petter Johansson, Lars Hall, Sverker Sikström and Andreas Olsson described this finding in *Science* in 2005, calling the phenomenon **choice blindness**. Their study asked a limited, testable question about preference judgments and detection of mismatch; it did not prove that everyone can be manipulated into buying anything. [1]

The difference matters. The stronger a demonstration feels, the more tempting it becomes to exaggerate what it proves. That temptation is particularly dangerous when the topic is persuasion.

## An explanation is not a time machine

Introspection often feels like a camera pointed inward. We retrieve a reason, and the reason seems to authenticate the act that preceded it: *I chose this because…* Yet a report obtained after an event is not a direct recording of the mental process that produced it. It may be a sincere explanation assembled from cues now available.

In the 2005 face-selection task, participants first expressed a preference between pairs of photographs. The experimenters occasionally engineered an outcome that did not match the indicated choice, then asked for explanations. The peer-reviewed abstract reports failures to detect some conspicuous mismatches and the production of reasons for the substituted outcome. [1][2]

This is not evidence that participants were lying. It is precisely because the explanations could be sincere that the result belongs in a study of perception and self-knowledge. The participant sees a returned photograph, remembers having decided, and has a plausible vocabulary for why such a photograph might appeal. The story is locally reasonable even when its premise has changed.

Nor is every failure of detection necessarily the same phenomenon. The experiment takes place in a controlled sequence, with an unobtrusive manipulation and a specific question. It does not measure someone's character, intelligence, moral reliability or everyday susceptibility to advertising. The experimental design itself should travel with the headline.

## From photographs to jam jars

The idea would have been simpler to dismiss had it remained confined to portrait photographs. In 2010, Hall, Johansson, Betty Tärning, Sikström and Thérèse Deutgen carried a related manipulation into a supermarket setting. Passersby compared varieties of jam or tea and made a preference judgment. At the retasting stage, researchers sometimes substituted the previously rejected option. [3]

Across manipulated trials, **no more than one third were detected**, according to the researchers' abstract. Even pronounced sensory differences did not guarantee detection. Some people went on to explain why they preferred the very sample that had been switched. [3][4]

It is worth picturing the ordinary surroundings: jars, a spoon, a shopper who is not expecting to take part in a lecture about consciousness. In that setting the experimental question becomes less exotic. How accurately can a person track a decision and its result when the sequence is interrupted and the result comes back through another person's hands?

Still, we must resist inflating the finding. The researchers did not study an online audio course, an affiliate referral, a meditation purchase, or a long-term customer relationship. Jam and tea preferences are not equivalent to consequential financial or medical decisions. The experiment supports a bounded observation about decision-outcome monitoring in that tasting task. It does not supply a universal persuasion algorithm.

## The inconvenient distinction between blindnesses

Final Vestige has documented change blindness: failures to notice changes across a visual disruption. Choice blindness shares the language of missed differences but asks something extra. It concerns the gap between an intended decision and the *outcome later presented as that decision*. When the subject then supplies reasons for the outcome, investigators can compare a reported explanation with a manipulated sequence.

Change blindness is not the same as inattentional blindness, and neither is automatically choice blindness. The invisible-gorilla task asks whether an unexpected event is detected during an attention-demanding task. The Thatcher illusion depends on orientation-related changes in the appearance of faces. Attentional blink measures conditions under which a second rapid target is less likely to be reported. The mechanisms, tasks and implications differ. They are better understood as a family of experiments on the limits of accessible experience, not one master theory that allows any claim about the mind.

The common lesson is modest but useful: **feeling certain that we would have noticed something is not the same as having tested whether we did**.

## Where history enters—and where it must stop

Long before laboratory choice-blindness experiments, audiences debated suggestion, mesmerism and the origins of apparently involuntary acts. In the late eighteenth century, Armand-Marie-Jacques de Chastenet, Marquis de Puységur, described somnambulic states in the milieu of animal magnetism. Those records are part of the history of how investigators and patients interpreted the mind, agency, authority and expectation. Final Vestige's published *Puységur's Animal Magnetism Files* offers that documentary doorway. [5]

The analogy is historical, not clinical: in both stories the question of who is authoring an explanation is fascinating. But Puységur did not discover the modern choice-blindness mechanism, and surviving descriptions of animal magnetism do not validate a magnetic fluid, paranormal influence or the effectiveness of contemporary hypnosis recordings. The material belongs alongside—not inside—the evidence for the 2005 and 2010 experiments.

A modern review of theories of hypnotic suggestion also notes that competing accounts attempt to explain changes in experience, behavior and the sense of agency. It is legitimate to investigate suggestion scientifically; it is not legitimate to leap from general hypnosis research to claims about a named commercial product without product-specific evidence. [6]

## A demonstration you can run without tricking anybody

Here is a small, nondeceptive decision exercise. It is **not a replication** of Johansson and colleagues, and its outcome cannot diagnose choice blindness.

Before reading further, choose between two useful free activities for the next seven days:

**A.** Spend five quiet minutes noticing sounds around you without trying to alter them.

**B.** Spend five minutes observing when your attention drifts from a chosen page.

Write down your choice and a single, concrete reason *before* trying it. On a later day, revisit the note. Ask whether the activity met your original purpose. If not, change your mind openly. You do not need to protect the earlier rationale.

Notice what this exercise does *not* do. It does not secretly substitute your answer. It does not claim to measure attention. It simply externalizes a preference and its reason so that a later account can be compared against a dated record. That distinction—between recording and persuading—is central to honest fieldwork.

## The ethical mirror of a sales page

Choice blindness can be misused as a story about how to slip a product into someone's decision. We draw the opposite editorial conclusion.

If ordinary people can sometimes construct plausible reasons around a substituted outcome, an ethical seller should reduce the occasions on which a buyer must reconstruct what happened. An offer should display the precise product title; the true current checkout price should be observable before payment; inclusions, refund conditions, disclosures and any recurring charges should not shift behind an ambiguous button. The reader should be able to compare alternatives without being rushed or nudged by fabricated scarcity.

The point is not that the 2010 jam experiment *proved* these design practices boost revenue. It did not. The point is that respecting decision-outcome continuity is a defensible consumer standard, quite apart from performance marketing. In Final Vestige's conversion work, it is a deliberately falsifiable commitment: if the page says Premium but checkout leads to Beginner, or if an audio product's stated price differs from the final charge without transparent explanation, the funnel has failed its own evidence contract.

A buyer can choose to pay for the convenience of a curated audio library without believing every metaphor on its sales page. A buyer can also choose a free timer, a public meditation exercise, or no audio at all. What choice-blindness research challenges is not the validity of either outcome but our habit of treating a fluent explanation as automatic proof that the original sequence was accurately perceived.

## What the experiment does not establish

Choice blindness does not mean that preference is fictional, that free will has been disproved, that hypnosis creates obedience, or that the brain can be rewritten with a recording. It does not show that a customer who buys an audio program was manipulated or that a customer who regrets it never wanted it. There are individual differences, task boundaries, detection events and ordinary questions of consent.

It also does not show that hypnotism, subliminals or commercial brainwave entrainment produces lasting changes in memory, wealth, medical conditions or attention. The studies cited here never tested Zen12 Premium, Hypnosis Live Total Focus or Brainwave Shots. A scientific paper about one phenomenon cannot be pasted into a product page and called proof of another.

Even the 2010 'no more than one third' figure belongs to **manipulated experimental trials in that study**, not all choices made by all shoppers. Context is evidence, too.

## An archive of decisions, not promises

The returned photograph in the original demonstration may look innocuous. The returned jam sample may taste plausible. The genuine puzzle appears when explanation and sequence come apart. Which part of the account is observation, which memory, and which an after-the-fact construction?

That question belongs in a historical research archive precisely because it resists easy selling. The best use of the evidence may be a small purchasing habit: write down what you intended to acquire, then verify that the offer and checkout actually match it. When they do not, interrupt the purchase. An honest record is more valuable than a good-sounding excuse.
