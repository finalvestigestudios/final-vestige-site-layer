# The Ninth Patient in the Footnote: What the Rosenhan Study Actually Counted
**Final Vestige Studios | Mind & Perception | Original investigation — unpublished editorial draft | 11 November 2026**
**Status:** SOURCE REVIEW / editorial and medical-context QA pending. No medical advice, advertisement, affiliate link, or claim about treatment effectiveness.

In 1973, a psychologist published a story that seemed to settle a disturbing question. Eight people with no serious psychiatric disorder, he said, had gained admission to psychiatric hospitals by claiming to hear unfamiliar voices. After admission, they behaved ordinarily. Their behavior, the story went, was nevertheless interpreted through the diagnosis they had been given. One of the most memorable experiments in twentieth-century psychology was built around the proposition that an institution might not recognize ordinary behavior once it had labeled a person mentally ill.

The problem is that an experiment must be tested against its **own surviving record**, too.

In 2019, journalist Susannah Cahalan documented discrepancies and gaps in the published story after investigating David Rosenhan's archive and the people who supposedly took part. In 2023, historian Andrew Scull went further and called the study a successful case of scientific fraud. Those are consequential charges, but they are not the same as proving that the six untraced pseudopatients never existed. The gap is itself a clue to a more useful lesson: one must distinguish what a study reported, what an archival investigator found, what a later scholar inferred, and what remains inaccessible.

This is the evidence behind the story—not another retelling of the famous punch line.

## Nine reported, eight analyzed, twelve hospitals — and the excluded footnote

On 19 January 1973, *Science* published Rosenhan's “On Being Sane in Insane Places,” volume 179, pages 250–258 ([record and abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/4683124/)). Rosenhan described an exercise in which eight apparently healthy people presented at psychiatric hospitals, reported voices, were admitted, and subsequently ceased reporting the invented symptom. His published analysis argued that the hospital context could transform the meaning assigned to behavior.

To understand why it mattered, imagine a commonplace act: taking notes. In a lecture hall, notes might indicate curiosity. In an institution where every observation is filtered through illness, the same action might appear as a symptom. Rosenhan's paper supplied stories that seemed to expose such interpretive drift. His conclusion was sweeping, and the paper became an enduring reference in discussions of psychiatric diagnosis, stigma, and institutional power.

But several questions have to remain separate. *Did hospital workers fail to discover the simulated admission symptom?* is not equivalent to *could psychiatric professionals reliably distinguish people with and without illness when given adequate truthful information?* A deception exercise can test one kind of vulnerability without settling every aspect of the broader diagnostic problem. Those distinctions were challenged within a few years, well before the archival investigation.

The original paper is **evidence for what Rosenhan claimed**, not independent verification of fieldwork. We inspected the university-hosted original *Science* scan at printed page 251 and printed page 258, note 6: https://www.sjsu.edu/people/jennifer.gregg/courses/c2/s0/Rosenhahn-being-sane-in-insane-places.pdf . Page 251 reports eight pseudopatients in the analyzed account and twelve hospitals. Note 6 explicitly states that a **ninth pseudopatient's data were excluded** because, Rosenhan wrote, the person had changed parts of his personal history, including marital and parental details. The footnote does not identify that person. This is a statement of the reported exclusion criterion, not independent substantiation for it.

The numerical story consequently has three separate entries: nine reported as having participated overall, eight in the published analysis, and one omitted under the author's stated rule. Harry Lando's 1976 supplemental report supplies a distinctive later account of an undetected simulated admission and positive treatment experience, but Rosenhan's note 6 does not itself name Lando. Nor does it establish that favorable treatment caused someone's exclusion. The relationship between Lando and the excluded ninth requires careful attribution to later analyses rather than a documentary leap.

## The objection did not begin in 2019

In October 1975, psychiatrist Robert L. Spitzer published “On Pseudoscience in Science, Logic in Remission, and Psychiatric Diagnosis,” *Journal of Abnormal Psychology* 84, 442–452 ([bibliographic record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1194504/)). In April 1976 he expanded that criticism in *Archives of General Psychiatry* 33, 459–470 ([abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/938183/)).

Spitzer's critique was not simply that the hospitals had behaved correctly. His argument was more specific: people who intentionally present fabricated symptoms can be admitted, and the consequences of that deception do not by themselves validate Rosenhan's much larger claims about the validity of psychiatric diagnosis. In the 1976 abstract, Spitzer argued that the design did not answer the problem its author claimed to address. That is an interpretation by a contemporary critic, not an established resolution of the dispute.

The best response to an influential experiment is not reflexive loyalty or dismissal. It is to ask what result each possible interpretation actually predicts. If a person deliberately tells a doctor something clinically serious that is untrue, the investigator must explain what conclusion follows from the clinician's response—and what conclusion does not.

The critical literature also matters because a sensational story can outlive its rebuttals. A documentary or short video has room for a vivid admission scene. The complete research record needs room for the methodological objection that arrived two years later.

## A different participant described a different hospital

In February 1976, Harry A. Lando published “On Being Sane in Insane Places: A Supplemental Report,” *Professional Psychology* 7, 47–52 ([University of Minnesota bibliographic record and abstract](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/on-being-sane-in-insane-places-a-supplemental-report/)). Lando described spending nineteen days in a public hospital after presenting as a pseudopatient.

There is an important dual result in his abstract. His simulation was not detected: staff interpreted his behavior in a way consistent with the initial diagnosis. But he also described facilities as excellent and therapy as extensive, and reported positive features of the hospital contrary to Rosenhan's strong emphasis on depersonalization.

So even the surviving supplementary account resists a neat binary. Failure to detect deception and evidence of positive institutional care can both appear in the same case. A responsible historical account must retain both.

A precise caveat: the accessible institutional abstract establishes what Lando reported in his published supplemental article. It does not establish everything that occurred during the hospital stay, and it should not be interpreted as a verdict about today's mental-health services.

## What the later investigation found—and what it could not

Cahalan's 2019 book, *The Great Pretender*, revisited the experiment through archives, witness searches, and interviews. Alison Abbott's *Nature* review, published on 29 October 2019, describes the troubling inconsistencies Cahalan uncovered ([review record](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-019-03268-y)). Andrew Scull's 2023 *History of Psychiatry* essay drew on Cahalan's findings and records she shared with him ([journal abstract](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0957154X221150878)).

This distinction of access is crucial. We directly inspected the **full 1973 article scan** at printed pages 251 and 258 (note 6), together with the available Spitzer 1976, Lando 1976 and Scull 2023 abstracts. We also inspected the **continuation of Alison Abbott's *Nature* review on printed page 623**, in a publisher-hosted PDF: https://media.nature.com/original/magazine-assets/d41586-019-03084-4/d41586-019-03084-4.pdf . Abbott describes Cahalan's search, her contact with Bill Underwood and discrepancies she reported between Rosenhan's study and surviving records. This is only a **partial secondary review**, not the entire *Nature* review, Cahalan's book, or authenticated charts. Cahalan's source files remain unexamined here, so chart-level allegations must be attributed and qualified.

Cahalan's reporting raised doubts about the participant roster and the consistency between hospital records and the paper's account. Abbott's review continuation reports that Cahalan eventually located one identifiable participant, Bill Underwood. The later shorthand about *six untraced* participants is a count attributed to investigative accounts, not a direct audit of six hospital charts performed here. A record that cannot be located is not proof that its subject never existed; it does prevent full independent verification.

Scull's 2023 essay makes the stronger scholarly judgment that the evidence constitutes fraud. His accessible abstract explicitly characterizes it that way. We can report his assessment as a cited historical argument without silently treating the conclusion as a judicial finding or offering a false suggestion that the underlying patient charts have been audited in this project.

The missing evidence is not just an embarrassment for a famous study. It is the feature that makes the story worth revisiting. An apparently decisive finding depends on a roster, records, procedures, and outcomes other people should be able to inspect. When those are unavailable, the level of confidence changes.

## Four records, four different kinds of knowledge

| Record | What it directly supports | What it does not establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rosenhan, 1973 | What the original investigator published and concluded | Independent corroboration of every described participant and event |
| Spitzer, 1975/76 | An early methodological rebuttal, with a specific alternate interpretation | That all historical hospitals behaved appropriately |
| Lando, 1976 | A participant's differing published account of treatment and undetected simulation | Typical care, prevalence, or overall diagnostic accuracy |
| Cahalan, 2019 / Scull, 2023 | Subsequent archival criticisms and a historian's strong published interpretation | Direct inspection by this article of every claimed hospital chart |

These records should not be averaged into a single certainty. They answer different questions.

This is also why the rhetorical move “the study was discredited, therefore the concerns it raised were false” does not follow. A flawed or even fraudulent experiment cannot prove a conclusion; it does not prove that all possible versions of the concern are unfounded. Stigma, coercion, and diagnostic uncertainty must be assessed using other relevant evidence and testimony rather than making this one contested paper bear the entire burden.

Equally, “the study raised real concerns, therefore its errors do not matter” fails for the opposite reason. A good question is not a license to provide unreliable data. Historical credibility has a cost: keep the question, correct the record, and move on to independently verifiable evidence.

## An original ten-minute source exercise

Choose a confident headline from an experiment or historical mystery that interests you. Before reading commentary, write down the exact assertion it appears to make. Then open the earliest accessible source, find its publication date, and classify what you can inspect: the full study, an abstract, a review, a summary, or a primary archive document.

Next, find one serious disagreement. Write the strongest version of the critic's objection and note whether the critic possessed original data or was arguing from a published report. Finally, write two sentences: **“The surviving evidence shows…”** and **“I still cannot verify…”**.

This is a documentary-source practice, not a psychological diagnostic exercise. It will not determine whether someone is healthy or whether a treatment works. Its purpose is narrower and more valuable: to keep an extraordinary historical claim from becoming more certain with every retelling.

## What we still cannot know from the sources checked here

We have not independently examined Cahalan's underlying patient-chart reproductions or the complete subscription-access text of Scull's paper. We have not authenticated all original pseudopatient identities. We have not determined how modern diagnostic practice compares with the hospitals described in 1973; that would require an entirely different body of research. We have also not evaluated whether the Final Vestige documentary's individual archival scenes perfectly match the primary record—this manuscript is an independent companion with its own stated reading limits.

That is not a weak ending. It identifies the **limits of the sources examined for this draft**, not the end of the available paper trail. Earlier research in this project and the university-hosted original paper invite fuller editorial comparison before publication.

A study about the danger of a label became a label in its own right: *the experiment that proved psychiatrists cannot tell*. The responsible conclusion is not to replace it with a more fashionable certainty. It is to keep the original question open, preserve the documented objections, and demand records that can survive scrutiny.
