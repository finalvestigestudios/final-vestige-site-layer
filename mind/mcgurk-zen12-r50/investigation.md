# The Sound the Mouth Invented
**Evidence desk · Perception & Attention · Unpublished editorial draft · 1 November 2026**

*When a soundtrack says one thing and a face appears to say another, the word you hear may belong to neither. The phenomenon is real. The easy explanation—that the mind simply believes its eyes—is not.*

In 1976, two researchers at the University of Surrey assembled a small contradiction. The sound on their film repeated a spoken **ba**. The woman's lips on the screen, however, made movements consistent with **ga**. Viewers did not reliably resolve this disagreement by choosing one of the two inputs. In the researchers' now-famous report, listeners sometimes described hearing a third syllable: **da**.

Nothing supernatural entered the room. The audio track had not been altered to say *da*. Nor had the filmed speaker mouthed it. What had changed was the act of perceiving speech.

Harry McGurk and John MacDonald called attention to a problem hidden inside an ordinary conversation. Speech has never been solely an affair of ears. We observe jaw movements and lips, changes in expression, and the rhythm between sound and gesture. Sometimes these cues agree so well that we hardly notice the collaboration. Sometimes, as in their deliberately mismatched film, that collaboration can be made visible.

Their article, *Hearing lips and seeing voices*, occupied only three pages of *Nature*. Its title promised an impossibility. Its experiment exposed a practical fact: the brain must decide what was said from evidence arriving through more than one sense. [1]

## One recording, three possible experiences

Picture an ordinary speaker saying **ba**. The sound begins with the lips coming together; the release is audible, and the mouth movement is visible. Now imagine replacing the picture with a different mouth gesture while keeping the sound. The visible movement no longer corroborates the audible one.

That is the core of the McGurk demonstration. It is tempting to treat the outcome as a stage trick: put two sensations together and generate a predictable third. But the details matter. The original researchers described not only a fused **da** response to one combination but a different response pattern when the dubbing direction was reversed. When the soundtrack was presented on its own, or the film remained unaltered, participants identified the syllables more accurately. Their point was not merely that people are easy to fool. It was that seeing a person articulate speech can change what the listener reports hearing. [1]

In a darkened room, a listener has an auditory signal. In a face-to-face exchange, the listener has a joint problem. If a voice is muffled by traffic, facial movements may help distinguish what has been said. The same capacity that assists communication can also lead us astray when the signals are deliberately made incompatible.

This is why calling the effect "your eyes overrule your ears" explains too little. In the classic fusion case, the listener does not simply report the visually presented syllable. The reported answer may be different from *both* signals. The interesting event is their interaction.

## A famous percentage, and its missing context

A striking statistic became attached to the illusion. In their 2021 reassessment, Laura Getz and Joseph Toscano note that the original experimental configuration elicited a fusion response from 98 percent of participants. That number is historically informative, but it is a poor promise about what a stranger will perceive when watching an arbitrary internet clip. [4]

In 2015, researchers presented 20 different McGurk stimuli to 360 people. Reactions varied dramatically. Some participants gave no fusion responses across repeated presentations; others reported them consistently. The talker shown on screen and the method used to ask the question also changed the result. When the same material was tested with forced choices rather than open-ended answers, the estimated frequency of fusion responses increased by approximately 18 percentage points. [2]

This is the sort of detail that vanishes in a viral demonstration. A video asks, "Did you hear da?" The viewer may already be wondering whether **da** is the answer they are supposed to find. In an experiment, the choice of possible answers is part of the experimental machinery, not a neutral afterthought.

Getz and Toscano ran experiments that varied whether respondents chose from a list or supplied their own answer, whether **da** appeared among the offered choices, what clips were used, and whether people took part in a laboratory or online. Across these conditions, fusion responses for the same stimuli ranged from 10 to 60 percent. They argued that researchers should be much more cautious about treating the McGurk response as a simple, stable measure of how strongly a person's senses combine information. [4]

None of this makes the original finding fraudulent. It makes the finding more scientifically useful. There is an effect worth explaining, and there are boundaries around when it occurs.

## A mouth is not merely decoration

In the 2018 study *What accounts for individual differences in susceptibility to the McGurk effect?*, Violet Brown and colleagues examined possible reasons why listeners react differently. The ability to extract information about where a sound is articulated from a visible mouth was related to susceptibility. Measures of attentional control, working memory, processing speed, and auditory perceptual gradiency were not, in that study, the convincing explanations many might expect. [3]

That finding has a surprisingly precise implication. Someone who does not experience a particular McGurk demonstration is not thereby deficient in attention, less suggestible, or mysteriously more rational. One explanation may simply be that different viewers extract different information from the visual signal—or that the demonstration itself was poorly constructed.

The research also resists a crude "more visual information means a stronger illusion" story. A study involving 110 participants played audiovisual syllables at different speeds. Slowing the presentation to half speed **reduced**, rather than increased, McGurk responses. The authors interpreted the result through models in which the reliability of sensory information matters, not just how long a person looks at the mouth. [5]

A useful theory must account for those awkward observations alongside the clean ones. If our explanation predicts that every clearer, slower, longer exposure should strengthen the effect, it is not yet a good explanation.

## The listener is not a machine with two independent meters

We often talk about seeing and hearing as though a private screen delivers pictures while a separate speaker delivers sound, and some executive in the head chooses which one to trust. Ordinary speech does not feel like that. The visible face and the audible voice arrive as a single person, a single event.

The McGurk effect invites a better question: what does the perceptual system do when the channels disagree?

Researchers describe forms of audiovisual integration, the use of visual information to constrain speech recognition, and models of sensory reliability. Those are frameworks for studying observed patterns, not a license to claim that every sensory illusion demonstrates the same mechanism or that a particular commercial recording can remotely rewire a brain.

Think about the difference between a direct recording and a recollection. A microphone can preserve the original **ba** track. The listener's experience may contain a **da**. Both reports can be made sincerely: one is about the physical stimulus; the other is about perception. The disagreement does not mean the listener fabricated anything. It means perception is itself an outcome that requires explanation.

That difference matters far outside a laboratory. A witness may be trying to describe faithfully what they heard. A family member may confidently recall hearing a word in a crowded room. An investigator can respect those reports while still asking how the material was presented, what alternatives were offered, and whether the original audio or video survives.

## The more careful bridge to animal magnetism

Final Vestige's **Puységur's Animal Magnetism Files** documents a much earlier history of reports shaped by attention, expectation, setting and observation. The archive is a historical research collection, not a proof that eighteenth-century animal magnetism operated by any modern perceptual mechanism. [6]

The connection here is methodological. The old sources and the new experiments force the same disciplined separation between *what someone reports experiencing* and *what a proposed cause has actually been shown to do*.

In Puységur's world, observers recorded remarkable states and reactions during magnetic treatments. They interpreted those observations through theories available to them, and later readers have supplied still more theories. To move from a report to a mechanism requires additional controls. The 1976 dubbing experiment offers an elegant modern example: the researchers could keep the audio constant, change the mouth movements, and observe how descriptions shifted.

That comparison should not be inflated into a claim that the McGurk effect explains hypnosis, that magnetism explains auditory illusions, or that a seller's "brainwave audio" has inherited support from either body of evidence. These would be three different claims needing three different lines of proof.

A historical record is valuable partly because it preserves the uncertainty its authors could not settle. A modern experiment is valuable partly because it narrows uncertainty under a specified set of conditions.

## Try the distinction, not the trick

You do not need a viral video to begin noticing the difference between a signal and an interpretation. This short exercise is deliberately **not** a reproduction of the McGurk experiment; without a carefully synchronized, incongruent recording, it cannot test the effect.

Ask a consenting friend to say an ordinary sentence quietly while facing you. First listen while looking away, then ask for a different sentence while facing the speaker. Write down which sounds or words became easier to distinguish, if any, and whether the comparison was affected by the speaker's volume, pacing, accent, or your knowledge of the sentence. Keep the volume comfortable and stop if the activity is difficult or frustrating.

What have you shown? At most, something about two real-world listening situations. You have **not** demonstrated a fused phantom syllable, measured sensory integration, or tested a treatment. This distinction—the limit of what a demonstration can legitimately prove—is the valuable part.

For an actual McGurk investigation, researchers need well-specified sound and video tracks, synchronized timing, controls in which the tracks agree, and a response method specified before people watch. The honest scientist reports how many people *did not* experience the effect, too.

## What the illusion can—and cannot—tell us

The McGurk effect shows that visible articulation can change how audible speech is perceived under particular conditions. The literature also shows that this is not an identical experience for everyone, and that stimulus choice, question format, and playback conditions alter response rates. [1–5]

It does **not** establish that thoughts are controlled from outside the body, that an untested audio program produces meditation benefits, that people can be made to hear anything, or that individual differences in the illusion measure intelligence or suggestibility.

There is a better ending than that.

The most unusual part of the 1976 film was not that a person could be wrong about a syllable. It was that a carefully controlled disagreement between picture and soundtrack made perception itself observable. When a film says **ba** and an observer hears **da**, an investigator has discovered a question worth asking—not a doctrine worth selling.
