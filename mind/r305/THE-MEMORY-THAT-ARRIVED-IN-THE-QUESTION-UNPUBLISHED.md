# The Memory That Arrived in the Question
## How a single verb—and a conversation after the fact—can change what a witness says they remember

The paywalled 1974 and 2003 complete articles and underlying participant data were **not** inspected. Statements below are limited accordingly. This is not legal, psychiatric or medical advice.*

The broken glass did not exist in the film.

That is the uncomfortable starting point of one of psychology's most frequently retold experiments. In 1974, Elizabeth Loftus and John Palmer showed participants footage of automobile collisions. The researchers later asked questions, changing a seemingly ordinary word. Some people were asked how fast the cars were moving when they *smashed* together; others saw less forceful verbs. In one experiment, the change in language was followed by a change in reported speed. In another, people previously given the stronger verb were more likely to report broken glass that was not on the screen.

An extraordinary claim would be that words secretly rewrite every memory. The paper's narrower claim is more revealing: an account of an event can become entangled with the words used to question its witness. What a person says later may reflect both what they saw and what happened afterward.

That distinction reaches beyond courtroom procedure. It belongs at the center of any archive that depends on reported observation—including strange historical testimony, hypnotic recollections and accounts that appear to become more detailed with retelling. It tells us something about how evidence can change on its way to the page. It does **not** prove that witnesses are always unreliable, that memories are usually invented, or that a commercial meditation or hypnosis recording can change, restore or improve memory.

### 1. The question was part of the experiment

The paper is titled *Reconstruction of automobile destruction: An example of the interaction between language and memory*. Its October 1974 publisher record describes **two** experiments rather than one continuous trial. In the first, observers watched films of car accidents and answered speed questions phrased with different verbs: *smashed, collided, bumped, hit* or *contacted*. The verb was a controlled difference in the subsequent question—not a different collision magically occurring before different viewers.

The publisher abstract states that questions using *smashed* elicited higher speed estimates than the alternatives. Speed estimates are reports, not measurements extracted from the film. A higher response could reflect a change in recollection, the semantic implications of the question, or a judgment made while answering. That is why the next experiment mattered.

In the later experiment participants received follow-up questions after a delay. One asked whether they remembered seeing broken glass, despite none being present in the film. The publisher's abstract reports that people earlier exposed to *smashed* more often answered yes.

The study illustrates vulnerability to a **post-event suggestion**. It does not, by itself, resolve exactly which cognitive process generated each answer. A respondent may sincerely experience a reconstructed memory; another may guess what a questioner expects; another may blur a separate image with the scene. Such explanations are not interchangeable, and the abstract does not adjudicate each participant's internal experience.

**What the inspected record supports:** wording in questions influenced reported speed and later false recognition of an absent detail in these experiments.

**What it does not establish:** every witness will change; the original trace was erased; all memories from interviews are false; hypnotic recordings recover hidden truths.

**Original source:** [Loftus and Palmer, *Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior* 13(5), 585–589 (1974), publisher abstract](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-5371(74)80011-3).

### 2. The detail nobody thought they were testing

Imagine the aftermath of an ordinary minor collision. Two people compare notes. One says it was a crash; another describes a bump. A third asks, "Was the broken window on the driver's side?" The grammar has placed a broken window inside the question before anyone has established that a window broke.

A good investigator would resist the temptation to fill the blank. The first inquiry should be open: "Tell me what you remember seeing." The next is narrower but still neutral: "What did you notice about the windows?" Only when the record supports it should anyone ask which window broke.

This sequence is an *editorial illustration*, not a claim that our hypothetical exchange happened in the Loftus–Palmer experiment. Its point is the difference between finding a fact and suggesting one. The strongest-looking recollection can be a story assembled after a question has already supplied its missing noun.

This is not a warrant to distrust people automatically. A camera can be incomplete. People can remember events accurately. A direct contemporary note may be stronger than a polished recollection written twenty years later. The practical lesson is to keep the earliest version and the later interventions visible at the same time.

### 3. When the other witness becomes the source

Nearly three decades later, Fiona Gabbert, Amina Memon and Kevin Allan examined what happened when people who had seen different versions of a video were allowed to discuss the event.

The design exploited a subtle advantage: each person in a pair watched an event from a version that contained some details the partner had not seen. Afterward, some pairs discussed what happened before making an individual report. Other pairs did not. The researchers' university-hosted abstract says **71%** of witnesses who discussed the event mistakenly recalled details that had come from their partner's description, not from the version they had personally viewed.

That number is frequently used without the essential qualifying words. It is not an estimate that 71% of real-world eyewitnesses provide false testimony, nor that 71% of everything witnesses remember is wrong. It describes **this particular comparison, sample and scoring definition**. And it is not proof that every contaminated report involved a fully believed internal false memory rather than conversational agreement or source confusion.

The deeper mechanism is unsettling: a person can possess a true memory of having **heard** a detail while misidentifying the source as having **seen** it. The error is not necessarily in remembering the statement. It can be in attaching the statement to the wrong event.

**Original study record:** [Gabbert, Memon and Allan, *Applied Cognitive Psychology* 17(5), 533–543 (2003), Aberdeen university-hosted abstract](https://abdn.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/memory-conformity-can-eyewitnesses-influence-each-others-memories/).

### 4. Three mistakes an archive can make

The first error is **verb inflation**. An eighteenth-century witness writes that a patient *appeared calmer*; a later writer says the patient *was cured*. The new statement is not just clearer—it is stronger. The evidentiary claim has grown even if no new observation has been added.

The second error is **source migration**. A statement appears in a witness's letter, then in a physician's summary, then in a newspaper's chronology. Four later articles repeat the newspaper. A reader may be impressed by five citations without realizing the five voices descend from one untested account.

The third is **retrospective certainty**. A later story adopts precise timings, dialogue or motives that were absent from the earliest note. Detail is not automatically fabrication; people can recover or remember things over time. But the later addition deserves a source label, not a silent promotion into the original record.

### 5. What a hypnotic recollection can—and cannot—add

The language of suggestion often enters the history of hypnosis through an alluring promise: a witness might retrieve something otherwise inaccessible.

That claim requires care. In 1985 the American Medical Association's Council on Scientific Affairs published a review on hypnosis-assisted recollection. Its accessible institutional abstract cautioned that such recall can involve confabulations and pseudomemories and may be less reliable than unhypnotized recall. It was a review of the **forensic use of hypnosis to refresh recollection**, not a clinical trial of every hypnotic technique or an assertion that every experience of hypnosis is false.

Similarly, Elizabeth Loftus's 2005 review of thirty years of misinformation research discusses how misleading information after an event can impair later memory reports. The review also recognizes that researchers have debated what happens to the original memory representation when a misleading account becomes dominant. That remains a scientific question, not a license to tell people their memories have been completely overwritten.

These distinctions matter when reviewing self-help products. A hypnosis recording offered to encourage a relaxation routine is **not** equivalent to an interview intended to obtain admissible factual testimony. Conversely, a seller cannot borrow the legitimacy of general hypnosis research to assert that a particular download restores memory, reveals buried truth, or guarantees concentration.

**Evidence:** [American Medical Association Council, *JAMA* 253(13), 1918–1923 (1985), abstract](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/article-abstract/397702); [Loftus, *Learning & Memory* 12, 361–366 (2005), abstract and review record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16027179/).

### 6. What the research does *not* let us conclude

An honest account cannot turn one experimental effect into a universal rule. The original studies used controlled presentations and specified questions. Real investigations involve differing stress, viewing conditions, recall opportunities, records, interviewers and incentives. Effect sizes depend on design and measures.

The word *false* is not a simple switch. A person may report an absent detail with little confidence, confidently repeat something someone else said, or experience a genuine memory-like impression. A reported error is not automatically deliberate dishonesty. An experimental task can show contamination without proving the specific mental state that produced it.

A separate line of research examines implantation of imagined childhood events. Its methods and ethical context are different from manipulating a verb in a speed question. We should not collapse every form of suggestibility into one dramatic origin story.

An investigator should also consider alternative explanations: Was the original scene ambiguous? Did the later question influence interpretation without changing stored memory? Did the participant simply follow perceived conversational expectations? Did the study compare equivalent versions and independent answers? The discipline is to keep those possibilities live when the evidence cannot separate them.

### 7. An original demonstration: write before you ask

This is a low-stakes exercise in documentation, **not** a reproduction of Loftus–Palmer or a diagnostic test.

1. Have a friend independently look for twenty seconds at a benign picture you both agree can be used. Each person writes down five things visible **before discussing them**.
2. Independently write three open questions about the picture: "Which objects were near the door?" is more neutral than "Was the red bag next to the door?"
3. Compare the written notes with the image, not with one another's guesses. If a detail appears only in the conversation afterward, mark it as *reported later*, not *observed initially*.
4. Change roles with another picture and repeat. Treat all results as informal observations. Do not use the exercise to assess anyone's honesty, cognitive health, fitness to testify, or susceptibility to hypnosis.

The value is not in producing a dramatic illusion. It is in seeing how quickly source labels can vanish when a group conversation becomes a single polished story.
