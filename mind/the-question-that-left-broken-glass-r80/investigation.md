# The Question That Left Broken Glass Behind

*Mind & Perception / An investigation into testimony, suggestion, and the misinformation effect*  
*Final Vestige Studios — unpublished editorial manuscript, 2 November 2026*  
*Editorial status: researched draft; independent verification and copy edit required before publication.*

## The glass that was never there

Imagine two people watching the same short film of a traffic collision. Neither sees any broken glass. They put down their pens. For one person, the investigator asks how fast the cars were moving when they **hit** each other. For the other, the investigator chooses a more forceful verb. A week later, both are asked about a detail that the film never showed.

This is a reconstruction of the experimental setup, not a transcript of any participant's experience. The crucial observation is not that some subjects lied. In Elizabeth Loftus and John Palmer's 1974 study, people whose earlier question contained **smashed** were more likely to report nonexistent glass a week later than people whose earlier question used **hit**. Words introduced *after* an event were associated with reports *about* that event. [1]

The temptation is to leap to a universal verdict: memory cannot be trusted. That is a mistake. A more exact question is harder, and more useful. Under what conditions can a later suggestion become entangled with an earlier perception—and what can a researcher actually infer from a mistaken answer?

Final Vestige often examines evidence that survived a catastrophe: a written order, a fractured component, a warning noted after the fact. Human memory is also an archive. But unlike a document that can be located and read again in the same state, a recollection is constructed when someone is asked to retrieve it. Sometimes the wording of the request becomes part of the answer.

## An adjective would have been easier. The researchers chose a verb.

The 1974 paper was titled *Reconstruction of Automobile Destruction: An Example of the Interaction Between Language and Memory*. Its first experiment involved short films of traffic accidents and questions about the speed of the cars. One group heard **smashed**; others heard variants such as **collided**, **bumped**, **hit**, and **contacted**. Mean estimates shifted with the verb. The second experiment included a delay before the question about glass. [1]

Notice the controlled alteration. The investigators did not swap the underlying film for one group or tell a witness outright that glass appeared. They changed the verb in a question asked about the same kind of observed event. One word was enough to move the distribution of answers. The design makes the suggestion a plausible cause of the observed group difference; it does not tell us precisely how every individual recollection changed.

A word has several jobs at once. It describes an event, implies its violence, calls up expectations, and tells the listener what the questioner thinks is relevant. If a stranger asks where the shattered window went, the grammar itself can quietly imply a shattered window existed. The participant still must decide whether to accept that implication. The researchers' work showed that this boundary between description and suggestion deserved to be studied experimentally.

The distinction matters because two different things were measured. A numerical speed estimate is not the same kind of judgment as recalling an absent physical object days later. The speed question could be influenced by the connotations of the verb without requiring a genuinely altered memory trace. The later glass question is more suggestive of memory contamination, but even there, response strategies, guessing, source confusion, and expectations can contribute. The experiment establishes a behavioral effect under its conditions, not a complete neurological explanation. [1]

## A stop sign becomes another sign

Four years later, Loftus, David Miller, and Helen Burns made the test more elaborate. Their 1978 paper used slides depicting an auto–pedestrian accident. Across five experiments and a pilot study, **1,242 participants** saw an event and later encountered information that was consistent with it, misleading, or irrelevant. On later recognition tests, exposure to misleading material made accurate responding less likely. [2]

A traffic-sign detail provides the memorable image for this research tradition: one source supplies an original object; a subsequent narrative describes another. In popular retellings, the scene is often compressed into a magical transformation—someone saw a stop sign, was told it was a yield sign, and *remembered* the second as though they had seen it. The studies support a more careful account: later information can lower accuracy about an earlier visual detail, and the size and interpretation of that effect depend on the task.

The PubMed abstract of the original 1978 article specifies that the investigators used both yes/no and two-alternative forced-choice recognition measures. That matters. Multiple-choice responding is a constrained judgment, not an open-ended autobiographical confession. The authors found that timing of the misleading information also mattered: influence was greater when misleading information arrived shortly before the final test than immediately after the original event. [2]

The result made a clean lesson for investigators, journalists, teachers, and readers of old testimony: when interviewing a witness, the question is itself an intervention. If a witness later adopts a suggested detail, it is hard to tell from the answer alone whether the detail came from firsthand perception, a subsequent conversation, the investigator's wording, or a guess.

## The experiment survives its first era

Classic experiments are attractive partly because they arrive as a tidy anecdote. But a finding should not be protected from new tests by its fame. A contemporary replication and extension by Sera Wiechert and colleagues was published online on **29 September 2025**, and appears in the **July 2026** issue of *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition*. [3]

This new study did not simply restage the old slide sequence. Participants watched a car-accident video constructed in *Grand Theft Auto*, then later encountered co-witness information about traffic signs. Participants given misleading sign information identified the original sign less often than controls. In a second test using four response options and confidence-based betting, the effect remained, but was smaller. [3]

That detail is an antidote to certainty. The authors found a pattern consistent with misinformation influencing subsequent reports, while also finding that response procedure changed the observed size of the effect. Someone saying the wrong word on a memory test is evidence about their response under particular conditions. It is not necessarily proof that the original mental representation has been permanently overwritten.

The authors describe the mechanism as debated. Some explanations emphasize alterations in what is retrieved; others place weight on competition between available sources or decisions about which answer to give. A responsible story can describe the reproducible phenomenon without pretending that the mechanism has been settled. Replication is not a license to erase the caveats.

A separate registered-report replication by O'Donnell and colleagues examined whether blatantly contradictory misinformation could protect against subtler false details. Their published conclusions caution that the expected protective effect did not replicate robustly under their conditions; they also caution against broad generalizations from the design. [4] The methodological lesson is that a warning, a contradictory detail, or a clever trick does not grant automatic immunity to later suggestions.

## The witness is not the experiment

An eyewitness, a survivor, and a participant responding to an image on a screen do not share identical circumstances. A real emergency can involve fear, fatigue, divided attention, multiple conversations, and repeated questioning. Legal fact-finding introduces further incentives and consequences. Laboratory findings can justify better practices, but they should not be applied as a numerical probability to a specific witness without case-specific evidence.

Nor should these results be turned into the claim that memory is fictional. People remember meaningful events accurately in many circumstances. The experiments show that some details are vulnerable to post-event information, not that every recollection is worthless. Source quality still varies, corroboration still matters, and a witness's confidence or sincerity is not, by itself, a certificate that every associated detail came directly from the scene.

The ethical implication is especially important. Once someone has given testimony, a careless interrogator cannot undo a leading question by declaring it harmless. If the goal is reliable evidence, ask open questions first, document the exact wording, distinguish spontaneous recollection from prompted recognition, and record what other accounts the person encountered before the interview. These are general evidence-handling principles, not a replacement for specialized forensic interviewing.

In a historical archive, the same logic becomes a way of reading. Was a detail written down before the alleged event, or only recalled later? Is the first surviving account first-hand? Did later readers translate ambiguous language into a stronger claim? Did someone describe an observation, or hand the observer an explanation?

## The long corridor back to Puységur

Final Vestige's published [Puységur's Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) examines how an eighteenth-century practitioner assembled testimony, witnesses, recorded predictions, signed statements, notarial acts, and recurring procedures to argue that animal magnetism was real. The source-controlled project preserves failures and records explicit concerns about consent, operator pressure, and false answers.

That historical archive is not an ancestor of the misinformation-effect experiment in any scientific or causal sense. We should not write Loftus back into the 1780s, or declare that modern experiments prove Puységur's subjects were mistaken. The bridge is methodological. Both bodies of material force us to notice how an expectation enters a record and how a record can acquire greater authority without gaining greater causal force.

The Puységur archive records cases in which subjects were vulnerable or dependent, where an operator set the terms of questioning, and where the surviving writing itself was part of the demonstration. One especially revealing lesson of the archive is that a notarial act can strengthen **provenance** without proving the physical mechanism a practitioner believed had produced an event.

Misinformation experiments add a modern reason to protect this distinction. A witnessed claim might be sincere, carefully written down, and still shaped by words that arrived after the event. Conversely, a misleading question does not automatically make every subsequent statement false. Each account needs an evidence trail, not an all-purpose theory that dissolves it.

The danger is not only gullibility. Total dismissal is a kind of shortcut too. Suppose an old file describes a subject answering an extraordinary question. One reader announces proof of a hidden force; another declares fraud. Both have skipped the same work: establishing exactly what the source reports, when the question was asked, whether the answer was recorded beforehand, and what independent check would distinguish competing explanations.

## Suggestion is not the same as hypnosis

The word *suggestion* invites confusion. In experimental memory research, a misleading statement or question is a form of suggestion. It does not follow that the participant entered a hypnotic state. It does not follow that guided hypnosis recordings can implant, erase, or improve memory on demand. Likewise, research involving highly hypnotizable participants and specific laboratory cognitive tasks cannot be generalized to an ordinary consumer audio download without product-specific trials.

In 2002, Raz and colleagues reported that a particular posthypnotic suggestion reduced Stroop interference in a small group of highly suggestible participants but not comparably in the less suggestible control group. The sample comprised sixteen people in each group; the outcome concerned a carefully structured laboratory task. [5] It demonstrates the scientific interest of suggestion as an experimental variable, **not** proof that a recorded product improves all concentration, reading, memory, or career performance.

This separation protects both fields. Hypnosis can be studied without borrowing false certainty from memory research. Memory contamination can be studied without converting ordinary leading questions into mystical mind control. And a publishing business can examine the history of suggestion without presenting the history as advertising for contemporary self-help software.

## A demonstration that does not try to change anyone's memory

For a free, non-deceptive exercise, take an image from a public museum collection and write down a description for thirty seconds. Close it. Later, read your description and separately write down what you remember. Finally reopen the image. Mark three columns: **directly visible**, **inferred**, and **not checked**.

Do not plant false details in someone else's recollection. This exercise is a source-audit, not an experiment that claims to replicate Loftus's results. Notice how easily a plausible inference—for example, why someone in a photograph is holding an object—can travel from your interpretation into an unqualified later statement. Then correct your caption to preserve the distinction. If you want to compare notes with someone, tell them the purpose in advance and obtain consent.

This small practice has no purchase requirement. It fits the same philosophy as the existing [Seven Day Focus Fieldbook](https://finalvestige.com/focus/): choose an observation, mark what the evidence actually supports, and take the next step without pretending a ritual guarantees a psychological outcome.

## The sentence that survives

The strongest historical investigator does not ask whether an account feels convincing. They ask what was recorded, when, by whom, under which conditions, and against what independent material. The best experimental psychologist asks how changing one element of those conditions changes responses.

The car collision in the old film did not acquire new glass. But the later account could acquire a detail the event never contained. The gap is where the story lives.

It is also where good research begins.
