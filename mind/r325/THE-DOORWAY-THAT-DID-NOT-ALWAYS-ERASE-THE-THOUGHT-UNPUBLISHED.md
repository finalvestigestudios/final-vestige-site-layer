# The Doorway That Did Not Always Erase the Thought

*Why crossing a room can make a thought harder to retrieve—and why the most revealing experiment found that it often did not.*

There is a particular kind of embarrassment that lasts about three seconds. You leave your desk with a clear purpose, reach the kitchen, and stand among the cupboards with no idea why you came. The destination is correct. The mission has vanished.

The obvious explanation is distraction. Maybe a notification arrived or another thought took over. There is, however, a more unsettling idea: the passage between rooms might itself matter. A doorway can mark the beginning of a new event, and the mind does not necessarily keep the old event equally accessible.

This is the premise behind the **doorway effect**. It is also a good example of how an appealing explanation of everyday life can outrun its evidence. In 2006 researchers produced a controlled experimental finding. In 2011 they took the work into physical rooms. In 2021 a different team tried several variations and, in most conditions, did not reproduce the familiar result. A 2024 experiment then found a related deficit in visual working memory under another carefully designed task.

All four findings can be discussed without making a doorway into a supernatural eraser.

## The object on the table

The initial experiment, published by Gabriel Radvansky and David Copeland in *Memory & Cognition* in 2006, did not recruit people who were genuinely trying to remember why they walked into a kitchen. Instead, participants navigated computer-generated rooms and performed a controlled task involving objects. Researchers probed their memory for the identity of an object recently carried or put down. They compared probes after movement within a room with probes after movement across a room boundary.

This substitution of a mundane household question with a precisely constrained laboratory task matters. The experiment could vary the relationship between a movement and a room change while asking about an object of known identity. It could not determine how frequently ordinary people forget errands, nor whether a given forgotten errand was caused by a door.

The 2006 report described poorer access to information after moving between rooms. The interpretation was an **event-model updating** account: the mind forms a representation of the current situation, then updates it when a significant boundary indicates that one episode has ended and another has begun.

The implication is not that the previous representation has been wiped from existence. A memory can be intact yet temporarily difficult to access. Retrieval depends on what cues the situation makes available.

Source: Radvansky and Copeland, "Walking through doorways causes forgetting: Situation models and experienced space," *Memory & Cognition* 34 (2006), 1150–1156. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17128613/

## Taking the experiment outside the screen

A virtual door is a strange object. It looks like a door, but crossing it does not require the same locomotion, sensory changes or embodied context as entering another room. In 2011 Radvansky, Sabine Krawietz and Andrea Tamplin extended the inquiry, exploring variations in the degree of immersion and conducting an experiment in an actual physical environment.

Their article, "Walking through Doorways Causes Forgetting: Further Explorations," was published online August 1, 2011. It asked whether the change depended on immersive experience or on the context shift itself. This work strengthened the argument that boundaries can affect retrieval beyond a single computer-game arrangement.

But even physical movement in an experiment is not the same as an unplanned memory lapse at home. Participants still know they are in a study, handle assigned objects, and answer prompts. The dependent measure is selected by an investigator, not chosen by everyday life. This is the price of identifying a causal factor with any discipline: one gives up some realism to gain comparison.

Source: Radvansky, Krawietz and Tamplin (2011), *Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology* 64, 1632–1645. https://doi.org/10.1080/17470218.2011.571267

## The experiment that refused to cooperate

In March 2021 Jessica McFadyen and colleagues published a study with a deliberately sobering title: "Doorways do not always cause forgetting: a multimodal investigation."

They conducted **four experiments**. Two used immersive virtual reality, the second adding a working-memory load. A third involved passively watching movement through an environment, and a fourth active movement in a real environment. Instead of merely reporting whether participants remembered the right item, the researchers considered **hits** and **false alarms**—a distinction that becomes crucial.

Across the four experiments, they did not obtain a general significant forgetting effect attributable to doors. A narrower effect emerged in the loaded virtual-reality condition. Under that additional demand, participants became more likely to incorrectly accept a mismatched memory probe after a boundary. In other words, their ability to distinguish a target from a similar lure deteriorated.

That is not the same as demonstrating that the original item had been deleted. The study reported no significant doorway-related reduction in hits in either VR experiment. Its evidence concerned increased **false alarms** in one condition.

Imagine a test that asks whether the object you just handled was a red cone. A failure to recognize a real red cone and an erroneous "yes" to a blue cone are different kinds of mistake. The first can look like loss; the second can indicate weaker discrimination under interference. Collapsing them into "the doorway made people forget" erases the difference that the newer study took pains to measure.

The authors did not claim that every previous doorway experiment was wrong. They argued that the effect's generalizability and robustness could be sensitive to seemingly modest changes in procedure. Their conclusion invites better models rather than a dramatic reversal.

Source: McFadyen et al., "Doorways do not always cause forgetting: a multimodal investigation," *BMC Psychology* 9, article 41 (2021). https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-021-00536-3

## A door is not necessarily the ingredient

Another 2021 paper, by Matthew Logie and David Donaldson, tested event segmentation in a virtual learning environment. Their experiments used word lists, boundaries and spatial-temporal gaps. Some arrangements helped later memory organization and recall. Crucially, **doorways were not required** for the observed memory benefits; gaps in the sequence could do some of the work.

This is an important turn in the story. Boundaries might sometimes impair immediate access to an earlier detail while helping memories form more organized episodes for later use. A theory that says boundaries are *always bad for memory* cannot explain both sides.

The distinction between what is being tested matters as much as the effect itself: short-term object recognition during navigation, organization of word lists across learning episodes and the spontaneous recovery of a household intention are not one interchangeable measurement.

Source: Logie and Donaldson, "Do doorways really matter: Investigating memory benefits of event segmentation in a virtual learning environment," *Cognition* (2021). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33422863/

## The 2024 visual working-memory result

A study published in 2024 examined visual event boundaries with animated movement through rooms. Observers encoded pictures in wall frames and were tested on them after a delay. In some sequences, a doorway was crossed during that delay; in others, it was not. The researchers controlled elapsed time, travel distance and distractibility.

They reported a consistent decrement for the first image encoded in the doorway condition compared with the no-doorway condition, even when observers were instructed to actively maintain the image in visual working memory.

This result is important, but it should not be used as a retroactive proof that every doorway manipulation affects every kind of memory. Its medium, target material, task and measure differed from those in the 2021 multimodal study. The research landscape remains more interesting than the viral formula "walking through doors makes your brain forget."

Source: "Visual event boundaries trigger forgetting despite active maintenance in visual working memory" (2024), open full text at https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11401123/

## One memory problem, three different questions

It helps to separate three things that are often compressed into one anecdote.

**What was stored?** A participant might encode a colored shape, a word list, or an intention to fetch an object. These representations have different demands and can be supported by different cues.

**What changed?** Movement through a door can change location, visual context, task, expectation and the boundary between perceived events. A good experiment tries to vary one feature while holding the others as constant as practical.

**How was retrieval tested?** Recognizing an exact object, declining a plausible distractor, freely recalling a word and remembering why one stood up are not the same result. A robust conclusion about one does not automatically transfer to the others.

This approach is consistent with the broader 2024 review by Heather Bailey and Maverick Smith on event perception and memory. Their review describes experiences as connected actions organized in time, while emphasizing the distance between laboratory stimulus studies and the complex texture of real-world experience. The public abstract and preview do not justify stronger claims about the exact causes of anyone's particular lapse.

Source: Bailey and Smith, "Event perception and event memory in real-world experience," *Nature Reviews Psychology* 3 (2024), 754–766. https://doi.org/10.1038/s44159-024-00367-0

## A field demonstration you can actually use

Try this without turning it into a test of personal intelligence. Before walking to another room for a harmless task—fetching a book, bringing back a mug—**name the object and purpose in a short sentence**. After you arrive, note whether the intention was still accessible. On another occasion, perform the same sort of action without naming it first.

Do not calculate an "effectiveness rate" from two attempts, and do not engineer hazardous distractions to make the test dramatic. Random interruptions, repetition and expectations make this an informal observation, not controlled research. If you already have memory concerns, the demonstration does not diagnose anything.

The value is in treating an intention as something you can *record and retrieve*, rather than assigning every lapse to a hidden mechanism. A sticky note or a short checklist can serve the same practical purpose, without claiming to alter the brain.

## The older archive: witnesses and explanations

What does a modern room-navigation study have to do with an eighteenth-century history archive? Not the mechanism. There is no responsible route from a doorway experiment to a claim that magnetic somnambulism works.

The connection is the much older problem of **how an observation becomes an explanation**. In the [Puységur Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/), historical witnesses recorded speech, apparent memory changes, predictions, claimed cures and failures. The surviving record can help reconstruct what people reported and how they interpreted it. It cannot, by itself, validate their proposed force.

The same discipline applies here. A person forgetting an errand is an observation; a claim that crossing a threshold erased it is an explanation. The experiments provide stronger evidence for certain controlled boundary effects, and the replications put needed limits on a simplified story. Neither gives permission to invent the mechanism for a specific forgotten errand.

This is also why an audio product advertised as improving focus cannot be validated by citing a doorway experiment. Memory retrieval, meditation practice, auditory stimulation and hypnosis belong to overlapping but distinct lines of research. The question "Could a routine make it easier to begin a task?" is not the same as "Has this branded recording been shown to prevent memory lapses?"

## What this story establishes

The doorway effect is not a magic brain reset. Some controlled studies found worse memory after event boundaries. Others failed to reproduce the common version of the effect, or found a narrower change in false alarms under load. Research on event segmentation also shows circumstances in which boundaries help organize later remembering. The careful statement is **event boundaries can change what information is accessible and how it is organized under particular conditions**.

The purpose of the investigation is not to turn a small everyday lapse into a law of the mind. It is to make the strength, meaning and disagreement in the evidence visible.

When someone next tells you that a doorway erased your thought, there is a better question than "Is that true?" Ask: *Which experiment, with what task, and which measure?* Once those three questions are answered, the apparent simplicity of the story gives way to something much more useful.
