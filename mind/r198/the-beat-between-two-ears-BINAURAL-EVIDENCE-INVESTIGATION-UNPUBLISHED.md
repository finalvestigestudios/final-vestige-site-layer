## The sound that isn't in either ear

Imagine two headphones, each carrying one steady tone. The left channel plays a pitch of 200 hertz; the right plays 210. Neither speaker plays a ten-hertz tone. Yet under some listening conditions, the listener may experience a slow rhythmic fluctuation. The perceived beat is an interaction in auditory processing, not a hidden ten-hertz oscillator tucked into the track. This is the basic premise of a *binaural beat*: slightly different frequencies presented separately to the two ears can produce the percept of a third, beat-like rhythm.

The tempting leap comes next. If a person perceives a ten-hertz beat, does the entire brain begin oscillating at ten hertz? Does that make the listener relaxed, focused, unusually creative, or suddenly proficient at meditation? Does any given paid MP3 produce those changes reliably? Those are four different claims. They require four different kinds of proof.

In 2023, Ruth Maria Ingendoh, Ella S. Posny, and Angela Heine collected fourteen published experiments that had examined the proposed neural-entrainment mechanism through electroencephalography (EEG). Five reported results consistent with the hypothesis. Eight reported contradictory results; one had mixed results. The authors concluded that experimental approaches were heterogeneous and the EEG evidence inconsistent. That does **not** show that sound has no influence on a listener. It does show why an intriguing auditory illusion cannot stand in as proof of a commercial mental-performance promise. [1]

## A listener and a laboratory are not the same thing

A binaural beat is not identical to an ordinary acoustic beat. Put two near frequencies into the air from the same loudspeaker and their waves can physically combine into fluctuations in pressure. For a binaural beat, the two tones are separated across stereo channels: the percept arises when auditory information from the ears is combined. This is why headphone separation matters in experiments, and why playing a "binaural" file on a phone speaker may alter what a listener actually receives.

Nor should the perceived rhythm be confused with the EEG frequency labels familiar from brain science. Alpha, theta and beta are categories describing aspects of recorded brain activity, not buttons that an audio file can simply press. The match between the numerical beat frequency and an EEG band is a *hypothesis about influence*, not itself evidence of an effect.

A genuine study of the proposed mechanism needs much more than "participants heard a beat." Researchers must choose carrier pitches, frequency differences, loudness, stimulus length, whether to mask the tones with other audio, when to record EEG, which electrodes and analysis windows to use, and how to distinguish a stimulus-following response from broader changes in oscillatory activity. Different decisions can produce results that are difficult to compare. The 2023 review emphasizes precisely this methodological variation. [1]

An EEG trace is also not a report card for daily life. Even if a controlled experiment detected a change in recorded activity at a specified frequency, the finding would not automatically show greater concentration, lower anxiety, better memory, or an outcome that lasts beyond listening. Conversely, a self-report of pleasant relaxation does not by itself prove the predicted EEG mechanism occurred. The honest interpretation keeps the measured mechanism and the experienced outcome on separate pages.

## Why the scientific summaries appear to disagree

Here is the apparent contradiction. In 2019, Garcia-Argibay, Santed, and Reales published a meta-analysis of 22 studies covering memory, attention, anxiety and pain perception. From 35 effect sizes, they reported an overall statistically significant medium effect, Hedges' g approximately 0.45. They explored timing of exposure and other study characteristics. [2]

A later review focusing specifically on attention and memory, by Basu and Banerjee (2023), identified fifteen studies and 31 effect sizes and reported a pooled g of approximately 0.40. It also explicitly described conflicting outcomes, particularly for different beat frequencies and particular attention or memory tasks, and called for more robust designs. [3]

Now return to the Ingendoh review: fourteen EEG studies, inconsistent evidence for the entrainment mechanism itself. [1] These reports are not simple votes on one identical question. A broad meta-analysis of outcomes, a narrower analysis of cognitive tasks, and an EEG-mechanism review select different studies and endpoints. A significant *average* across varied interventions and populations does not mean every recording works, nor that every endpoint improves. A mixed mechanism literature does not automatically invalidate every behavioral result. There may be several pathways through which listening changes experience, including ordinary sound preference, expectation, attention to the task, or other factors that particular studies have not isolated.

One more caution comes from a 2024 systematic review by Chee and colleagues. Across thirty-one studies of music, ambient and white noise, and binaural beats in relation to autonomic arousal and cognition, they found the relevant physiological and cognitive evidence mixed or insufficient for firm conclusions. The review noted that arousal measures and performance were often evaluated separately. [4]

The practical lesson is less dramatic than "brainwave audio is a hoax" or "brainwave audio is proven." It is that a claim's **endpoint** matters. Ask whether the evidence concerns a percept, an EEG signal, a laboratory attention score, self-rated mood, a clinical outcome, or the actual purchased audio library. There is no universal promotion from one category to the next.

## A small story about persuasion, from the archives

Before digital audio, another technology of expectation traveled through European salons. In the late eighteenth century, Franz Anton Mesmer's proposed animal magnetism offered a sweeping explanation for health and behavior. The French royal commission's experiments in 1784 investigated claims made for magnetism, including the roles of belief and imagination. What those observers could demonstrate under their circumstances was different from the sweeping theory marketed around the practice.

Puységur's subsequent accounts of magnetic somnambulism pose a related archival problem. His case materials preserve experiences, descriptions and witness testimony. They are historically significant. They do not become controlled modern evidence just because an account is detailed or a witness appears sincere. The Final Vestige **Puységur Animal Magnetism Files** keeps that distinction explicit, with case dossiers, primary references and source trails. [5]

An honest modern audio guide ought to make the same separation. People may sincerely find a track enjoyable, reassuring or useful as a personal routine. The report can be worth listening to. But a testimonial does not by itself isolate the cause of the experience; a neuroscience word in an advertisement does not supply the missing study.

This is a history-of-evidence comparison, not an assertion that binaural-beat researchers and eighteenth-century magnetists used identical methods or that either research program reached the same conclusion. Modern EEG experiments involve defined stimulation and measurable signals; an archival magnetism dossier concerns different questions. The common editorial habit is to ask: *which observation supports which inference?*

## A no-equipment experiment that doesn't pretend to be neuroscience

You do not need a special track to practice distinguishing an observation from its explanation.

Take a sheet of paper and draw three columns: **What was presented**, **What I noticed**, and **What I can reasonably infer**. In row one write: "Ten minutes of quiet sitting, without audio." Afterwards record whether you felt restless, calm or distracted. In the inference column, avoid assigning a biological cause to those feelings.

On a different day, if you already own ordinary music you like, listen at a comfortable volume for ten minutes and make the same notes. Keep the time of day and preceding activity as similar as reasonably possible. Notice when your mind reaches for causal language: "The sound changed my brainwaves," or "Silence made me concentrate." Your informal notes cannot establish either mechanism. You have not randomized conditions, blinded yourself, used a validated attention task or measured EEG. The useful result is learning the difference between *what you experienced* and *why you think it happened*.

## What an audio seller can prove, and what it cannot infer

For a reader evaluating a twelve-minute meditation-audio product, three categories should be kept apart.

**Contents and format.** An official product page can tell you what the seller advertises: number of tracks, lengths, levels, file type, support materials and a stated refund policy. These are descriptions of an offer. The merchant is the primary source for what it claims to sell. Actual delivery, compatibility, checkout totals and refund performance require their own verification.

**Mechanism.** If the seller invokes "brainwave entrainment" as an explanation, the purchaser can ask which frequency, stimulus, listening setup, measured EEG effect, duration and comparison group support that exact description. A general review of binaural beats is not proof that the specific purchased files produce an effect of that size.

**Human outcome.** The proposition that listening will reliably improve concentration, treat anxiety, alter sleep, or deliver a certain quantity of meditation is stronger still. Good evidence would identify participants, randomization, appropriate controls, predefined outcome measures, meaningful follow-up, harms, and product identity. A study of another soundtrack, a clinical hypnosis protocol or intensive meditation training is not an efficacy study of a particular store's MP3.

For this reason Final Vestige's related, independently written Zen12 product guide is deliberately separate from this investigation. No buying link or commercial recommendation is embedded here. The investigation remains informative regardless of whether an affiliate partnership ever exists.

## The apparently attractive comparison: twelve minutes equals an hour

The phrase is memorable because it sounds quantitative. But what is the unit of "an hour of meditation"? A minute of clock time cannot be replaced by a feeling of depth without defining and measuring an outcome. Is the seller referring to a brain signal, subjective calm, task performance, accumulated practice, or something else? Are listeners trained meditators or beginners? What happens on day thirty rather than minute twelve?

The official Zen12 page describes a twelve-minute session and makes a one-hour meditation equivalence claim. This investigation has not identified an independent, adequately controlled evaluation of **that exact branded product** establishing such equivalence. We therefore report the slogan only as an attributed seller claim, not a conclusion. [6]

This distinction is compatible with acknowledging that some peer-reviewed syntheses report positive aggregate findings for binaural-beat interventions on selected measures. It would be unscientific to erase those findings; it would be equally unscientific to assign them to an untested commercial program without a matching protocol.

## A buyer's ten-question evidence audit

Before paying for a mind-related audio library, ask these questions and keep the answers next to their sources:

1. What exact files, formats and manuals are included at the final checkout stage?
2. Does the audio require headphones or another specific listening setup?
3. Does the seller provide a complete sample that can be evaluated without purchase?
4. What is the difference between the seller's **product specification** and a promised **effect**?
5. Can the claimed effect be located in a trial of this precise product, not an adjacent technique?
6. Was the comparison a matched soundtrack, silence, sham audio, an active practice or no treatment?
7. Which outcome was measured: EEG, task performance, subjective rating, clinical symptoms or something else?
8. Does the result apply to the audience and duration being advertised?
9. Can the final payment, taxes, renewal/upsell path and refund terms be independently checked?
10. Would you still value the structured audio files if the advertised psychological outcome did not occur?

A "yes" to the last question may indicate a straightforward convenience purchase. A "no" is a reason to demand stronger product-specific proof before spending. Neither answer makes the reader gullible; it locates the decision in what the buyer actually wants.

## The important limits

The studies in this investigation were obtained through primary indexed records and reviews, not an exhaustive systematic search performed by Final Vestige. We have not reanalyzed individual participant data. Effect sizes summarize selected literatures, and study overlap, methodological heterogeneity and publication bias can affect interpretations. Not all auditory beat methods are identical, and even a correctly reported population-average effect cannot guarantee an individual result.

The 2023 EEG review's five/eight/one split reflects studies grouped by its authors' criteria. It is not a referendum by every experiment ever conducted, and it does not prove the mechanism impossible. The pooled estimates in the other syntheses must not be rounded into a guaranteed percentage improvement for a consumer. Clinical or psychological conditions require qualified care, not extrapolation from a commercial recording.

The safest editorial conclusion is not that listening is worthless. It is that **the listener can judge an audio product for its stated contents and personal convenience without pretending the underlying science has established every sales promise**.
