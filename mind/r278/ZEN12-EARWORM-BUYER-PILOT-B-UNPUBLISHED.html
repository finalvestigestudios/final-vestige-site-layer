01 / The evidence behind the feeling

The song in your head wasn't playing outside it.

Involuntary musical imagery—often called an earworm—occurs when musical fragments arrive unbidden. A 2020 review mapped 47 studies in 33 articles. A 2017 study compared the musical features of songs nominated as earworms with other popular songs. Studies of memory are real; the leap from an internally replayed tune to a specific product's alleged effect is not justified.

Find the full source-led manuscript in the unpublished Mind R278 research set: The Song That Played Without a Speaker. It is not a public destination yet.

Three different questions

What did you hear? That is observation.

Why did it happen? That is a mechanism requiring investigation.

What should you buy? That is a value, trust and checkout decision—not a lab result.

05 / One useful demonstration

The twelve-minute preference test.

Before visiting any checkout, use a free timer for twelve minutes on one day. On another day, if you already own an ordinary music track you enjoy and can legally use, listen to that for twelve minutes instead. Jot down whether either format was pleasant, manageable and worth repeating. You need not play anything unfamiliar, submit personal data or buy anything.

These subjective notes can help identify your preferences. They do not measure brainwave entrainment, isolate placebo effects or prove clinical effectiveness. Two unblinded sessions are not an efficacy trial.
