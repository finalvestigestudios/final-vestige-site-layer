# The Work You Left Behind Followed You

**Final Vestige Studios — Mind & Perception Archive**  
*An unpublished investigation into attention residue, interruptions and the science of finding your way back. Editorial research, not a product endorsement. 10 November 2026.*

The message appears while you're almost finished. You are writing the last sentence of something difficult when another task demands your attention. You answer it; you return; and the original sentence suddenly looks as though somebody else wrote it. The trouble is not necessarily that you forgot what you were doing. You may have brought too much of the first task with you into the second.

There is a revealing asymmetry here. We usually describe an interruption as something that damages the work we were doing before it arrived. But an unfinished task can also interfere with the work that arrives *after* it. The mind does not always close the first file simply because the hand opens another.

That is the hypothesis behind **attention residue**. It is an unusually useful piece of perception research because its evidence concerns something people can recognize without a laboratory demonstration: thoughts about the earlier job continue competing for attention, even while the person is ostensibly engaged in the next one.

The hypothesis is not a diagnosis, a brain scan, a law of productivity, or evidence that a particular meditation recording repairs your attention. Its value is narrower, and therefore more interesting. It gives us a way to ask what happens when a task ends on the calendar before it ends in the mind.

## A workday can contain two different interruptions

Imagine an editor has been checking a historical document against a transcription. One discrepancy remains unresolved. The editor is then asked to review a different article. There are now two possible problems.

The obvious one will occur later: when the editor returns to the original source, the context may need to be reconstructed. Where was that disputed line? Which version of the manuscript was authoritative? What was being checked?

The less obvious problem happens *now*, while the editor is reading the second article. The transcription dispute keeps returning to mind. Even if the second article is perfectly comprehensible, portions of attention may be occupied by the unresolved first question.

Both problems matter, but they are different outcomes. A study of how quickly people resume the interrupted job does not automatically tell us whether they performed well on the temporary job. Conversely, reducing distraction during the interruption does not prove that returning to the original task is effortless.

That distinction is central to the experimental record.

## What Sophie Leroy actually tested

In 2009, Sophie Leroy published a paper in *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes* titled *Why Is It So Hard to Do My Work? The Challenge of Attention Residue When Switching Between Work Tasks*. Two experiments investigated the transition between tasks. The research found that people could carry cognitive activity about an earlier task into the next one, with consequences for performance. Importantly, simply finishing the earlier task was not a universally sufficient condition for disengagement: the circumstances surrounding completion, including time pressure, mattered.

This is more precise than the casual maxim that multitasking is bad. The studies did not ask whether every person can perform only one simple action at once, nor did they calculate a universal number of minutes lost per switch. They examined specific transitions and a proposed explanation for performance changes.

A later article by Leroy and Theresa Glomb, published in *Organization Science* in 2018, investigated an even more awkward situation: a person working under time pressure is interrupted, knows the abandoned task will still demand attention on return, and must perform a different task in the meantime.

The anticipation of a difficult return can itself become part of the interruption. A person may keep rehearsing what remains unfinished because letting go feels risky. The mind is trying to preserve the original work, but that preservation can tax the next job.

Across four studies, Leroy and Glomb examined a **ready-to-resume plan**: a brief reflection on the interrupted task and a concrete plan for returning to it. Their reported results supported the possibility that this intervention reduced attention residue and improved performance on the interrupting task. [Original 2018 paper](https://doi.org/10.1287/orsc.2017.1184); [University of Washington research summary](https://www.washington.edu/news/2018/01/16/task-interrupted-a-plan-for-returning-helps-you-move-on/).

That result has a quietly striking implication. The helpful act was not a special sound, a hypnotic state, or a demand to empty the mind. It was a plan. Instead of holding an incomplete job in memory as an unresolved alarm, participants had an explicit place to return.

## The seductive version of the story is not the full story

Popular explanations often combine attention residue with another psychological idea: the **Zeigarnik effect**, commonly summarized as the claim that unfinished tasks are remembered better than finished ones. The two ideas are not synonyms.

The Zeigarnik claim concerns **recall** of completed and interrupted tasks. Attention residue concerns **continued task-related cognition during another activity**, and in Leroy and Glomb's research, performance on that interrupting activity. A third question—whether somebody voluntarily **resumes** an interrupted task—is associated with the Ovsiankina effect.

In July 2025, Romain Ghibellini and Beat Meier published an open-access meta-analysis in *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications* covering 59 publications on the Zeigarnik and Ovsiankina effects. Using weighted comparisons, they found **no general memory advantage** for unfinished tasks, while finding a broad tendency to resume interrupted work. Across the publications for which the interrupted-versus-completed recall ratio was computed, the weighted ratio was approximately 0.99, effectively parity rather than a universal memory benefit. [Read the 2025 meta-analysis](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-025-05000-w).

This is not a refutation of Leroy's experiments. It is a warning against converting a family of distinct findings into one folk-psychology rule. If a person can't stop thinking about an unfinished task, it does not follow that they will remember it more accurately later. If they return to the task, it does not establish that they were distracted throughout their time away. Each proposition requires its own test.

The most reliable way to use psychology is to preserve the measurement that generated the result. A memorable name is not a substitute for a careful outcome definition.

## The study that gave an interruption a landing place

The 2018 research becomes especially useful when we examine its limits rather than just its headline. The University of Washington's summary describes a field survey of 202 working professionals and a series of laboratory studies. The two intervention experiments included groups of 66 and 44 student participants. Subjects completed tasks such as examining résumés under experimental conditions. The intervention involved writing out how they would return to the earlier task.

These are meaningful controlled observations, but they are not evidence of a universally effective workplace protocol. An experimental interruption while evaluating a résumé does not have the same demands as emergency dispatch, classroom teaching, programming, or a clinical consultation. The researchers did not establish that the intervention prevents every attention lapse, or that it has the same effect during every kind of interruption.

Most importantly, the study focused on performance **during the interrupting task**. It did not directly demonstrate that people later resumed the original task faster, with fewer errors, or with better long-term productivity. In the university's account, that question was explicitly left for future research.

The finding is interesting enough without adding a result that was not measured.

## Try the return note—not as a cure, but as an observation

Here is a small, original exercise inspired by the question the research asked. It is a personal workflow check, not a replication, clinical intervention, or proof of what caused any improvement.

When you have to leave a task unfinished, take a sheet of paper or a local text note. Write three short lines:

**Where I stopped:** “Comparing the second eyewitness account with the original date.”

**What remains unresolved:** “Source B gives a different month; check the archive index.”

**My first action on return:** “Open the index at the bookmarked reference, then compare both dates.”

Now set the note aside and deliberately move to the next task. Notice whether thoughts about the first task recur. If they do, you may choose to remind yourself that the return route is recorded; you do not need to rehearse it continuously.

The important variable isn't whether you feel serene or whether a timer tells you to stop. It's whether you can describe *what you are measuring*. Did the new task receive your full attention? Did you forget your return step? Did you complete anything? Were circumstances comparable across different days? A single pleasant experience cannot isolate the cause.

If there is no interruption, do not manufacture one merely for the exercise. If the interrupted work is safety-critical or subject to professional handover procedures, follow those procedures instead of a casual note. For an ordinary writing or study task, a brief return record is a low-cost way to test whether uncertainty about unfinished work is part of the problem.

## A historical parallel: the explanation arrives after the experience

Final Vestige's published [Puységur Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) and [Mesmerism investigation](https://finalvestige.com/2026/11/02/mesmerism/) document an earlier period in which remarkable reported experiences were surrounded by competing explanations. The historical actors, institutions, procedures and stakes are not the same as those in modern cognitive experiments. The comparison is methodological, not a claim that animal magnetism is equivalent to contemporary meditation, hypnosis or task-switching research.

In the late eighteenth century, the important question was not simply whether participants reported unusual sensations or behaviors. It was what observations could actually distinguish the suggested mechanism from competing explanations. Today's temptation is more familiar: a marketing page invokes an EEG frequency or a special recording, and an ordinary subjective experience is presented as proof of that mechanism.

Evidence of a subjective change is evidence that *something was experienced*. Evidence of a change in task performance under a specific design is stronger, but only for the outcome that was measured. Neither automatically establishes the effectiveness of a branded audio recording.

That last boundary is especially important where a real difficulty with attention becomes a sales hook.

## The most useful question after an interruption

The ordinary question is, “Why can't I concentrate?” The more discriminating one is, “What is my mind still trying not to lose?”

The answer may be a missing bookmark, an unfinished argument, a promise to somebody else, or the worry that the next interruption will make the original job impossible to recover. Some of these are practical problems that can be addressed with a plan. Others will need a different explanation or help entirely.

An honest research article should not claim to know which one applies to you. It can show you what researchers measured, which claims survived scrutiny, and why a piece of paper may sometimes be a better first experiment than a product advertised to change your brainwaves.

The work you left behind might follow you. That does not mean you need a miracle to find your way back.
