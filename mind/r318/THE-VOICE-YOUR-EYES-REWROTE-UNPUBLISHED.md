## The sound that wasn't on the soundtrack

Imagine a very short recording: a person faces the camera, speaks a syllable, and stops. You listen carefully. Nothing seems ambiguous until you are told to close your eyes and play the same soundtrack. The sound you report changes. The recording has not. What changed is the evidence your brain was permitted to use.

In December 1976, Harry McGurk and John MacDonald described a particularly striking version of this phenomenon in *Nature*. A film of a speaker articulating /ga/ was synchronized with an audio recording of /ba/. Adult listeners could report /da/: a syllable that was neither the sound alone nor the visible articulation alone. The reverse mismatch could produce combination reports such as /bagba/ or /gaba/. Hearing the soundtrack alone or seeing matched recordings did not produce the same mismatch in the conditions described in their abstract. [S1]

This became known as the **McGurk effect**, one of experimental psychology's most recognizable examples of multisensory perception. Its lesson is deeper—and narrower—than the internet's preferred formulation that "your ears lie." Sight is part of the normal evidence available to someone understanding a speaker. Under contrived conflicting conditions, the listener's final speech report can differ from the words a silent audio recorder would register.

It is not evidence that someone can control thoughts from a video, that hearing can be "reprogrammed," or that any meditation or hypnosis product changes the mechanism.

## What the researchers actually manipulated

A camera gives two channels that usually agree: moving lips and accompanying speech. A filmmaker can uncouple them. McGurk and MacDonald used mismatched dubbing to test how a perceiver deals with two streams that appear to come from one speaker but offer conflicting information. This is an experiment in *cue combination*, not an experiment in credulity or obedience.

Consider the three records one ought to keep:

1. **The audio track:** the spoken consonant and vowel physically on the soundtrack.
2. **The visible mouth:** the articulation presented in the image.
3. **The listener's report:** the syllable the person says they perceived when the two are played together.

The report is data, but it is not identical to either input. A listener is not necessarily pretending, guessing, or making an error of attention. A coherent conscious percept can emerge from conflicting inputs.

The mismatch must be kept in the story. Taking the famous /ba/–/ga/–/da/ sequence out of context makes it sound like everyday conversation is fundamentally unreliable. In ordinary conversation, mouth and voice are usually congruent. Real speech perception is the product of probabilistic cues, context and familiar communication, not a permanent neurological trick.

## A 98 percent result that does not describe everyone

The original report is often associated with exceptionally high rates of fusion. A 2018 *Scientific Reports* investigation states that McGurk and MacDonald reported illusion perception in **98% of adults** in their original setting, whereas later estimates have ranged from **17% to 81%**. [S2] Those numbers do not describe one stable property shared by all humans. They reflect different stimuli, participant groups and laboratory protocols.

The 2018 researchers asked a useful historical question: why did the original stimulus appear so potent? Many modern experiments presented one short mismatched syllable. The initial experiment used repeated, co-articulated syllables: the mouth and sound moved through a longer sequence. Co-articulation means that one speech movement is affected by the movements around it. Context within the recording itself can change how convincing it feels that the mouth and sound share a single source.

In an experiment with 76 participants, the 2018 team compared a single-syllable presentation against a longer co-articulated, repeated sequence. They reported mean McGurk responses of **48% versus 68%**, respectively, with both figures belonging to that study's stimuli and measurement conditions—not a population-wide detection rate. The article also explicitly critiques simple statistical treatment of proportion outcomes and reports additional mixed-effects modeling. [S2]

A single extra design detail altered the illusion substantially. That is the kind of finding that should make a careful investigator curious rather than sensational.

## The question a viral demonstration cannot answer

Suppose a short-form video shows a talking face and invites viewers to hear a surprising syllable. Some viewers may report it, some may hear the actual audio, and others may hear another combination. A comment section will mix headphone differences, phone speakers, volume, prior expectations, replays and the creator's own framing.

Even perfect-looking engagement statistics cannot establish the prevalence of the illusion in the audience. Nor can a creator infer cognition, mental health, suggestibility or hearing condition from a single self-report.

A 2015 study of **360 participants and 20 McGurk stimuli** documented large differences among both people and recorded talkers. Some participants never gave illusion responses across repeated trials, while others did so consistently. The authors also found that forcing respondents to select from offered answers increased apparent illusion frequency relative to open-ended responding by an estimated **18 percentage points**. [S3] The way a question is asked can change the measured answer.

This makes the form of a social-video poll a serious methodological decision. Asking "Did you hear da?" points at a response. Asking "What did you hear? Write it before opening the explanation" better preserves the distinction between observation and suggestion, though it still does not create a controlled experiment.

## The brain result and its boundary

Researchers have also examined brain activity during mismatched audiovisual speech. Nath and Beauchamp's 2012 neuroimaging work compared people who frequently perceived McGurk fusion with people who did not. The study reported an association between susceptibility and activity in the **left superior temporal sulcus**, a region implicated in audiovisual integration. [S4]

Association is not a wand that establishes a causal switch. The study was about responses to a specific kind of lab stimulus. It cannot tell us that some people have superior minds, that the region can be tuned by a marketed sound file, or that any participant's preferences or beliefs are manipulated by watching lips.

The attractive but unsupported jump would be: "If visual information rewrites perceived speech, guided audio can rewrite habits." The first clause refers to a well-defined perceptual experiment. The second invokes behavior change and a different intervention. The causal bridge between them has not been supplied.

## The sharper critique arrived decades later

In 2022, Van Engen, Dey, Sommers and Peelle argued that research should move beyond the McGurk effect when evaluating *everyday audiovisual speech*. Their review explained that isolated, conflicting syllables are artificial: ordinary conversation consists of meaningful words and sentences, with audio and vision generally aligning. Susceptibility to the illusion does not reliably stand in for skill understanding natural speech with visible faces. [S5]

That critique does not demolish McGurk and MacDonald's finding. It asks an essential question about what a laboratory outcome measures. A precise experimental tool can demonstrate a mechanism without being a suitable proxy for all the behaviors that seem vaguely related to it.

The difference is the essence of research literacy. A finding may be real, reproducible and dramatic, and still not establish the convenient application a seller would like to draw from it.

## History: from the visible witness to the written witness

The Final Vestige archive on Puységur's animal magnetism documents a radically different era of persuasive evidence. The operator recorded extraordinary statements, predictions, experiences and testimony, sometimes supported by signatures and notarial acts. The archive keeps failed predictions, conflicts, patient vulnerability and operator pressure in the file instead of smoothing away the contradictions. [FV1]

There is a structural resemblance, not scientific equivalence. In both settings, what a person *reports* requires interpretation. Yet the evidentiary differences are enormous. The McGurk experiments deliberately control the mismatch between visual and auditory sources. Puységur worked within an eighteenth-century doctrine, with uneven controls and serious consent concerns. Modern audiovisual research does **not** vindicate animal magnetism, hypnotic obedience, clairvoyance or nineteenth-century therapeutic claims.

The useful bridge is procedural: record what was presented, record what was perceived or alleged, and avoid silently promoting the report into proof of a preferred mechanism.

## A small source-aware exercise—without fabricated footage

You can practice the method without creating a fake McGurk demonstration.

Take a twenty-second clip of someone speaking with captions available (with their permission and rights cleared). Before watching, note who produced it and whether the caption is autogenerated, edited or human reviewed. Play the clip normally and write down a phrase that seems ambiguous. Then listen with the screen hidden. Compare your transcription to the visual-and-audio version and, if possible, to a trusted original transcript.

You have not reproduced the McGurk effect: the clip may contain no intentionally mismatched phonemes. Instead you have practiced distinguishing three records—audio, visual cues and interpretation. That is worthwhile in its own right. Do not use other people's voice or face recordings to deceive or embarrass them, and do not infer an individual's cognitive status from the outcome.

For a classroom demonstration of the actual illusion, use a researcher-authorized clip with clearly credited rights, consent and experimental caveats. The original 1976 film is **not** reproduced or licensed by this article.

## A record worth keeping

The McGurk effect is not proof that perception can be commanded. It is proof that a coherent report can arise from more than one sense, and that an apparently simple question—"What did you hear?"—can have an answer shaped by the visible context and by the way the experiment was built.

One of the most revealing numbers in the entire story is not the striking 98% from the earliest report. It is the **twenty-point difference** produced in a later controlled comparison by changing how syllables were presented. The same subject, the same broad effect, and a substantially changed result when the stimulus design changed.

A trustworthy investigator preserves that change. A trustworthy seller does not use it to promise something it never tested.
