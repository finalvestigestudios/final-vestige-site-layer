# The Second Number Vanished

**An attentional-blink investigation — unpublished editorial manuscript**  
Final Vestige Studios / Mind & Perception Archive · Research checked 10 November 2026  
**Status:** Source-controlled draft. No commercial recommendation, no clinical advice, no permission to publish.

A single numeral flashes past. You recognize it. A second numeral follows so closely that you look straight at it and still fail to report it. You are not blind, and nobody has dimmed the screen. A fraction of a second has exposed a peculiar rule of attention: recognizing one event can temporarily make another event harder to report.

It sounds like the premise of an illusion video. It is also the subject of an unusually revealing scientific dispute about meditation, attention, and what a short recording can reasonably promise.

In 1992, Jane Raymond, Kimron Shapiro, and Karen Arnell published an experiment in which letters appeared one after another at the same location, a technique called *rapid serial visual presentation* (RSVP). Observers identified a target letter and then tried to detect a second probe. The probe was especially likely to be missed in a short interval after the first target, although it could be detected when it appeared immediately afterward or later in the stream. The authors reported a window of reduced probe detection beginning about 180 milliseconds after the target and lasting roughly 270 milliseconds under their conditions. The paper's name gave the phenomenon its enduring metaphor: the *attentional blink*.[1]

A blink normally means an eyelid closing. Here nothing closes. The eyes can stay fixed on the screen. What briefly falters is the ability to report a second target while processing the first.

This distinction matters. A dazzling demonstration can make a laboratory effect feel universal. The experiment was narrower: specified stimuli, timings, target instructions, and a particular performance measure. It did not prove that the brain stops receiving all information, or that people are helplessly inattentive for the same number of milliseconds in ordinary life.

## What actually disappeared?

Imagine twelve characters appearing in the same spot, roughly one at a time. Your instructions are to remember a white letter embedded in darker distractors and then report whether a second marked character appeared. The second event arrives while you are still doing the work demanded by the first.

The study asks about **conditional report performance**, not mystical invisibility. An observer may be exposed to an item yet unable to identify or report it under the task's conditions. When scientists say that target two, or T2, is often missed, they must also ask whether target one, T1, was identified correctly. If the first target was never identified, it is harder to know what generated the second-target deficit.

A later methodological review by Mary MacLean and Karen Arnell offers a useful discipline: measure the difference in T2 accuracy between short and long T1–T2 separations, rather than mistaking any low score for an attentional blink.[2] A participant who misses every second target because the display is too fast has not demonstrated the same specific deficit as a participant whose second-target accuracy collapses only at one interval.

The next complication is stranger: sometimes a second target presented *immediately* after the first is spared. In the literature this is called lag-one sparing. It is one reason to avoid describing the attentional blink as a simple timer that starts the moment we notice something. The effect depends on the stream, the distractors, the target definitions, and how selection unfolds.

## The race between noticing and knowing

In 1995, Marvin Chun and Mary Potter studied multiple target detection in rapid streams and proposed a two-stage account. An early stage can register candidate items quickly; a subsequent stage is more selective and limited in how it consolidates information for report. Under this model, competition for the later stage can leave a second target vulnerable, particularly at separations around 200–500 milliseconds.[3]

This is an account, not a universally accepted diagram of an invisible machine. Other models emphasize interference by distractors or the way attention admits new inputs; the details remain a research question. In their 1994 follow-up, Shapiro, Raymond, and Arnell found that whether a target was defined as an object, rather than by a time interval, affected the emergence of the blink.[4] The stream is part of the explanation.

That is the extraordinary element often lost in the thirty-second internet version. The second numeral is not necessarily less vivid or farther away. It is caught at the wrong moment in a process we usually experience as seamless.

## An experiment that changed the commercial conversation

The temptation is immediate: if a mental exercise can change the attentional blink, perhaps meditation can help us notice more. In 2007, Heleen Slagter and colleagues published an intriguing study in *PLoS Biology*. After **three months of intensive mental training**, participants in the practitioner group showed a reduced attentional-blink deficit, alongside changes in an EEG measure called the P3b associated with processing the first target.[5]

Those are important findings. They are also easily distorted.

First, this was intensive training over months, not the purchase of one short audio track. Second, the outcome was performance on a rapid visual-target laboratory task, accompanied by an electrophysiological measurement. Third, an EEG change associated with task processing is not a standalone certificate of improved creativity, job performance, stress reduction, or health.

The study suggests that sustained mental practice can alter how some participants allocate attention in this specialized setting. It does not identify a consumer audio product that produces the same change.

The difference between a carefully designed experiment and a slogan is not academic fussiness. It determines whether an audience can make a meaningful buying decision.

## What happens when the session is brief?

A larger test of *brief* meditation drew a more restrained conclusion. Paul Sharpe, Ben Whalley, and Chris Mitchell reported a 2021 experiment comparing brief focused-attention meditation, open-monitoring meditation, and relaxation. Their design included forty participants in each group and assessed attentional-blink performance using both Bayesian and conventional statistical approaches. They found **no evidence of a difference in attentional-blink magnitude** among the three conditions, although some measures of overall target accuracy differed.[6]

This result does not negate the 2007 intensive-training study. The time scales, participants, interventions, and measured contrasts were different. Nor does it prove that short meditation is useless. It does, however, challenge a common shortcut in marketing logic: evidence associated with months of training cannot automatically be transferred to one twelve-minute session.

The 2021 investigators were particularly careful about what the data did and did not say. They did not find the distinct attentional-blink benefit they set out to test. They entertained a narrower possibility involving overall performance; that remains an interpretation requiring further testing, not a commercial promise.

A 2022 computational model by Nadav Amir, Naftali Tishby, and Israel Nelken explored how the effect and its modification might arise. Modeling can clarify plausible mechanisms, but fit to behavioral patterns is not the same thing as testing an advertised recording on customers.[7]

## A twelve-minute observation that requires nothing to buy

You can learn something valuable from this research without pretending to reproduce the original apparatus. In the original experiments, timing is controlled to the millisecond and results are collected over many trials. A casual video played on different phones, under different refresh rates and network conditions, cannot deliver the same standardized evidence.

Instead, try an exercise in *what counts as proof*.

First, choose two moments when you have felt certain you noticed everything on a screen. Write down what you were attending to and what you later discovered you had missed, if anything. Do not assume that memory alone proves an attentional blink. Ordinary distraction, change blindness, weak memory and inattentional blindness are different possibilities.

Next, read the 1992 paper's abstract and predict what would happen if the observer **did not have to identify the first target**. The authors tested this; their characteristic reduction in subsequent probe detection was not found under that condition.[1] Now predict what happens if a brief blank interrupts the post-target stream. Again, the experimental condition matters.

Finally, take a timer and quietly sit for twelve minutes. Count this as an exercise in noticing your own expectations, not as a treatment, performance test, or substitute for the published protocols. Before beginning, write down one modest question: *Am I buying a structured routine, or am I buying a promise about my brain?* Afterward, record what you can honestly say you experienced, and what you cannot know without controlled comparison.

You have measured no improvement in T2 report accuracy. You have not proved that your neural processing changed. But you have practiced something useful: resisting an attractive conclusion that outruns its evidence.

## A historical echo without a historical endorsement

The language of a hidden force influencing perception has a history. In the eighteenth century Franz Anton Mesmer proposed animal magnetism; Armand-Marie-Jacques de Chastenet, the Marquis de Puységur, developed his own influential accounts of magnetic sleep and suggestion. Their sources are valuable evidence of what people reported and believed, but those reports do not establish a transferable force or validate modern brainwave marketing.

Final Vestige's published **Puységur’s Animal Magnetism Files** traces the archival problem: what is a first-person report, what is an interpretation, and what has actually been demonstrated? [8] The site’s published examination of Mesmerism revisits Franklin’s commission and the significance of expectation and controlled comparison.[9]

There is a family resemblance in the *questions*: observers can confidently report real experiences while explanations for those experiences remain contested. There is no scientific warrant for saying that Puységur anticipated the attentional blink, or that a modern audio recording inherits proof from historical hypnotism. The subjects, methods, and evidence are fundamentally different.

## What the second number teaches

The most remarkable feature of the attentional blink is not that we miss something. Everyone knows we miss things. It is that researchers can make the miss depend on timing and the demands of the event that came immediately before.

The 1992 experiment establishes a real constraint of attention under particular conditions. Later models debate why the constraint appears. The 2007 meditation experiment shows an interesting result after intensive training; the 2021 brief-session comparison prevents us from casually treating that result as a universal property of short practice. These are not contradictory slogans. They are different pieces of an unfinished scientific account.

A number vanishes from awareness in a controlled stream. The evidence is compelling precisely because the investigators did not stop at astonishment. They changed one condition, measured another, and asked what remained.

That is a more useful promise than any recording can make: learn to ask what was actually tested before deciding what to believe.
