# The Broken Glass That Wasn't There
*How one question can become part of a memory — and why suggestion is not proof*

**Final Vestige Studios · Mind & Perception · Original investigation · Unpublished editorial draft · 14 November 2026**

> **Evidence standard:** The experiment showed differences in answers after differently worded questions. Its results do not let us read any participant's mind, certify an individual memory as false, or prove that hypnosis recordings alter memory.

## The glass

Imagine a witness sitting down a week after watching a collision. The question on the page is simple: Did you see any broken glass?

The film contained no broken glass. Yet some viewers said yes. What made this detail plausible was not another angle of the accident, a newly discovered photograph, or a second witness. It was a word in a question asked the previous week.

In 1974, Elizabeth Loftus and John Palmer published *Reconstruction of Automobile Destruction: An Example of the Interaction Between Language and Memory* in the *Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior*. Their two experiments are now staples of introductory psychology. Familiarity has sometimes flattened the finding into a cartoon: memory is a lie, and one word rewrites history. The actual research is more interesting, more constrained, and more useful to anyone who works with evidence.

The authors asked people who had viewed films of traffic accidents to estimate speed. Some received a question using a forceful verb; others encountered milder verbs. Their reported speeds differed. A second experiment brought participants back to questions about the filmed collision a week later. Participants previously prompted with the strongest wording more often affirmed broken glass — which had not appeared in the film.

That difference is a measurable laboratory outcome. Why it happened requires more caution. People might reconstruct their recollection, infer what a questioner expects, rely on a general impression of severity, or choose an answer they regard as plausible. These mechanisms are not interchangeable. The study provides evidence that *post-event wording can change later reports*, not a scanner showing the exact contents of every person's remembered scene.

**Primary source:** Loftus & Palmer (1974), [doi:10.1016/S0022-5371(74)80011-3](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-5371(74)80011-3).

## A verb enters the record

The first experiment used several short accident films. Participants answered speed questions in which the crucial word varied. In the second experiment, a separate group saw a collision and answered an initially framed question; one week later came the broken-glass question.

Here is the counterintuitive part. The later question could be grammatically identical for everyone. The earlier framing differed, and so did subsequent answers. A record formed during the first interview can influence the next. The interviewer is not merely extracting information; the form of the question is itself another piece of information that the witness encounters.

In the second experiment, the paper reports 16 of 50 participants in the “smashed” condition answering yes to broken glass, compared with 7 of 50 in the “hit” condition and 6 of 50 in a group not asked the earlier speed question. These are experimental group counts, not the probability that a real-world witness will fabricate a memory or that a particular memory is inaccurate. They also do not prove that all 16 people experienced a detailed visual recollection of glass; the outcome was their response to a question.

The missing-glass test makes a different point from the speed estimate. A number given under suggestive wording can be a judgment made on the spot. A subsequent assertion about a nonexistent feature raises a stronger concern about the information available when the person later reports an event. Still, both experiments measure answers under specified procedures. An investigator who jumps from that to an all-purpose claim that confidence proves nothing is making the same category error the research warns us against.

It matters that no original eyewitness needed to be dishonest for the result to occur. A sincere response can be affected by how evidence was solicited. Sincerity and accuracy answer different questions.

## How a memory can acquire a source it never had

We habitually talk about remembering as if retrieving a file. That metaphor helps when comparing two saved documents, but it obscures human recollection. A scene can be partly remembered, partly inferred, and partly reconstructed as we make sense of it. The word used by the interviewer can become entangled with the image used by the witness.

Source monitoring is the practical problem underneath. Was the image of a particular object seen in the film, inferred from ordinary accidents, mentioned by someone afterward, or supplied by a leading question? The person may remember the content without retaining a reliable label for its origin.

A 2024 meta-analysis by Schincariol, Otgaar, Greene, Murphy, Riesthuis, Mangiulli and Battista examined 13 articles on false news and autobiographical false memory and belief. Its pooled descriptive results suggest that exposure to invented news can be associated with reported memory and belief for some fake events. But the percentages depend heavily on the unit of analysis. Roughly four in ten participants reporting **at least one** false memory is not the same as four in ten fabricated stories being remembered: the authors' separate event-level calculation was around 22 percent. Neither figure can be transferred to an unrelated marketing product or a single eyewitness interview.

The review concerns experimental exposure to fake news, not Zen12, Hypnosis Live, or an archive interview in nineteenth-century France. It offers a modern test of the general distinction between seeing an assertion and remembering an event.

**Review:** Schincariol et al. (2024), [*Fake memories: A meta-analysis on the effect of fake news on the creation of false memories and false beliefs*](https://doi.org/10.1017/mem.2024.14).

## The cost of a confident story

The danger of a leading question extends beyond courtroom scenes. Families revise stories across generations. A photograph acquires a caption years after it was taken. A researcher reads a letter through the lens of what a later biographer says the writer believed. A historical exhibit can put a precise date beneath an object whose original provenance was never so precise.

Each version may be sincere. Each version may also introduce a new suggestion. Consider the difference between “What happened next?” and “Was this when the patient entered a trance?” The second question contains a proposed interpretation before the witness has described an event. If the record then says the witness confirmed a trance, the apparent agreement may be partly an artifact of how the question was formed.

This is not an argument to discard testimony. It is an argument to preserve the **sequence**. First note what was observed without prompting; then record the wording of the questions; then distinguish a later explanation from an earlier description. What changes between versions is part of the evidence.

The distinction has a commercial counterpart. A buyer may enjoy a listening routine and accurately report that it feels pleasant. The seller may then ask whether the buyer is feeling 'more focused, healthier, and transformed.' That framing changes the conversation. Enjoyment is not a controlled performance endpoint. Anecdotal calm is not an independently verified treatment outcome. If the product under review is an audio recording, the claim must concern that actual recording, at that dose, in an appropriate test — not a laboratory finding about memory or a clinical treatment taught by a therapist.

## Back to Puységur: preserve the claim before interpreting the cause

Final Vestige's [Puységur Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) belong to a different era, with different methods and documentary limits. Accounts of apparent somnambulism and suggestion are historically important because they reveal what observers believed they had witnessed and how those beliefs were argued over. They do not constitute a blinded clinical trial of modern hypnosis; nor can a twentieth-century memory experiment retrospectively prove what every eighteenth-century observer experienced.

The richer question is documentary: whose wording entered the surviving account? Was an observation recorded before an explanatory theory was offered? Were failures and dissenting descriptions preserved beside successes? Were later editors copying a testimony, paraphrasing it, or quietly supplying a new causal story?

A historical investigation fails when it treats every striking report as proof of the mechanism that contemporary believers assigned to it. It fails just as badly when it assumes that because a modern explanation is plausible, a historic witness must have been lying. The source permits some claims. ## Does hypnosis create false memories?

There is no responsible one-line answer of the form 'hypnosis always uncovers truth' or 'every hypnotic recollection is false.' Different procedures, people, questions, contexts and endpoints matter.

A 2021 systematic review by Muschalla and Schönborn included 59 laboratory articles plus two qualitative sources and examined imagination inflation, false feedback and memory-implantation methods. Its findings support the concern that suggestive instructions and some procedures can produce false beliefs or recollections in experimental settings. The evidence is heterogeneous; a reported percentage from selected experiments is not a clinical diagnosis or a universal rate.

A 2024 scoping review on differences between true and false autobiographical memory reports included 22 articles and 24 studies. It found some repeatedly observed differences but highlighted major conceptual limitations. This is a helpful counterweight to an attractive shortcut: **vividness, confidence and detail are not a truth machine.** Nor does lack of certainty prove a recollection false.

Sources: [Muschalla & Schönborn (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1002/cpp.2567); [2024 scoping review](https://doi.org/10.1027/1016-9040/a000513).

Notice the narrow chain of reasoning. A study on implanted memories can justify caution about suggestive questioning. It cannot establish that any particular person has a false memory, nor does it independently verify the promised effects of a downloadable hypnosis MP3. Historical evidence of operators giving commands is not a safety or efficacy evaluation of a commercial audio library.

For the same reason, Final Vestige should never promote 'recover your hidden memories' as a product benefit or present emotionally charged guided-memory exercises as a casual demonstration. The risks are different from observing how you describe a neutral everyday task.

## A twelve-minute source-check exercise

The most useful experiment for a reader is not to plant an upsetting false memory. It is to practice keeping an observation separate from an explanation.

Choose an ordinary object on a desk, an unlabeled photograph you own, or a harmless thirty-second silent video that you have permission to review. Avoid distressing footage or asking someone else to report a traumatic event.

For the first two minutes, write a neutral inventory: visible objects, actions and sequence, without causes or motives. For the next three, write what you *think* happened and label it explicitly as inference. Then write down a suggestive description of the same scene — perhaps 'the hurried person dropped the cup' — and underline every word that introduces a fact not established by the original observation.

Spend two minutes returning to the source. What can you actually check? Keep the original notes unedited. For the remaining five, assemble two columns: 'observed in original' and 'introduced by description or inference.' If you are unsure, write 'uncertain' rather than filling the gap.

This is an editorial exercise, not a validated cognitive training protocol. It does not test your suggestibility or prove improvement in memory. Its value is the habit of preserving separate layers of evidence.

## What this means for meditation-audio offers

The official Inspire3 catalog markets **Zen12** as a time-efficient meditation-audio program and **Hypnosis Live** as a library of hypnosis audio. Its Zen12 sales page describes twelve levels, four listening styles per level and 48 MP3 files in the Premium package, and advertises a year-long refund guarantee. The page also claims a twelve-minute session confers the benefit of a much longer meditation experience.

The package description is a seller-verifiable statement about what the merchant says is included. The equivalence, cognitive and health effects are efficacy claims that require product-specific substantiation. The Loftus–Palmer experiment, a review of false memory, and studies of meditation in general do not supply that substantiation.

This distinction makes for a better buying decision. An interested reader can ask whether an organized downloadable listening library has value *as a format*, whether they prefer guided instruction or ambient sound, whether a free routine already serves their practical need, what access and refund terms really apply, and what the merchant can show about its exact product.

## The interviewer's second camera

A camera records the light in front of it; it doesn't ask the subject whether a collision was violent. A human interview adds another layer: question wording, expectations, pauses and the choices a person makes while answering. Those layers are evidence too.

The glass was absent from the filmed crash. The question about the glass was real. So were the answers. What a rigorous archive must not do is collapse those three statements into one invented fact.

A truthful account keeps the footage, the question and the later testimony separate. That is the discipline that matters, whether the subject is a traffic accident, an eighteenth-century mesmerist, an eyewitness memory or a twenty-first-century sales page.
