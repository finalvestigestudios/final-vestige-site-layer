# The Hand That Wasn't Yours
## Why a fake limb can feel real—and why the evidence still has boundaries

There is a peculiar confidence to the word *mine*. Your hand is yours because you have always seen it attached to your arm, because you can move it, because the sensations arriving from its fingers seem to come from exactly where your fingers are. It does not feel like a conclusion you have reached. It feels like a fact too basic to require an argument.

Now imagine sitting at a table with one hand hidden behind a screen. In roughly the place where you would expect to see it, someone sets a realistic rubber hand. An experimenter brushes the visible substitute and your concealed real hand at matching times. Your eyes see one hand touched. Your skin feels another. In a familiar laboratory arrangement, some participants begin reporting an unsettling sensation: the hand they are watching begins to feel, in some respect, like their own.

It is tempting to introduce this as a magic trick about how easily the brain can be fooled. That makes the experiment a little too easy to dismiss. A better question is **what counts as evidence that a body part belongs to you, and what happens when the evidence conflicts?**

The original scientific report was a single-page correspondence in *Nature*, published on 19 February 1998 by Matthew Botvinick and Jonathan Cohen. Their abstract describes the referral of touch sensations to an artificial limb and an interaction between vision, touch and proprioception—the body's information about where its parts are positioned. The accessible journal record includes figures for questionnaire responses and a reaching experiment. The accessible abstract, by itself, is not a license to invent precise sample counts, rates of illusion, or universal experiences. [1]

The story matters to Final Vestige because it is not merely a story about sensation. It is a story about **a witness comparing incompatible records**. Your eyes, skin and sense of limb position may supply different information, and the resulting experience can be persuasive without any single stream having sole authority. That is the same editorial discipline we bring to archives: one testimony is not the entire event.

## Three witnesses at the table

Imagine trying to establish where your right hand is without looking at it. You can often make a useful estimate. You have a sense of where your fingers, wrist and arm are, even when no mirror shows them to you. That is not supernatural awareness; it is a family of sensory signals usually grouped under *proprioception*.

Now let your eyes take a turn. Vision says a hand-shaped object is on the table. Touch says your hidden fingers are being stroked. The crucial correspondence is *timing*: does the seen brushstroke coincide with the felt one? When different signals appear to describe one coherent event, the perceptual system has a reason to integrate them. But it must still contend with what your unseen hand's position suggests.

A rubber hand illusion setup changes the evidential balance. Researchers compare arrangements in which the seen and felt contacts occur together with control arrangements in which the contacts differ in timing, or where the rubber hand's position or appearance weakens the plausible match. These are not party tricks: comparison conditions help separate competing explanations of what a participant says they felt.

The original observation was neither that people literally sprout rubber fingers nor that all observers are deceived. It was a shift in reported bodily ownership under an engineered sensory conflict. The difference matters because a strong headline—*the brain accepted a fake limb*—can erase the variables that made the phenomenon interesting.

It also hides a methodological difficulty. How do we know someone experienced a change of ownership, rather than simply guessing what the researcher hoped they would say? A surprising subjective report should start an investigation, not end one.

## What moved: a feeling, a pointing response, or both?

Two outcomes often discussed together are not identical. A participant can report that the fake hand feels like theirs. Separately, an experimenter can ask them to indicate where the hidden real hand is located. If their indicated position shifts toward the artificial hand, researchers call that *proprioceptive drift*.

The two measures sound as though they must be different readouts of one hidden event. But science needs to earn that assumption. A changed estimate of finger position might result from combining visual and positional cues even when the observer does not feel genuine ownership of the rubber limb.

In 2011, Marieke Rohde, Massimiliano Di Luca and Marc O. Ernst put that distinction under pressure. They repeatedly measured the perceived location of a hidden hand while varying synchronous brushing, asynchronous brushing and simple vision of the fake hand. Their full open-access *PLOS ONE* paper reports that with frequent measurement, drift appeared not only during synchronized strokes but also in control arrangements. In the relevant comparisons, drift during synchronous stroking was not larger than drift produced by looking at the hand alone. Yet questionnaire reports of ownership followed a different pattern: synchronous stroking mattered for the subjective feeling. [2]

The authors concluded that proprioceptive drift and ownership can dissociate. This does not abolish the rubber hand illusion. It warns against using an indirect location judgment as if it were a one-to-one ownership meter. A study reporting drift may have measured something important while leaving open the stronger conclusion its readers want to draw.

Think of a court hearing. A witness points to where they believe something happened. Another witness says an event felt as if it happened to them. Both accounts can be sincere, and both deserve scrutiny. They answer different questions. A reliable archive records the distinction rather than smoothing it into one dramatic sentence.

## Where the brain enters the story

The illusion acquired a second kind of evidence in 2004. H. Henrik Ehrsson, Charles Spence and Richard E. Passingham studied brain activity during a rubber hand illusion procedure using functional magnetic resonance imaging. Their *Science* report, published in August 2004, found that activity in premotor cortex reflected participants' reported feeling of ownership. They proposed that multisensory integration in this region contributes to bodily self-attribution. [3]

This is more specific than saying scientists located “the self” or found a single neuron that decides what belongs to us. Brain imaging measures patterns correlated with a task and a reported state; an associated region is not a complete theory of why the subjective experience arises. Nor can a neural correlate on its own certify that a questionnaire answer is immune to experimental expectations.

Still, the combination of behavioral and brain-imaging work makes the rubber hand more than a curious anecdote. It asks a tractable question about how the nervous system evaluates visual, tactile and positional information. The question can be tested in different ways, with different evidence, and the results need not all point in exactly the same direction.

Modern theoretical work often describes this as *causal inference*: given noisy and partly conflicting signals, does the observer infer that sight and touch originated from the same event? One study published in 2022 manipulated both the timing of visual and tactile signals and the amount of visual noise. The authors found patterns of reported ownership that favored a causal-inference model over the alternative they tested. That is evidence about one computational account in a specified experiment—not proof that everyone experiences the illusion, or that a seller can reliably induce it at will. [4]

Notice how the scientific story becomes stronger when it is less theatrical. Instead of one claim that “the brain is easily hacked,” we have precise propositions: timing can matter, visual uncertainty can matter, and feeling an object belongs to you is not reducible to pointing slightly closer to it.

## The uncomfortable question: did the volunteer expect it?

There is a more difficult interpretation worth taking seriously. If a participant knows the study is about a rubber hand seeming to become their own, how much might expectation influence what they report? Even a carefully designed control can carry different expectations if one condition looks more convincing.

Peter Lush addressed this problem in a 2020 *Collabra: Psychology* paper. Participants read a description of the illusion and saw a demonstration, then predicted how they would respond to standard illusion and control statements in synchronous and asynchronous conditions. Those expectancy patterns differed in ways similar to published ownership ratings. Lush argued that the customary control methods can be confounded by demand characteristics—the cues that tell people what a study seems to expect. [5]

That does not establish that every ownership report was invented, that the original experiments have been fully explained away, or that perception never changes. It is a methodological challenge about what a particular measure can isolate. The 2020 study assessed expectations in a quasi-experimental design rather than directly replacing every earlier physiological and behavioral result. This is exactly where good science resists the temptation to declare victory for either side.

Why include such a criticism in an article about an extraordinary illusion? Because omitting it would give the reader only half the evidentiary record. If a study depends partly on what a participant says they experienced, the participant's interpretation of the researcher's intention is a plausible competing factor. Strong experiments must confront that possibility, not simply ignore it.

There is also a difference between *suggestion affecting a report* and *suggestion producing every part of an experience*. A useful test would try to separate these possibilities with better-blinded procedures, carefully constructed controls, multiple measures and predeclared predictions. The research conversation continues precisely because those distinctions are hard.

## A historical echo that is not a scientific equivalence

Final Vestige's archival work on Armand Marie Jacques de Chastenet, Marquis de Puységur, addresses a much earlier world of mesmerism and reported states of altered awareness. The surviving historical claims include accounts of unusual responsiveness and suggestion. They deserve a fair reading as documents about how people interpreted experiences in their period. They do **not** provide modern controlled evidence that “animal magnetism” is a treatment or that historical reports explain a modern visual-tactile experiment. [6]

What links the two topics is a question, not a proof: **how should investigators evaluate an experience that is vivid to the person reporting it?** The historian asks who wrote the account, under what conditions, for what audience, and with which independent corroboration. The experimental psychologist asks what cues were delivered, what controls were present, how responses were measured and which competing explanations remain.

That bridge is worth building because commercial mind products often blur the line. A dramatic illusion proves something narrow about perception in a given setting. A historical narrative proves that an account was recorded, not that the phenomenon happened exactly as described. Neither demonstrates the efficacy of a particular hypnosis recording, meditation track, or auditory program.

It may be tempting to regard the rubber hand as a demonstration that thought can be reprogrammed. That is not the experimental question and not what the cited papers establish. It is a metaphor dressed up as an outcome.

## A free observation you can try without pretending to replicate the experiment

**A record-of-evidence exercise, not a rubber hand induction.** No one touches you, no deception is needed, and you do not need to buy or download anything.

Take a sheet of paper and make three headings: *what I see*, *what I feel*, and *what I infer*. First, place your hands on a table and look at them. In the first column write only observable features: where the fingers are, whether they move, whether they contact the surface. In the second, write the sensations you can describe without interpretation: pressure on the palm, temperature, the feeling of a wrist resting on wood. In the third, write conclusions such as “this is my right hand” or “my index finger points toward the page.”

Then close your eyes briefly, if comfortable, and repeat the second and third columns using only ordinary stationary hand sensations. Reopen your eyes. Note what visual information adds. This does not induce the laboratory illusion, quantify accuracy, or measure suggestibility. There is no diagnosis or score.

The point is to practice separating sensory observations from the inferences they support. That distinction is useful when reading research and when investigating historical events. You can make a vivid story from a single observation; you can make a better record by admitting what was not observed.

Avoid touching another person's body or trying to stage synchronized brushing without consent. The original experiment's effects should not be promised, and a home imitation cannot substitute for a controlled protocol.

## The lesson of a hand that wasn't yours

The rubber hand illusion has an enduring hook because ownership seems beyond dispute until conflicting signals begin to push at it. But the best part of the story is not that the mind can be fooled. It is that researchers devised ways to ask *which signals were involved*, *what was actually measured*, *which interpretations survive controls*, and *what remains uncertain*.

Botvinick and Cohen's original correspondence pointed to an interaction among sight, touch and position. Rohde and colleagues later showed why location drift and ownership should not be equated. Ehrsson and colleagues supplied brain-imaging evidence associated with reported ownership. More recent computational work proposed models of multisensory inference, while Lush challenged researchers to scrutinize participant expectations.

None of those records alone gives the final word. Together they show why an experience can be powerful and still demand rigorous interpretation.

A hand on the table may look like yours. A confident answer may sound like proof. The responsible investigator keeps the conditions, measurements and competing explanations visible.

That is the difference between demonstrating an illusion and selling a certainty.
