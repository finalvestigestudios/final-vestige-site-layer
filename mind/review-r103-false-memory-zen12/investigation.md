# The Word That Was Never Said
**Mind & Perception | Investigation | Draft, unpublished | 3 November 2026**

*Memory can tell the truth about the meaning of a moment while getting the facts of the moment wrong. That is both its remarkable gift and its danger.*

Imagine a quiet room, a researcher, and a list of ordinary words. Nobody is being hypnotized. There is no concealed projector, theatrical music, or magician waiting for applause. A participant hears a series of words connected by a common idea: pillow, dream, blanket, drowsy, bed. Then comes a memory test. Some people confidently supply the very word that organized the list in their minds, although no one ever said it: **sleep**.

That simple mistake is the opening to a complicated history. If a word can be remembered without having been presented, what does a confident account actually establish? If two observers remember different things about the same event, what evidence should we seek before deciding which one is right? And if someone sincerely believes an account of an experience, what may we fairly infer about the event that supposedly produced it?

These questions belong in Final Vestige's archive alongside chronicles, documents, witnesses and claims whose surviving records do not speak with one voice.

## The first missing word

In 1959, psychologist James Deese published a study on predicting intrusions into immediate recall. An **intrusion** is an item that appears in a person's answer despite not having been on the original list. Deese found that the associative relationships among presented words could help predict the likelihood of certain intrusions [1]. The experimental subject was not being asked to reconstruct a lost childhood or give testimony under oath. The assignment was small, controlled and immediate: remember words.

That matters. A laboratory word-list error gives researchers a tractable phenomenon with which to test explanations. It does not, without further evidence, quantify how often witnesses misremember crimes or how to evaluate an individual person's autobiographical account. It is a starting point, not a universal verdict on human credibility.

More than three decades later, Henry Roediger and Kathleen McDermott revisited the earlier method. Their 1995 paper *Creating False Memories: Remembering Words Not Presented in Lists* helped establish a widely studied experimental approach, often called the Deese–Roediger–McDermott or **DRM** paradigm [2]. Participants studied sets of associated words. The missing theme word—often called the **critical lure**—frequently entered their recollections.

In the first experiment, critical lures were recalled approximately **40%** of the time under the reported procedure; in the second, the reported figure rose to **55%**. The authors also investigated later recognition judgments, including confident endorsements of never-presented words. These are results from particular laboratory procedures, **not estimates of the prevalence of false memories in everyday life**.

The startling part is not that people failed to pay attention. Often, they were doing precisely what good memory does: organizing material by meaning. Words near one idea helped produce a coherent theme. Later, the mind could retrieve the theme while losing track of whether its defining word had been heard or merely inferred.

Researchers have proposed and compared more than one account of this effect. Associative activation offers one family of explanations: studied words help activate related concepts. A complementary family concerns the retention of meaning or “gist” relative to exact detail, plus the later task of determining a remembered idea's **source**. These are useful scientific frameworks, not proof that every false recollection shares a single mechanism.

## How to try the idea without misleading yourself

Here is a modest, original exercise, not a diagnostic test.

Ask a friend to read the following list aloud **once**, slowly, without showing it to you: *window, door, hallway, room, corridor, staircase, entry, threshold, passage, wall*. Put your phone down. Wait thirty seconds, then write every word you remember. Only after you have finished should your friend reveal the original list.

You may insert a plausible but absent word, such as *house* or *building*; you may not. Either result is informative about one attempt, not about your intelligence or your susceptibility to hypnosis. The list above is original and **has not been validated to induce a DRM effect**. A nonoccurrence does not refute the published studies; an occurrence is not proof that a larger memory is false.

There is another lesson hidden inside the exercise: the friend should keep the source list unchanged. Without a record of what was actually presented, there is no independent way to separate a remembered item from a reasonable inference. Archival work depends on precisely this distinction.

## A question can change the next answer

Word lists are only one way to investigate constructive memory. In 1974, Elizabeth Loftus and John Palmer reported two experiments in which people watched traffic-accident films and answered questions phrased with different verbs [3]. Asking how fast cars were traveling when they “smashed” into each other produced higher speed estimates than phrasing with verbs such as “contacted” or “hit.” In a later test, participants exposed to the “smashed” wording were more likely to report seeing broken glass, though no glass appeared in the relevant film.

The result illustrates why the wording of a question matters. But it also requires a precise statement: the researchers changed question wording within an experiment; they did not establish that a single question permanently rewrites everyone's memory, or that any individual recollection can simply be dismissed as suggestion. The particular film, delays, questions and response measures define what the experiment tested.

One can learn something practical immediately. “What color was the coat?” assumes a coat existed. “What clothing, if any, did you notice?” leaves more room for an accurate answer. Neither wording alone guarantees accuracy; original contemporaneous records, independent accounts and uncertainty are still essential.

## The consequence for investigation

Final Vestige works with two kinds of missing information. Sometimes a document is gone: the record no longer survives. At other times, a record survives but the account was constructed by a person who had imperfect sensory access, memory and motives. These are not the same problem. A newly discovered document may change what we know. A careful interview may illuminate what someone recalls. Neither should automatically be treated as an unfiltered playback of an event.

A memorable account can be sincere without being precise. Conversely, a mistake in one detail does not prove that every other part of the account is wrong. Historical inquiry needs gradations: contemporaneous documentation, independently consistent accounts, original correspondence, later retellings, translations, and published interpretations. When the evidence cannot distinguish possibilities, the proper answer may remain *we do not know*.

It is tempting to use cognitive psychology as a universal debunking machine. That would repeat the same mistake in reverse. The presence of documented memory errors establishes that **errors are possible and experimentally demonstrable**; it does not establish that an unfamiliar historical claim is false. The empirical research is most useful when it changes our questions rather than supplying an instant verdict.

## A witness who may believe every word

The history of animal magnetism offers a particularly instructive bridge. Final Vestige's published **[Puységur's Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/)** examines accounts of extraordinary behavior and reports made in the language of eighteenth-century magnetism. The historical record can be investigated as testimony, practice and social context. Modern experiments on memory offer a reminder to distinguish **what someone reported experiencing** from **what caused it**.

The DRM and accident-question experiments did **not** test Puységur's subjects or explain the specific mechanism of animal magnetism. They cannot adjudicate a historical case across two centuries. But they do offer a sound methodological warning: the existence of a detailed, confident report does not itself verify the explanation attached to that report. Nor should an investigator assume fraud simply because memory and expectation may have mattered.

This is where the history of suggestion becomes more interesting than the usual theatrical portrayal. Suggestion need not involve a commanding voice or an altered state. Ordinary questions, associations and expectations can shape what a person reports. The scientific task is to study when, how much and under what conditions—not to insist that the same process accounts for every unusual claim.

## A correction worth making to the popular story

You may encounter the claim that “scientists proved memory is fake.” That is not what the published experiments show. Most participants can remember many presented items accurately while also occasionally producing specific kinds of errors. Reliable remembering and false remembering coexist. An experiment demonstrating one does not eliminate the other.

You may also hear that a 40% or 55% laboratory figure means 40% or 55% of childhood memories are invented. It does not. Those percentages are dependent on the particular word-list materials and immediate recall procedures in a 1995 study. They must not be transplanted into therapy, legal proceedings or family disputes.

Another seductive claim is that the brain deliberately creates fake memories “to protect us.” Such a sweeping motivational explanation is not established by the cited experiments. Understanding meaning and reconstructing details are useful cognitive operations; whether a specific error serves a protective function requires independent evidence.

What, then, is the strongest conclusion? **A subjective feeling of remembering is evidence of a present memory experience, not by itself sufficient evidence that every recalled detail was originally encountered.** This is narrower than declaring memory worthless—and more useful.

## A free field practice: preserve the first account

The next time you investigate an unusual story—an old photograph, a family anecdote, a historical mystery—try a source-preservation exercise.

Write down the first account as close to the witness's words as possible. Record its date and how the witness learned about the event. Separate direct observation from things repeated by others. If you ask a follow-up, use an open-ended question before a leading one. Preserve the earlier version even when a later version becomes more detailed. Make a second column marked **inference**: everything that seems likely but lacks direct support.

This does not solve the puzzle; it stops the puzzle from changing silently while you solve it.

The same habit can be applied to your own attention. The existing **[Seven Day Focus Fieldbook](https://finalvestige.com/focus/)** is an optional, free practice resource for readers who want to document observations and routines. It is not proof of a treatment effect, and this investigation makes no product recommendation.

## What remains in question

The enduring research questions concern the conditions under which associations, expectations and source judgments generate errors; what helps people recognize their uncertainties; and how laboratory findings translate, with appropriate safeguards, to complex real-world accounts. The existing studies do not establish a complete theory of autobiography, testimony or hypnotic recall.

A missing word on a researcher's list seems like a small thing. But it carries a powerful warning for anyone who loves mysteries: **the mind can give us a compelling answer before we have established what was actually there**.

The right response isn't to stop trusting witnesses. It is to become better at preserving and testing what they say.
