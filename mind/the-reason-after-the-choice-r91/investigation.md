# The Reason Came After the Choice

*How a switched photograph, a spoonful of jam, and a changed survey answer exposed the limits of what people can say about their own decisions.*

At the table there are two photographs. You study both faces. One seems more appealing. You point. The researcher picks up your selection, turns the card toward you, and asks what made it the better choice. You answer without much hesitation: perhaps the eyes, perhaps the smile, perhaps something you cannot quite put into words.

There is only one problem. **The photograph in your hand is not the one you chose.**

This scene captures the experimental manipulation behind a 2005 study by Petter Johansson, Lars Hall, Sverker Sikström and Andreas Olsson, published in *Science*. Participants compared pairs of faces for attractiveness; through a card-switching procedure, the researchers sometimes supplied the unchosen photograph as if it were the selected one. Some participants failed to detect the mismatch and nevertheless explained the outcome. The authors named the phenomenon **choice blindness**. [1]

The discovery was not that everyone always misses obvious substitutions. It was not that choices are fictitious. It was more exacting: **under particular experimental conditions, people can fail to notice that a presented outcome disagrees with their expressed preference, then give reasons for that outcome as though it had been their choice.** The discrepancy between the act and its explanation matters to any investigation that relies on testimony, including an investigation of the mind itself.

## The point at which a choice becomes a story

In ordinary life, deciding and explaining feel like one uninterrupted event. We choose the sandwich and say we wanted something light. We pick a route and say it will be faster. We describe our reasons to somebody else, and the fluency of the account makes it sound as if every motive was available before we acted.

But an explanation is a new event. It arrives *after* the decision, in a particular social setting, in response to a particular question. The explanation might describe the original judgment faithfully. It might be incomplete. It might also absorb information introduced since the judgment was made.

The 2005 experiment separated these events. The participant supplied a preference; the researchers controlled which photograph was then presented for explanation. Once the chosen and presented outcomes could diverge, the investigators had an observational test. Did the person catch the change? If not, what happened when the person was invited to narrate the supposed decision?

The notable result was not simply the missed substitution but the **introspective account** that followed it. Someone might explain the appeal of facial features belonging to the person they had just rejected. The verbal report is real speech produced by a real person. It is not reliable evidence that those features caused the earlier choice. That distinction—between a sincere answer and a correct causal history—is the investigation's center.

The original article is short, and its public abstract does not provide enough detail to turn every result into a universal percentage. We therefore avoid the viral shortcut that claims, without qualification, that a fixed fraction of all people are blind to their choices. Detection depends on stimuli, procedure, timing, and the way researchers ask the question. [1]

## A market stall, a jar, and the taste that changed

A laboratory card trick can invite a fair objection: perhaps people do not care much which unfamiliar photograph they chose. A face-rating task is not necessarily representative of familiar preferences.

Five years later, researchers moved the question into a supermarket. Lars Hall and colleagues invited shoppers to sample pairs of jams and teas, choose a favorite, sample the purported winner again, and explain the preference. In some trials, the researchers switched the contents. The participant was actually tasting or smelling the option they had not chosen. [3]

The differences were not always subtle. The published abstract identifies a contrast between cinnamon-apple and bitter grapefruit jams and between mango and Pernod-like tea aromas. Across the manipulated trials, the researchers reported detection in no more than about one-third; detection for especially dissimilar examples was still far from universal. That result is tied to **this supermarket manipulation**, not a claim that a third of people would detect any substituted meal, brand, or memory. [3]

The setting changed the stakes of interpretation. These shoppers had made an immediate sensory judgment using smell and taste. The experimenters did not merely ask them to remember an image; they gave them a second sensory encounter. Some participants still described a preference inconsistent with their previous selection.

The discomfort here is useful. We often assume that *feeling* familiar with a reason is enough to verify it. But the marketplace experiment reminds us that immediate sensory evidence and retrospective explanation can diverge—especially when the environment presents a changed outcome as the choice already made.

The studies did not reveal what every participant was privately thinking. Researchers observed selections, manipulations, detection, and reports. They could not directly read the internal sequence by which a person generated each reason.

## Could a changed answer change an attitude?

Choice blindness also entered a more consequential setting: political opinions. A 2013 *PLOS ONE* study by Hall, Strandberg, Pärnamets, Lind, Tärning and Johansson investigated what happens when survey responses are covertly altered before participants explain them. In that controlled research context, the manipulation could affect reported attitudes or intentions. [4]

The finding does **not** mean that a person can be reliably converted to any political position by changing a sheet of paper. It does not justify deceptive campaigning. The study is evidence that a manipulated record and a request for explanation can influence what people say and sometimes how they subsequently position themselves, under specified research conditions.

It also reveals why the ethical dimension matters. In a valid experiment, temporary deception requires oversight, safeguards, and explanation afterward. Outside such protections, secretly changing another person's choices is manipulation, not a harmless parlor trick. Final Vestige does not recommend trying the original deceptive procedure on an unsuspecting friend, customer, voter, or child.

## Introspection is useful, but it is not a recording device

A 2006 analysis by Johansson, Hall, Sikström and Olsson used linguistic methods to study introspective reports collected in choice-blindness experiments. The work joined a much older psychological argument about whether people have direct access to the processes that produce their judgments. [2]

There is a temptation to treat this line of research as an indictment of all first-person accounts. That would be too simple. A person's report can tell us what they experienced, what they remember, what they believe, and how they currently make sense of an event. Those are all legitimate forms of evidence. The difficulty begins when the investigator asks that same report to establish **an unseen causal mechanism**.

A witness who honestly reports an unusual sensation is not thereby proving the mechanism they or their practitioner attach to it. A consumer who sincerely says a recording improved concentration has reported an experience—not automatically established that the recording changed brain oscillations, altered memory, or outperformed an alternative. An explanation can be meaningful without being a controlled test.

The solution is not cynicism. It is better method: preserve the original decision before showing the outcome, document the exact wording of questions, separate observation from inference, and look for independent verification where the causal claim matters.

## The historical bridge: Puységur's witnesses

Final Vestige's [Puységur Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) reconstruct an eighteenth-century world of magnetic somnambulism, practitioner instructions, case testimony, and extraordinary explanations. The archive tracks recorded sources rather than simply repeating the claims made in them.

Choice blindness was not studied in Puységur's century, and modern researchers did not experimentally test his historical subjects. This is **a methodological comparison**, not a retroactive diagnosis.

The parallel lies in an evidentiary problem: when an observer asks a participant to explain an experience within an already supplied framework, the answer may be shaped by the presentation, the questions, and the expectations surrounding the event. That possibility does not license dismissing a historical subject as dishonest. It means the investigator must distinguish the report of an experience from proof of the agent said to cause it.

For an earlier methodological turning point, read Final Vestige's investigation of [Franklin's 1784 animal-magnetism commission](https://finalvestige.com/2026/11/02/mesmerism/). The commission's tests were not experiments in choice blindness; they were tests of a proposed magnetic mechanism. The connecting idea is that an outcome described in good faith should still be checked against a design capable of distinguishing rival explanations.

## A safe experiment that does not trick anybody

You can learn something about the space between a preference and a reason without secretly altering another person's choices.

**Make a record before you explain.** Take two pieces of art, two short pieces of music, or two photographs you own the rights to display. Quietly choose which you prefer. Write the choice down and date the note. Without looking at it, jot down the first two reasons that come to mind, then look at the original selection. Repeat on a different day.

This is an observation of your own changing descriptions, **not** a reproduction of the laboratory effect. Because nobody swaps your selection, it cannot measure your susceptibility to choice blindness. The exercise instead makes a useful distinction visible: a decision is one fact, and its explanation is another. Both are worth preserving.

A second, simpler version is to say, 'I prefer A, although I may not know every reason why.' That sentence is less dramatic than claiming perfect self-knowledge, but it is unusually precise.

## What the experiments prove—and what they do not

The studied effects are robust enough to have been investigated across different tasks, but they remain bounded by the evidence:

- **Supported:** Manipulating the presented outcome can lead some participants to miss discrepancies between what they chose and what they are asked to explain. [1, 3]
- **Supported within studied settings:** Participants sometimes provide plausible explanations for mismatched outcomes. [1, 2]
- **Supported within a specific political-survey paradigm:** Altered response records can affect subsequent reports. [4]
- **Not established by these studies:** That everyone is easily persuaded, that every reason is invented, or that a commercial audio product can rewrite preferences or mental states.
- **Not measured here:** Any therapeutic, wealth, intelligence, meditation, or hypnosis outcome associated with a consumer product.

These boundaries are not anticlimactic. They are the discovery. A careful experiment can show that a confident explanation sometimes follows the wrong outcome, without declaring that confidence is always meaningless.

## The question to carry out of the room

Imagine the researcher placing the photograph before you again. You have a reason ready. You can name the feature, explain the expression, construct a fluent little history of the decision.

Then the original record appears.

You chose the other face.

The most important part of the experiment is not that the participant has been fooled. It is that a record exists against which the story can be tested. That is what makes choice blindness research rather than mere skepticism—and what makes evidence worth preserving, especially when the mind itself is the witness.
