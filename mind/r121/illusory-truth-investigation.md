# The Lie That Arrives Already Familiar

*The oldest trick in a noisy information environment is not always a perfect deception. Sometimes it is an ordinary sentence said one time too many.*

Consider an unfamiliar claim about how the mind works. The first time you encounter it, it sounds doubtful. The second time, it has acquired a recognizable shape. The third time, you catch yourself thinking that you have heard something about it somewhere before. The source may have disappeared from memory, yet the sentence remains. Is it more believable now? That question has a surprisingly long experimental history.

The distinction matters because a statement can become familiar without becoming better supported. A claim repeated across a dozen accounts might come from one promotional page, one poorly interpreted scientific paper, or one anonymous post that everybody else copied. Twelve appearances are not twelve witnesses. And the brain, which evolved to navigate an environment where familiarity often is useful, does not automatically keep a ledger of independent sources.

Psychologists call the resulting shift in judgment the **illusory truth effect**: repeated statements tend to be rated as more likely true than comparable statements encountered for the first time. It is not a claim that repetition hypnotizes everyone or forces belief. It is an observed tendency in judgments, constrained by content, context, knowledge and task. The effect is subtle enough to overlook in a single encounter and consequential enough to deserve careful attention in an era of frictionless copying. [1–5]

## An experiment conducted before the feed existed

In 1977, Lynn Hasher, David Goldstein and Thomas Toppino asked people to evaluate plausible statements spanning ordinary areas of knowledge. Across three sessions separated by two-week intervals, some statements returned while others appeared only once. The researchers asked for truth judgments, not advertising preferences or political allegiance.

The findings were disquieting: both true and false repeated statements received increasing validity judgments, while new statements did not show the same pattern. Nothing about a repeated statement had changed. No fresh witness had entered the room; no new document had been discovered. What changed was the person's prior encounter with the sentence. [1]

That was an important demonstration, not a universal law. The task involved plausible trivia statements under laboratory conditions. It did not measure actual purchases, votes, medical choices, or a person's enduring beliefs. Nor did it establish that every repeated claim will gain ground. Yet the result exposes a weakness in one of our most natural ways of making sense of information: we often use the feeling of knowing as partial evidence that something is known.

Imagine a researcher replacing the numbers on a file folder every time it is copied, then putting the same document in front of you again and again. Nothing inside the folder is independently corroborated. But the label grows familiar. The experiment did not use such folders; the image is a way of understanding why repetition without corroboration matters.

## The ease of reading can masquerade as evidence

One widely studied explanation concerns **processing fluency**. Material that has been encountered before can feel easier to process. That ease can be mistakenly interpreted as a sign of validity. The explanation is not that a reader consciously decides, "I have seen this twice, therefore it is true." The cue may be experienced as a general sense that the statement makes sense or rings a bell.

Dechêne, Stahl, Hansen and Wänke reviewed 51 studies in a 2010 meta-analysis. Across those experiments, repetition and subjective truth were related, although the size and form of the effect depended on how researchers measured it. Comparisons between repeated and new statements were not interchangeable with changes in ratings of the same statement over time. The authors discussed fluency as a major framework, not as a license to assume one identical process explains every result. [2]

That distinction is the difference between a phenomenon and a slogan. "Repeated ideas feel true" is a useful headline. It cannot tell us the magnitude of the effect for a particular person, claim or setting. It cannot tell us whether someone's expressed truth rating becomes an actual decision. A careful account starts with that uncertainty rather than concealing it.

## Knowing the right answer is not a magic shield

A reader may reasonably object: surely this works mainly when people do not know the underlying facts. Lisa Fazio, Nadia Brashier, B. Keith Payne and Elizabeth Marsh tested an important version of that assumption in 2015. Participants saw statements that were true or false, including some that contradicted commonly known facts; later they evaluated their truth.

Repeated false statements gained a relative advantage in truth ratings even when participants could supply the correct underlying answer in a separate knowledge task. That did **not** mean participants completely forgot facts or consistently accepted the falsehoods as true. Knowledge continued to matter. The striking result was that familiarity could affect truth judgments despite available knowledge. [3]

The finding suggests a narrower, more useful lesson than "facts do not matter." Facts matter, but readers do not consult every fact they possess on every encounter. If something can be evaluated quickly using the shallow cue of familiarity, sometimes that is what happens. The difference between *having* a correction and *using* it is a central part of the story.

It also creates a danger for responsible publishers. A story that begins by repeating a sensational false claim in large type and only corrects it deep in the article may give that claim another exposure. This does not prove all corrections backfire; sweeping claims about correction effects are themselves risky. It suggests a better editorial habit: make the verified fact visible first and attach the evidence immediately.

## From the laboratory to the social feed

In 2018, Gordon Pennycook, Tyrone Cannon and David Rand tested the effect with fabricated news headlines presented in formats resembling social-media posts. Prior exposure increased subsequent perceived accuracy, including after a delay of a week in one experiment. The work also found a boundary condition: repetition did not make extremely implausible claims more credible under the tested conditions. In other words, the effect was not limitless. [4]

There is a commercial implication that deserves restraint. Repetition can make a message familiar; that does **not** demonstrate that it will make a reader buy an audio course, trust a creator, or change a habit. A platform can show the same headline thousands of times without producing a thousand independent validations. Reach, endorsement and evidence are three different things.

This is particularly relevant to modern self-improvement products. A claim about a special frequency, a hidden historical method or a guaranteed neurological result might travel from an original sales page into reactions, reposts and affiliate promotions. Eventually the pitch appears to have a long public history. The appearance of consensus can be partly a record of distribution. A careful review asks where the claim began, whether anyone tested the *specific product*, and what alternative explanations remain.

The connection is methodological: the question of how people came to believe a claim is different from whether the claim was true.

## There is a countermeasure, but it isn't a talisman

In four experiments reported by Brashier, Eliseev and Marsh, a prompt to focus on accuracy during the first encounter prevented later illusory truth under specific conditions—importantly, when participants held relevant knowledge. It is a finding about the timing of evaluation, not a promise that any five-second trick makes a reader manipulation-proof. [5]

There is a practical lesson here: ask the question of evidence **before** a statement becomes comfortable. What kind of claim is this? What observation would support it? Is the source the one that performed the study, the one selling the item, or someone repeating the seller's phrasing? If a claim is about health, wealth or a proprietary audio method, was that product tested under suitable controls, and were the results independently reported?

The questions are deliberately less glamorous than a universal "bias hack." They ask you to recover the difference between a document, a repetition and a result.

### A private, five-minute source audit

Choose one non-sensitive claim you have seen more than once this week. Do **not** share it publicly for this exercise; repeating a false claim would be a strange way to counter its spread. On paper or in a local note, record where you first remember encountering it, where you saw it again, and whether those appearances lead back to the same original source. Then look for one primary document or independently conducted study that actually bears on the claim.

Put the claim into three columns: **what is stated**, **what is observed**, and **what is not established**. If the original source is a merchant, separate the features it describes from the outcomes it promises. If it is a research article, note who or what was tested and the size of the question the experiment answered. If you cannot identify an independent source, write "not verified," not "false." Missing proof and disproof are different findings.

This is an informal source-checking exercise. It is **not a validated psychological intervention**, not a diagnostic test, and not a measurement of whether you personally exhibit the illusory truth effect.

## The hardest part of attention is deciding what deserves it

Some perception experiments expose blind spots in what we *see*. Illusory-truth experiments expose a blind spot in what we think we have reason to *believe*. Both can be overstated: neither makes the mind uniquely foolish, and neither erases the many ordinary ways perception and memory succeed.

The investigative habit is simple, if not easy. A repeated statement is still one claim until independent evidence arrives. A polished presentation is still a presentation. A persuasive testimonial is still a person's report, not a controlled trial of what would have happened otherwise.

The next time a headline feels familiar, perhaps ask the least fashionable question on the internet: *familiar from where?*
