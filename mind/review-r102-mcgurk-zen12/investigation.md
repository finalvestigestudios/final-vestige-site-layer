# The Voice That Was Never Spoken
## Why your eyes can change what your ears seem to hear

*Final Vestige Studios | Mind & Perception Archive | Original investigation | Editorial draft, not published | 3 November 2026*

**The sound has not changed. The mouth has. And suddenly, the word you thought you heard may change with it.** That is the unsettling proposition behind an experiment published fifty years ago—not a proof that sound is imaginary, but evidence that ordinary speech perception involves more than hearing.

In December 1976, Harry McGurk and John MacDonald published a short report in *Nature*. Its title, *Hearing lips and seeing voices*, was almost a riddle. The researchers paired a recording of one spoken syllable with video of a face articulating another. In a widely cited condition, listeners heard the sound /ba/ while watching a mouth shape /ga/. Some reported a third sound: /da/. Neither the original audio track nor the silent mouth movement literally contained that combined answer. [1]

There was no stage hypnotist, no elaborate deception, and no paranormal explanation. There was an ordinary task: identify a syllable. The departure from an ordinary result came from the mismatch between two channels of information. The researchers' finding became known as the **McGurk effect**.

What makes the effect worth investigating is not merely that it can fool us. It is that the phrase “I heard it” usually seems like a direct report from the outside world. In this experiment, that sentence can also describe the result of an interpretation assembled inside the listener.

### The discovery was not a magic trick

The researchers' original design matters. They did not simply tell participants to expect a new word and watch what happened. They changed the relationship between the soundtrack and the visible movement of a speaker's mouth. When sight and sound corresponded, recognition worked differently from when they conflicted. Some of the mismatch combinations produced reported fusion percepts; other combinations produced combinations of sounds rather than a simple third syllable. [1]

The distinction is easy to lose in a viral edit. An online video may promise that every viewer will hear /da/ and treat anything else as failure, an attention deficit, or evidence that the viewer's brain is uniquely wired. The original observation supports none of those broad claims. It shows that a particular audiovisual arrangement can alter reported perception. How often that happens depends on the people, stimuli and procedure.

The visual information is not peripheral decoration. Watching speech can supply clues about how sounds are articulated. A closed pair of lips and an open mouth are different signals, and listeners make use of those signals when speech is difficult to hear. Yet the strongest viral version—sound A plus lips B always equals hearing C—mistakes a useful experimental demonstration for a universal rule.

### Forty years later, the co-author revisited the story

John MacDonald returned to the experiment in a 2017 retrospective. He described the discovery's origins, the construction of the test materials, and the path by which an unexpected result became an enduring research tool. [2] His historical account is an unusually valuable kind of source: someone who helped construct the original investigation describing how it actually came about, with the perspective of decades of subsequent research.

That retrospective is a reminder that scientific findings have histories. The neatly packaged classroom phenomenon was once a concrete problem involving film, sound, experimental choices and human responses. Its later name can make the effect seem more unified, settled and inevitable than the original design warranted.

There is also a caution about how to tell this story. It would be wrong to present the early experiment as a demonstration that everyone hears illusions every time they watch a speaker. That interpretation is tempting because it makes a stronger headline. It is also testable—and later work has made the simple version untenable.

### An effect can be genuine and still vary dramatically

In a 2015 investigation, researchers measured responses to different mismatched audiovisual syllables across 360 people and twenty stimulus examples. They reported extreme variability, including people who almost never reported a fused response and others who did so very often. The identity of the talker, the particular stimulus, and the format of the response mattered. Changing a question from an open answer to forced choice changed the frequency of fusion responses, even when the stimuli were the same. [3]

Why does the response format matter? Imagine hearing something between two familiar syllables. When asked “What did you hear?” you might describe it in your own words. Given four buttons, you must select whichever answer seems closest. The recorded outcome now reflects not only the percept, but also the response demanded by the experiment. That doesn't make the illusion fictitious. It limits how confidently a score on one task can be translated into the statement that someone has a fixed degree of “multisensory ability.”

In 2018, Brown and colleagues reported that part of the individual variation was associated with the ability to extract a particular articulatory cue from lip movements. The study did not find relationships with several other broad cognitive measures it tested, including working memory and attentional control. The correlation with lipreading-related skill explained only a small part of the variation. [4]

This matters for Final Vestige's mission because the strongest historical investigations ask not only whether a witness reports an experience, but **how the observation was obtained**. A fixed set of buttons, an instruction, a demonstration shown once rather than repeatedly—each can change what gets recorded. The observation remains evidence. Its meaning depends on the procedure.

### The dispute about what the illusion measures

Another layer of caution emerged as researchers compared the McGurk task with understanding real conversations.

The classic experimental arrangement uses incompatible audio and visual speech—exactly the sort of mismatch that ordinary conversation usually avoids. An everyday discussion involves words, context, sentence meaning, shifting attention and reasonably synchronized sight and sound. Researchers Van Engen, Dey, Sommers and Peelle argued in 2022 that a listener's response to a McGurk syllable should not simply stand in for their ability to integrate audiovisual speech in real life. They advocated studying more natural spoken language. [5]

That is not an announcement that the 1976 result vanished. It is a disagreement about the reach of its interpretation. An effect that exposes a mechanism under controlled conditions is not automatically a clinical test, a personality measure or a universal model of communication.

A separate methodological warning comes from Magnotti and Beauchamp's 2018 research into published group differences. They demonstrated how selective reporting of statistically significant results could inflate estimated differences in measures such as McGurk susceptibility. Their examples showed why a dramatic difference between groups in a published experiment may look smaller on replication. [6]

Put these studies together and a more interesting picture appears. The visual contribution to hearing is real enough to warrant decades of experiments. But the strength of a given demonstration is shaped by the sample, the clip, the instructions, and the response measure. What the brain is doing cannot be settled by a single viral clip.

### A no-purchase field exercise that does not fake a demonstration

The original effect requires deliberately mismatched synchronized speech audio and video. A still picture with captions cannot reproduce it. Nor can a silent face GIF by itself demonstrate that a sound has changed. Rather than staging a fake “hear what you read” exercise, here is a more honest experiment in observation.

Find a legitimate demonstration that preserves separate **sound-only**, **video-only**, and **combined mismatched** conditions, ideally from an academic source with its method stated. Use headphones only at a comfortable volume if needed; do not treat listening on one device as equivalent to a lab protocol. Before watching the combined clip, write down which syllable you hear with your eyes closed. Then play the combined version and record your first response without choosing from a provided menu. Finally, replay the audio-only version and compare the three answers.

The possible results are equally informative. You might hear a fused syllable. You might hear the original audio. You might hear a combination, or perceive something unstable. You might have difficulty distinguishing the sound clearly. **None of those outcomes diagnoses your attention, suggestibility, hearing, intelligence or mental health.**

If you can't find an appropriately documented source clip, skip the audiovisual demonstration and instead try the lower-stakes evidence exercise: choose two short accounts of the same historical event, note which observations each source actually reports, and underline later interpretations in a different color. This is not a substitute for a McGurk experiment. It practices the related habit of separating an observation from the story built around it.

### The bridge to eighteenth-century suggestion is methodological, not mystical

Readers of Final Vestige's [Puységur Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) have encountered an earlier world in which striking human reports demanded explanation. In Puységur's era, apparent trance states, conversation and suggestion could be interpreted within the theory of animal magnetism. Modern readers have other concepts, but their task is similar in one essential respect: distinguish the reported event from the mechanism proposed to explain it.

The McGurk effect does **not** verify animal magnetism. It does not prove that spoken suggestions have hidden powers, that hypnosis rewrites the brain, or that audio recordings can produce an hour's meditation in a few minutes. And Puységur's historical accounts do not establish anything about a contemporary audio product. The connection is in how we investigate explanations, not in an assertion of shared causation.

A person can sincerely report an unusual experience. The investigator still has work to do. Was the stimulus controlled? Were alternative explanations tested? Were observers given choices? Can another group reproduce the result? How far beyond the original setting can the result reasonably travel?

That is the difference between treating perception as a source of curiosity and treating it as a sales funnel.

### The evidence that stays when the trick ends

The most reliable lesson from the McGurk literature is not “trust nothing you hear.” That would be an overcorrection, and a dangerous one. Seeing and hearing together frequently support ordinary communication. One engineered conflict tells us something about how cues can interact; it does not invalidate conversation, testimony or all sensory evidence.

The more useful principle is modest and demanding: perception is an achievement, not simply a playback device. Under particular conditions, information from one sense can change a report about another. Under other conditions, people differ sharply. Both findings deserve a place in the record.

That insight travels from a laboratory film to historical inquiry without making either of them mystical. The question is not whether the listener was fooled. The question is what was presented, what was reported, and which explanation survives the controls.
