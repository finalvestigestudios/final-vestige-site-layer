# The Sound You Saw

**Final Vestige Studios · Mind & Perception · Historical/experimental investigation · Draft**

**Dek:** In 1976, a voice said one thing while a face said another. Some listeners heard a third syllable that had never been recorded. The experiment was small; the implication was enormous.

## The mouth had said something else

Consider a screening room with a film of a woman speaking. There is nothing especially unusual about the picture: a face, moving lips, the repeated articulation of a syllable. But someone has quietly changed the soundtrack. Where the lips form the movement for *ga*, the voice on the recording says *ba*. Ask a listener what was said, and the answer may be neither. The listener can report *da*.

That is the deceptively simple mismatch described by Harry McGurk and John MacDonald in their letter, “Hearing lips and seeing voices,” published in *Nature* on December 23, 1976. The researchers described dubbing a recording of /ba/ over a face articulating /ga/. Adults reported a fused /da/ percept under that condition. When hearing the soundtrack alone, or seeing untreated film, participants could report the corresponding syllables correctly. The investigators also described a reverse pairing that often produced combined responses such as /bagba/ or /gaba/ rather than the same simple fusion. [1]

This was not an experiment in hearing voices in silence. The sound existed. The visible articulation existed. The interesting result lay in the way a listener's perceptual system resolved their disagreement. The reported /da/ was not a physical /da/ secretly encoded on the soundtrack. It was an outcome of combining evidence arriving by two senses.

The distinction matters. The familiar headline “your ears lie” is a memorable way to sell an illusion, but it is a poor description of the science. Ears collect acoustic energy. Eyes register visual change. A report of speech is an interpretation of those signals, in a setting where mouths and voices normally belong to the same person. In the demonstration, the expectation that they belonged together did work without announcing itself.

## A short letter with a long afterlife

The original paper occupied only pages 746–748 of volume 264 of *Nature*. Its abstract begins with an ordinary circumstance: we often hear speech while seeing the speaker. Speech researchers had commonly treated speech perception principally as an auditory task. The dubbed film exposed a powerful qualification. Visual information can alter what a person *hears*, not merely what a person believes the speaker meant. [1]

In popular retellings the effect sometimes becomes a magic trick with one guaranteed answer. That is a different proposition from the experiment. The phenomenon is associated with particular phonetic stimuli, recording conditions, and individual listeners. The 1976 paper itself distinguished a fused response under one audio–visual pairing from combined responses under the reverse manipulation. A careful illustration must preserve those alternatives. If the demonstration produces no change for an individual viewer, that alone is not a diagnosis of deficient attention, poor hearing, or unusual intelligence.

The original abstract is publicly readable, while the complete article may have access restrictions. We have checked the original bibliographic record and abstract, not reanalyzed the underlying participant-level dataset. The word “fused” describes a kind of report elicited by the stimulus arrangement; it should not be expanded into a universal neurological mechanism.

A useful thought experiment follows. Play a clean recording of *ba*. Ask a person to identify it without any face. Now show a face clearly articulating a conflicting syllable while keeping the sound identical. What changed? The audio waveform, if carefully held constant, did not. The evidence presented to the observer did. What feels like a direct auditory measurement may include a visual contribution.

## Why it is not a universal trick

More than forty years later, Violet Brown and colleagues investigated why individuals differ so greatly in how frequently they experience McGurk-type fusion. Their 2018 *PLOS ONE* paper emphasized a methodological fact often absent from viral videos: group-level demonstrations can reproduce reliably while individual susceptibility varies widely. Across studies, reported fusion rates for individuals have ranged from zero to one hundred percent. [2]

Brown and coauthors examined perceptual and cognitive predictors and found that skill in extracting place-of-articulation information from lip movements explained a small part of the variation. They did not find the same predictive relationships for measures of attentional control, processing speed, working-memory capacity, or graded auditory categorization. [2] A single successful demonstration therefore does not award a certificate of superior “multisensory intelligence,” just as an unsuccessful one does not establish that someone is perceptually impaired.

It would also be wrong to tell readers that a stimulus which works in one laboratory must work equally well in a compressed, muted, badly synchronized social-media clip. Timing, audio intelligibility, visual clarity, stimulus choice, recording context, and the viewer's own perceptual tendencies all matter. A clip can introduce artifacts the original researchers did not intend. Our planned short film illustrates the experiment with typography and animation; it does **not** claim to produce the true McGurk percept without a separately made, controlled audiovisual stimulus.

Research continues past the classic classroom version. In 2023, Wataru Teramoto and Marc Ernst investigated whether visual speech information that was masked from awareness could influence later phonetic perception. Their experiments examined a *McGurk aftereffect*: changes in responses to a subsequent audio-only syllable following exposure to mismatched audiovisual speech. This is a distinct experimental question from the original finding, and the authors qualified their conclusions to the stimuli and task used. [3]

No single one of these studies gives us a master key to consciousness. They illuminate a specific place where perception combines imperfect evidence under experimentally controlled conditions.

## The suggestion problem

Final Vestige has another archive in which a person reports an experience and an observer is tempted to convert that report into a cause. The eighteenth-century physician and magistrate networks around Amand-Marie-Jacques de Chastenet, Marquis de Puységur, recorded altered-state speech, predictions, witness statements, procedures, uncertainty, and apparent failures. Our published **Puységur’s Animal Magnetism Files** tracks those sources across 153 image-locked records and sixteen dossiers. The archive insists on a vital distinction: the existence of a documented testimony is not itself proof of the magnetic mechanism offered to explain it. [4]

The analogy to McGurk is about the *logic of evidence*, not a claim that hypnotism and audiovisual speech illusion are the same process. The researchers in 1976 manipulated a visual signal and an audio signal and compared reports. A historical magnetic case might report a meaningful sensation, a changed state, a failed prediction, or a witnessed action without isolating the mechanism. In both situations, an editor must resist the impulse to smuggle an explanation into the sentence that describes an experience.

We can make the analogy sharper. “A listener reported hearing /da/” is an observation tied to an experimental condition. “The audio recording contained /da/” is a different claim. “A listener reported a sensation under magnetic treatment” is an archival observation. “Animal magnetism caused the sensation” is a different claim. Moving from one to the other requires evidence not furnished by the report alone.

That restraint makes the historical material more interesting, not less. The papers preserve what people saw, wrote, promised, contradicted, and later reconsidered. They also preserve ethical complications about consent and authority. If a modern account merely declares the witnesses gullible, it discards the very documentary texture the archive was built to examine.

## The commercial shortcut the research does not license

A contemporary audio seller may describe a recording as scientifically designed, based on frequencies, or capable of changing internal states. There is an obvious superficial resemblance: both an audiovisual illusion and a marketed audio program concern what listeners experience. But a McGurk experiment does not establish that prerecorded meditation music changes brainwaves, reduces illness, restores focus, or reproduces the benefits of long-term meditation. It does not test any commercial recording.

This is one of the reasons we maintain two editorial rooms. The **investigation** asks what a source and its design establish. A **separate independent purchasing guide** can ask which features a seller advertises, what research bears on the claimed mechanism, and what remains unknown. The first must never act as a disguised product endorsement for the second.

## A demonstration you can reason through without a video

The most useful free exercise here is not to force yourself to hallucinate a particular syllable. It is to separate the components of a claim.

Write down three statements: **the recorded sound**, **the visible mouth movement**, and **the reported syllable**. For the classic mismatched illustration, put /ba/ in the first, /ga/ in the second, and the often-reported /da/ in the third. Ask which statements are facts about the physical stimulus and which are facts about the observer's response. Now write the sentence “the listener was influenced by visual speech information.” Unlike the sensational claim “the ears were reprogrammed,” it can be related to the actual intervention and response.

If you watch an independently sourced demonstration, use comfortable volume, try audio-only first, and be aware you may not get the textbook percept. Do not repeat the clip while driving or performing safety-critical work. For people using captions, subtitles can themselves set expectations; a deliberately accessible educational treatment should explain the illusion without requiring the experience of it.

A listener who reports /ba/ may be accurately reporting their experience just as a listener who reports /da/ may be accurately reporting theirs. The experiment is not a contest with a correct magic password.

## What remains in the record

The original observation was not a secret sound and not a new substance flowing through a brain. It was the ordinary architecture of speech perception exposed by an engineered mismatch. A camera provided one stream of evidence. A speaker provided another. What the listener reported could be a third thing.

That is why a three-page report remains relevant fifty years later. It changes the question from “Did they really hear it?” to “What information was available when the hearing happened?” It also gives a remarkably practical instruction to anyone investigating claims about attention, memory, suggestion, or historical testimony: do not flatten a record of experience into proof of your preferred cause.
