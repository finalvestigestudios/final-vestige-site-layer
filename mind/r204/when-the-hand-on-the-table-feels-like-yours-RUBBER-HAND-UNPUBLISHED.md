# When the Hand on the Table Feels Like Yours

*The rubber-hand illusion, the experimental problem of owning a body, and the trouble with treating expectation as proof.*

Imagine a table with two hands on it. One is your own, hidden behind a screen. The other is made of rubber, placed where you can see it. The experimenter draws a brush across the rubber fingers while another brush touches your concealed hand. The strokes arrive together. After a while, some people report an odd sensation: the touch seems to be happening where the rubber fingers sit. Some even describe the artificial hand as belonging, however briefly, to them.

That is a perceptual report, not a supernatural transfer. No hand changes owners. Yet the question behind the report is profound: how does the brain decide which objects count as *parts of me*?

The classic experiment was published on February 19, 1998, as a one-page scientific correspondence by Matthew Botvinick and Jonathan Cohen in *Nature*. Its title was disarmingly literal: “Rubber hands ‘feel’ touch that eyes see.” The authors described an interaction among vision, touch and proprioception—the sense that tells you where your limbs are even when you cannot see them. [1]

An everyday hand is rarely a puzzle. You can watch your fingers move; you feel your skin being touched; your joints and muscles signal where the limb is. Those cues generally agree. The laboratory is interesting because it makes them disagree in a controlled way. The person can see a plausible hand receive a touch, physically feel a touch elsewhere, and nevertheless report an experience that favors the visible object.

The popular retelling often stops there: *Your brain can be tricked into believing a rubber hand is real.* That sentence conveys some of the astonishment but loses the most important part of the research: precisely what was measured, why different measurements sometimes disagree, and what alternative accounts remain viable.

## The evidence in the room

The classic arrangement aligns a fake hand with a participant's concealed real hand and uses simultaneous brushing. A delayed or mismatched brushing condition can serve as a comparison. In the proposed account, matching visual and tactile events invite the perceptual system to treat the visible hand as a likely source of the felt touch. A person's prior body knowledge and hand position are part of that inference. [1]

But we need to ask what the researchers mean by *ownership*. It is not a legal claim, a belief held after contemplation or a permanent modification of body identity. Investigators typically record agreement with statements about the subjective experience during the procedure. They may also ask participants to indicate where their hidden finger lies, sometimes observing a change in estimated location toward the rubber hand. Researchers call that second response **proprioceptive drift**.

Those are different measurements. One concerns what the person says the rubber hand *felt like*. The other concerns a spatial estimate of where the unseen real hand is. If both happen together, they are related observations; neither can automatically stand in for the other.

This distinction explains why the next major chapter matters more than a dramatic video of someone startled by a rubber hand.

## The finger that seemed to move

In 2011 Marieke Rohde, Massimiliano Di Luca and Marc O. Ernst published a study with a title that reads like a corrective in the margin of a famous experiment: “The Rubber Hand Illusion: feeling of ownership and proprioceptive drift do not go hand in hand.” [2]

Using automated brushing and a projected probe, they interrupted exposure at different intervals and asked participants to judge where their unseen hand was. They varied synchronous brushing, asynchronous brushing and conditions with vision but no brushing. Their abstract reports an unexpected pattern: with frequent measurement, an apparent drift in felt hand location occurred even in control conditions. Drift under synchronous stroking was not greater than in a just-vision condition. By contrast, subjective ownership ratings were tied to synchronous stroking and not the asynchronous condition.

That finding does **not** say the rubber-hand illusion was disproven. It says a convenient proxy does not measure exactly the same thing as a reported experience. Depending on the task and timing, the location estimate may shift without a corresponding sense that the artificial hand belongs to the observer.

This is a recurring lesson in perception research. A stopwatch measurement, a questionnaire and an outward behavior can all appear to describe the same experience while tapping different processes.

A second 2011 study, by Holle and colleagues, reported proprioceptive drift with hands rotated by 180 degrees even when questionnaire reports of ownership were absent. This reinforced the possibility that hand-location estimates and felt ownership can come apart. [3] Again, the result belongs to those experimental conditions, not to a blanket assertion that drift is meaningless.

The follow-up literature did not simply choose one side. In a 2023 Bayesian meta-analysis, Giorgia Tosi, Benedetta Mentesana and Daniele Romano reported evidence favoring a correlation between subjective embodiment and proprioceptive drift, with a correlation around **0.35**. That indicates an association, not identity. The authors argued that the measures capture different facets of the phenomenon. The full article is restricted in the version consulted for this draft; this numerical result is drawn from its publisher abstract. [4]

The difference between *some association* and *the same thing* sounds modest. It is not. Suppose a seller claims that because an audio session makes someone feel calm, it must therefore change a specific brain rhythm in a lasting way. A useful consumer would ask whether the feeling and the alleged physiological change were measured independently. The rubber-hand literature teaches the importance of that question. It does not validate the seller's audio claims.

## A brush can also deliver a suggestion

There is a second problem that the elegant demonstration sometimes hides: the participant knows they are in an experiment. People form ideas about what is supposed to happen. A laboratory procedure can convey expectations without anyone uttering an explicit command.

Peter Lush's 2020 paper in *Collabra: Psychology* challenged the adequacy of conventional rubber-hand controls. Participants were shown information about the procedure and asked what experiences they expected in its conditions. Their expectations for illusion and control statements varied in ways resembling patterns of published self-reports. Lush argued that **demand characteristics**—the cues a research procedure supplies about the expected response—may confound common questionnaire comparisons. He proposed stronger controls. [5]

That is an important methodological challenge, particularly in a field that studies subjective experience. But it would be premature to transform Lush's argument into the verdict that the illusion is “just hypnosis.”

In 2022, H. Henrik Ehrsson and colleagues published a *Nature Communications* Matters Arising analysis titled “No specific relationship between hypnotic suggestibility and the rubber hand illusion.” Their reanalysis of an existing dataset of **353** participants compared suggestibility measures with the difference between synchronous and asynchronous conditions. They reported no significant association on the relevant adjusted ownership measure, and disputed the interpretation that suggestibility specifically accounted for the illusion. [6]

This is a real scientific disagreement about mechanisms and controls. Lush identifies expectancies and the limitations of existing comparison conditions. Ehrsson and coauthors argue that, under the contrasts they consider appropriate, the results support a primarily multisensory account. Neither finding is a license to insist that every report of an illusion must be generated by one mechanism alone.

An intellectually serious piece of writing should put both arguments before readers. The contrast is especially valuable at Final Vestige because the site has already examined the history of Mesmerism and Puységur. Historical practitioners used language about suggestion, magnetic fluid and altered states long before contemporary laboratories possessed these measurement techniques. The similarity in *questions*—how expectations, attention and sensation interact—is worth noticing. The historical claim of animal magnetism, however, is not confirmed by a twenty-first-century experiment with brushes and plastic hands.

## A demonstration you can evaluate without needing to feel an illusion

You do not need to buy a plastic limb—or ask anyone to conceal their own—to learn something from the study.

Start with an ordinary scene: a written diagram of a *visible fake hand*, a *hidden real hand*, and two brushes. Label one sequence **synchronous**, with both strokes at the same instant. Label another **asynchronous**, with one stroke noticeably delayed. Before reading an article's conclusion, write down three predictions.

First, would you expect the perceived location of the real hand to change? Second, would you expect the participant to *say the fake hand felt like theirs*? Third, could a person report one without the other?

Now compare those predictions with the findings above. Rohde and colleagues found drift under some conditions that did not produce the matching ownership report. Tosi and colleagues later reported a moderate association across studies, not equivalence. And the dispute over suggestion shows why an experiment's presentation, not merely its intended stimulus, matters to the answer.

This is a **research-reading exercise**, not a rubber-hand illusion administered to you, not a standardized psychological test and not a treatment. It demonstrates what different dependent variables would mean on paper. You cannot infer your own suggestibility from whether the diagram seems convincing.

For a second pass, read the Lush and Ehrsson abstracts side by side. Underline each author's measure of interest and the comparison condition. You will find that two researchers can examine overlapping data while asking importantly different statistical questions.

If you'd rather explore another perceptual demonstration directly, the site's published [Change Blindness experiment](https://finalvestige.com/2026/11/02/change-blindness/) offers a genuine interactive task with an explanation of its timing differences from the original laboratory procedure.

## Why this matters outside a laboratory

The spectacle of the rubber-hand illusion makes for a compelling short video. It supplies an arresting image: an object you know is artificial appears to participate in an intimate experience of self. It would be easy to cut that video against a claim that perception is endlessly “programmable,” or that a purchased recording can modify the brain in whatever direction its narrator promises.

The studies do not establish that. They concern controlled relationships among visual, tactile and bodily signals, measured in particular ways with particular participants. They do not demonstrate that an audio track can cure anxiety, change personality, repair trauma, produce exceptional concentration or permanently “rewire” a listener. They do not establish the clinical efficacy of consumer hypnosis or commercial meditation audio.

There is a less sensational and more useful conclusion: **our feeling of certainty is not itself a measurement of the process that produced it**. It is a report worth taking seriously, alongside other evidence and controls.

That same discipline belongs in an independent purchasing guide. When a company promises better focus or greater calm, ask what it actually supplies. Is it an MP3 library, an instructed practice, a course, a coaching session? Were the outcome measures predefined? Was the specific product studied, or is it borrowing prestige from loosely related work? Are there free alternatives? What does the seller actually promise about refunds? Those are the questions that turn a striking piece of psychology into a practical standard for evaluating claims.

The official Zen12 sales page, for example, describes a structured set of twelve levels with four listening styles per level. That is a description of what the seller offers. The same page makes strong claims about brainwave entrainment and meditation outcomes that we have not independently substantiated for this branded product. Readers should not mistake the product's format, or a fascinating psychology experiment, for proof of those results. [7]

## Questions that survive the demonstration

**Does the rubber hand become part of the brain's body map?** The research offers evidence of altered short-term experiences and hand-location judgments under laboratory conditions. The exact mechanisms, measures and their relationship remain topics of research. The phenomenon does not imply a permanent change in body identity.

**Can everybody feel it?** No universal response rate follows from the articles cited here. Individual reports and experimental conditions vary, and the measures are not interchangeable.

**Is the illusion created by hypnosis?** Suggestion and demand characteristics are part of an active methodological debate. The 2020 challenge and 2022 reanalysis should be read together rather than using either as a universal verdict.

**Does the experiment establish the value of Zen12, Hypnosis Live or Brainwave Shots?** No. None of the cited experiments tests those branded products.

**Can I try a home version?** A video or home demonstration may be interesting but is not equivalent to a controlled experiment and cannot diagnose or treat anything. The paper-based reasoning exercise above is the demonstration offered here.

## Reading the record

The first experiment is a landmark not because it tells us that all experiences are fake, but because it gives us a way to make a private feeling publicly discussable. The later studies then reveal how difficult that discussion becomes when a feeling, a spatial judgment and the expectations of the experiment have to be disentangled.

An archive is strongest when the revisions remain visible. We should admire the original puzzle, preserve the best explanations and put honest boundaries around what the findings warrant. That is how a rubber hand on a table becomes more than a trick.
