# The Doorway Did Not Steal Your Thought
**Original Final Vestige Mind & Perception investigation · Unpublished · November 3, 2026**

*You entered the room for a reason. Five seconds later, the reason seems to belong to another person. It is tempting to blame the doorway itself. What do the experiments actually show?*

## The threshold
Picture an ordinary household errand. A person decides to retrieve a folder, leaves a desk, crosses a hallway and enters another room. There is the folder, perhaps. But the purpose has gone missing. The experience seems so definite that a familiar explanation has grown around it: the brain closes its previous mental file every time a person passes through a door.

That explanation has a scientific ancestor. Yet the scientific record is more complicated, and increasingly interesting. An experiment can demonstrate worse performance on a particular memory task after someone crosses an event boundary without demonstrating that every doorway erases intentions. New studies published in January 2026 make the simple folklore even harder to defend. [S01–S04]

The difference between observed experience and assigned cause is Final Vestige territory. It is the difference between seeing something, sincerely reporting it, and proving why it happened.

## The room-change experiments
In experiments published in 2011, Gabriel Radvansky, Sabine Krawietz and Andrea Tamplin examined changes in access to recently encountered information as people moved between locations. Participants handled or encountered objects while moving through virtual or physical rooms. Researchers probed memory after movement across a doorway or within the same room, using tasks designed to distinguish the change of location from movement alone. The researchers found location-updating effects under their conditions. [S01][S06]

The experiments mattered because distance was not the whole explanation. If the person walking within one room had traveled as far as someone entering another, then additional steps alone did not account for the difference. In one experiment the investigators brought participants back to an earlier room. The return did not restore the original advantage. That posed difficulties for the idea that memory loss simply reflected the absence of matching surroundings. [S01]

But what was being tested? It was not a sample of hundreds of unprompted kitchen errands. Nor was it a sweeping census of how often ordinary people lose goals. It was performance on probes for experimentally designated objects under deliberately structured conditions. The result is real evidence about that arrangement of memory and environmental context. The leap from that to a universal doorway switch in the brain requires much more evidence.

The authors interpreted their findings through an event-cognition account. The mind maintains a working representation of what is happening now: people, objects, intentions, places, and probable next actions. When a meaningful boundary interrupts that activity, the current model may be updated or replaced. Details that belonged to the previous episode may become harder to retrieve. This is a proposed model of cognition, not literal biological filing cabinets clicking shut at every threshold.

## One boundary; several changes
What is special about a doorway? It announces a new location. It often introduces a different visual scene, a changed social situation and a different task. Sometimes all those changes occur at once. This makes doorways interesting experimental stimuli but difficult causes to isolate completely.

Consider a simple contrast. One participant moves through a doorway while carrying an object to remember. Another crosses the same distance within a single room. The first answers a later probe less accurately. We have evidence about the consequence of a particular controlled transition. We do not yet know whether the decisive ingredient was spatial context, attention diverted by a new scene, the demand to update the current event model, interference from a later object, or some combination.

A 2018 study by Kyle Pettijohn and Gabriel Radvansky added a useful distinction: people actively navigating an environment and people passively observing the same movement could both show location-updating effects, although the effect was smaller for passive observers. Thus movement of one's own body was not the only plausible contributor in that paradigm. [S05]

Experimental wording matters. Recognition accuracy, false alarms, the sequence of learned items, and subjective loss of an intention are not identical outcomes. A theory that explains one of them cannot automatically be claimed to explain them all.

## The 2021 challenge
Jessica McFadyen and colleagues published a deliberately inconvenient paper in 2021: *Doorways do not always cause forgetting*. The researchers conducted four experiments, using immersive virtual reality, passive video viewing and real-world movement. They manipulated working-memory demands and tested recognition of objects associated with locations. [S02]

The study did not find a general, reliable doorway-linked decline in the way a sweeping headline would imply. Under substantial working-memory load in one virtual-reality experiment, however, a specific difference did emerge: more false alarms to mismatched memory probes. Participants were more likely to treat certain incorrect probes as if they matched what was relevant.

That detail changes the story. It suggests that remembering under competing demands may become more susceptible to interference when the environment shifts. It does not establish that a doorframe, by itself, deletes information; nor that all memories behave alike. The authors questioned the generalizability of the earlier phenomenon to changes in task design. They did not prove every earlier finding false. They made the conditions visible.

A good research history includes the experiment that did not cooperate. Final Vestige should preserve such inconvenient results instead of erasing them from a compelling narrative.

## Two January 2026 studies
In January 2026, Julie Lamont and David Bilkey published an immersive VR investigation in *Memory & Cognition*. Ninety participants explored a five-room virtual building containing interactive objects. Some encountered distractor tasks as they traversed doorways; others encountered such tasks in the middle of rooms. Researchers then assessed memories for object order and location. [S03]

The control group showed a boundary-related pattern. But a demanding visuospatial distraction impaired object memory when it arrived inside a room, while placing the same kind of interruption at the doorway did not simply produce an additional effect. One inference is that demanding interruptions can segment an ongoing experience even without the physical boundary. The precise account depends on the task and the dependent measure, and the study was not a trial of everyday forgetting or an audio intervention.

A second January 2026 paper challenged the expectation that boundaries always make memory worse. Noah Crockett, Dani Parra, Abigail Doolen and Gabriel Radvansky tried to replicate an earlier finding that separating word learning between rooms could improve later performance. Across two experiments, they found limited overall evidence for a broad benefit. One experiment did show a localized advantage for words near a boundary; that advantage disappeared after a short delay during which participants moved through other rooms. [S04]

The implication is not that people should walk through more doorways to strengthen their memories. It is that events can sometimes be organized into useful chunks, while particular recent details may become less accessible after a transition. These measurements can point in different directions because they ask different questions.

## Why the myth persists
The anecdote is unusually memorable. There is a visible threshold, a moment of disorientation and an immediately plausible culprit. It offers a cause people can point at. It also compresses a difficult body of research into a trick simple enough for a short video.

But everyday forgetfulness can have multiple contributing factors: divided attention, interrupted goals, distractions and changing cues, among others. A person can sincerely say an intention vanished while not knowing why. Even within the controlled literature, a finding about laboratory objects may not generalize to tasks involving personal plans, language or long-term autobiographical memory.

The strongest account is more careful than the catchphrase: **event boundaries may alter the organization and accessibility of information, but the effect depends on what is remembered, how it is tested and what else demands attention.** [S01][S02][S03][S04]

## A free, non-diagnostic field observation
Try an exercise only during safe, routine activities. Write down a harmless goal before taking a short walk from one room to another: “retrieve the blue notebook,” for example. Record whether the intention was readily available when you arrived. On another occasion, note your experience with an equally ordinary task that does not involve crossing rooms. Record distractions, interruptions and whether another thought competed for attention.

There is no need to try to make yourself forget. Do not run, use dangerous equipment or conduct an experiment while driving. Keep the notes private. Two household observations cannot isolate cause, yield a reliable effect size or recreate the conditions of published controlled studies. They can show how readily our explanations outrun the observations.

There is also a stationary alternative: write three short groups of neutral words beneath separate headings, rest briefly and later recall them without looking. Observe how the headings affect your own description of the groups. This is an illustration of categorization, not a validated measure of cognitive health.

## The historical bridge: a report is not a mechanism
The published [Puységur Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) examines eighteenth-century reports of extraordinary experiences. Its source trail retains both dramatic testimony and failures, separating what the documents say from what their authors hoped animal magnetism could establish. That historical archive does not anticipate modern event-boundary experiments or validate any magnetic or hypnotic force. The connection is methodological: the discipline to resist claiming more than a report can prove.

The same standard applies to contemporary sellers of audio programs. Experiments about spatial updating provide no product-specific reason to conclude that Zen12, Hypnosis Live or Brainwave Shots improves memory or focus. A related buying guide may help readers evaluate a soundtrack format, but should not borrow credibility from a phenomenon it has never been shown to affect.

## What survives
A doorway can be meaningful to cognition without becoming magical. The 2011 experiments, 2021 conceptual challenge and two distinct 2026 studies show a living field working through boundary conditions, competing tasks and time scales. That is more interesting than a universal neurological rule, because it is open to being wrong in a testable way.

The question to keep is not “What did the doorframe do to my brain?” It is “Which change, under which conditions, made this information harder or easier to retrieve?”
