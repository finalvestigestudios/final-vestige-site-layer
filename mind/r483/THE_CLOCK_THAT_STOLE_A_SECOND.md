# The Clock That Stole a Second
### Chronostasis, the stopped-clock illusion, and the problems with turning perception science into product promises

**Final Vestige Studios | Original Mind & Perception manuscript | 18 November 2026 | UNPUBLISHED / scientific QA pending**

You look up at the clock. Its second hand appears to stop. The next tick seems late, almost indecently late, and then the hand returns to the familiar, indifferent tempo. Nothing has happened to the clock. The hand did not pause; the experienced duration of its first visible interval was wrong.

This small illusion has a formal name: **chronostasis**, literally the sense of time standing still. It reveals something more interesting than the truism that time flies when we are busy. The brain does not necessarily give conscious experience a timestamp that corresponds exactly to the moment a sensory signal becomes available. That may be a useful compromise in a system continually looking from one thing to another.

## The jump your eyes make

In everyday life our eyes are rarely motionless. They jump rapidly between targets in movements called saccades. When reading, we travel between groups of words. In a room, we look from face to face and from an object to the hand reaching for it. Yet the world usually appears stable and unbroken. The eye movements are real; we do not watch the world smear and vanish in the way a badly operated camera might.

In 2001, Kielan Yarrow, Patrick Haggard, Ron Heal, Peter Brown and John Rothwell asked what this perceptual continuity costs in the judgment of time. Their paper in *Nature*, “Illusory perceptions of space and time preserve cross-saccadic perceptual continuity,” examined duration judgments in the period following eye movements. The first visual stimulus after moving the eyes could appear to last longer than its actual interval.

They proposed a form of **backdating**. The percept of the newly acquired target, they suggested, was effectively assigned an onset earlier than when that detailed view became available—around the beginning of the saccade. The first interval thus seems to cover more time than it actually did. Nothing about this account implies that physical time slowed, that the next second was predicted, or that a person can choose to create the effect at will.

One of the paper's crucial details is easily lost in retelling. If the target moved unpredictably during the eye movement, breaking the target's apparent spatial continuity, the effect disappeared in that experimental condition. This observation provided a way of discriminating between ideas about temporal estimation. The hypothesis was not merely that “the eyes cause a magic pause.” It linked subjective duration to the system's attempt to preserve a stable target.

**Primary source:** Yarrow et al., *Nature* 414, 302–305 (15 November 2001), https://doi.org/10.1038/35104551 .

## Why the kitchen-clock version is not the experiment

The ordinary stopped-clock anecdote lacks a controlled interval, an independent timing measure, and a procedure specifying where observers look. When someone says that the hand paused for a second, the report is evidence of their impression, not a stopwatch measurement of their illusion. The laboratory study is stronger because it compares judgments to an interval whose duration is known and varies specific aspects of the situation.

This difference also limits sweeping claims about perception. The experiment does not mean people constantly see the wrong time, nor that eyewitnesses are unreliable in every circumstance. It addresses a conditional distortion under particular visual tasks. We can be impressed by the effect without pretending that every glance is an error.

A review and commentary by Kai Thilo and Vincent Walsh in *Current Biology* in February 2002 placed the phenomenon alongside saccadic suppression and compression of visual space. Commentary is not a second independent trial. It can explain the original study, but counting a discussion paper as a fresh replication would inflate the evidence ledger.

**Context, not an independent experiment:** Thilo & Walsh, “Vision: When the Clock Appears to Stop,” https://doi.org/10.1016/S0960-9822(02)00707-8 .

## A stopped clock for the ears

The simple story is that the mind covers the visual interruption made by the eyes. But what if subjective time can be distorted when the eyes are not the decisive moving part?

In October 2002, Iona Hodinott-Hill, Kai Thilo, Alan Cowey and Vincent Walsh published “Auditory chronostasis: hanging on the telephone.” They studied perceived duration of silent gaps between sounds delivered over headphones. In one condition listeners attended to sounds presented to the same ear; in another, their attention shifted between ears. The shift changed duration judgments in a way the researchers called auditory chronostasis.

The result challenges the claim that chronostasis is exclusively a visual phenomenon arising only from saccadic eye movement. But it does **not** show that vision and hearing share precisely the same cause. A shared phenomenological label is not a discovered shared neural mechanism. Different tasks can yield superficially similar timing judgments, and a careful theorist must explain both the similarities and differences.

**Primary source:** Hodinott-Hill et al., *Current Biology* 12, 1779–1781 (15 October 2002), https://doi.org/10.1016/S0960-9822(02)01219-8 .

## What happens when a hand touches something?

Another variation came in July 2003. Yarrow and Rothwell studied manual chronostasis in relation to tactile perception and movement. Their paper's provocative title, “Manual chronostasis: tactile perception precedes physical contact,” should not be read as evidence for precognition. It describes a controlled contrast between physical event timing and subjective judgment.

Here the stopped-clock idea leaves the clock behind altogether. The common issue is how a person perceives the temporal placement or duration of a sensation around an action. We can ask whether the sensory modalities depend on one timing process, several related processes, or task-specific combinations. These questions are legitimate precisely because the experiments do not force a single answer.

**Primary source:** Yarrow & Rothwell, *Current Biology* 13, 1134–1139 (1 July 2003), https://doi.org/10.1016/S0960-9822(03)00413-5 .

## Why a later experiment matters

An account that starts with a striking demonstration can harden into a tidy teaching myth. Eye jumps, image fills in, time stops. In 2020 a *Scientific Reports* paper revisited candidate mechanisms: action-backdating, intentional binding and post-saccadic temporal expansion. The study contrasted voluntary and cued action settings and compared keypresses with eye movements while measuring perceived onset of actions and tones.

The reported timing distortions did not simply behave the same way across action types. Saccades showed temporal expansion rather than the compression pattern associated with keypress conditions, and movement intentionality mattered. The implication is not that the original work was wrong. It is that a family of duration effects may arise through more than one interaction between action and perception.

**Primary source:** “The role of action intentionality and effector in the subjective expansion of temporal duration after saccadic eye movements,” *Scientific Reports* (2020), https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-020-73830-6 .

The best research narrows our possible explanations instead of handing us ever bigger adjectives. “The brain changes time” sounds wonderful. “Observers overestimated the earliest post-saccadic interval when a stable target was maintained” is less dramatic and considerably more useful.

## What does this have to do with suggestion?

Final Vestige has a historical archive dedicated to another extraordinary realm of experience: [Puységur's Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/). The case dossiers preserve reports of unusual states, claimed cures, witnesses, signed documents, failures and warnings about operator power. Their paper trail provides evidence that particular claims were made and recorded. It does not establish that a magnetic fluid existed or caused what observers reported.

Chronostasis and eighteenth-century animal magnetism are not instances of the same experimental phenomenon. Their value together is in teaching an evidentiary discipline. We must separate a report from the mechanism a reporter or investigator assigns to it. We must not treat historical documentation as a direct demonstration of an invisible cause.

Final Vestige's published investigation of [Franklin's 1784 Commission on mesmerism](https://finalvestige.com/2026/11/02/mesmerism/) makes the same point from another direction. In blindfold and sham-treatment conditions, the commissioners tested what would happen when a participant's belief about magnetization and the actual operator action diverged. They observed responses, but rejected the proposed magnetic fluid as an explanation for them. Their findings apply to the particular operations examined, not all later hypnosis research.

## Not an advertisement for a better brainwave

There is a particular danger when perception research travels into commercial self-help: a study about sensation or timing is made to imply that a music track can change the brain on demand. None of the chronostasis experiments reviewed here tested Zen12, Hypnosis Live, Brainwave Shots or any branded meditation audio. Their results cannot substantiate product-specific promises about accelerated meditation, curing anxiety, strengthening memory or producing a guaranteed mental state.

Even broader EEG research cannot automatically bridge that gap. In 2023 a systematic review of fourteen heterogeneous binaural-beat EEG studies found inconsistent results—five supporting a brainwave-entrainment hypothesis, eight contradictory and one mixed. The paper did not test Zen12. It would be misleading to invoke that review as proof of the product or to treat the existence of auditory neuroscience as a certificate of efficacy.

**Independent review:** https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0286023 .

A useful commercial tool may still be worth considering because of its audio format, accessibility, convenience or personal preference. Those are consumer questions. The health, cognitive and performance claims are different questions, and each requires its own substantiation.

## A free twelve-minute source audit

You can perform this reading exercise without exposing yourself to flashing stimuli, paying a subscription, or using an audio product.

**Minutes 0–3:** Read the abstract of the 2001 visual study. State the result in ordinary words without claiming that physical time changed. Identify the role of eye movement and target continuity.

**Minutes 3–6:** Read the auditory chronostasis abstract. What would an exclusively eye-based explanation have trouble explaining? Write down one plausible alternative, marking it as an untested hypothesis.

**Minutes 6–9:** Read the manual/tactile abstract and the 2020 study's description of competing mechanisms. Write one question that the experiments have not fully resolved.

**Minutes 9–12:** Compare these studies with a claim from a hypothetical seller of “brainwave” audio. Ask whether that exact recording, marketed outcome, audience and dose were tested. If not, put the claim in the “not demonstrated here” column. Do not confuse “a scientific-sounding term appears in both places” with evidence.

## Why the second felt stolen

The stopped clock is a reminder that an apparently simple first-person report can be exquisitely real as an experience yet incomplete as a measurement. Good science does not merely explain away a curious second; it develops tests that can distinguish ideas about why the second seemed to last.

Good historical investigation works the same way: keep the original report, record the conditions, make failures visible and refuse to promote the favored mechanism beyond the evidence. The clock kept moving. The discipline of looking carefully is what we gain.
