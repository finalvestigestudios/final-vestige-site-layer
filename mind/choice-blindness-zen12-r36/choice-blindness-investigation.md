# The Photograph You Never Chose

**FINAL VESTIGE / Mind & Perception / UNPUBLISHED independent investigation**

*You select one photograph. You explain another. Choice blindness tests the distance between an actual decision and the account that follows it.*

## A photograph, a choice, a missing moment

Imagine two portraits placed in front of you. You study the expressions, decide which person seems more appealing, and point. A moment later, the experimenter hands back the photograph you supposedly selected and asks why. You explain the eyes, the expression, something you cannot quite name. Except the image in your hand is the one you rejected. The experiment has not changed the past. It has changed the evidence of your decision being presented to you.

This unsettling sequence is the basis of a 2005 experiment by Petter Johansson, Lars Hall, Sverker Sikström, and Andreas Olsson, published in Science. In a series of choices between faces, the researchers sometimes covertly exchanged the outcome of a selection. Participants could fail to detect a mismatch between what they had picked and what they were shown. Some nevertheless gave explanations for why they supposedly preferred the displayed result. The researchers called this choice blindness. [1]

The popular version of this story tends to leap past the difficult part. It may claim that people do not really know what they want, that all decisions are fictions, or that a sufficiently persuasive person can make you believe anything. The study shows none of these propositions. The original choices were real, the outcome sometimes was manipulated, and certain mismatches went undetected under defined conditions. A compelling finding deserves an explanation precise enough to survive a skeptical rereading.

## The experimental question

At first, this seems a study of taste in faces. More precisely, it tests monitoring: when your intended choice is different from the outcome handed back to you, do you recognize the discrepancy? Researchers could independently compare the selection with the presented result, something the participant could not necessarily do from memory alone.

Part of the experiment's force comes from an ordinary social expectation. When someone conducting a task returns the object you just chose, you tend to assume they followed your instruction. There is not always a reason to recheck the procedural history. That expectation can carry an incorrect outcome directly into the next question: why did you choose this?

Some explanations were fluent and specific. Yet fluency and historical accuracy are different properties. A person can truthfully describe what seems appealing about an image now without accurately reporting what caused an earlier decision. The later justification is itself evidence about current judgment, but not necessarily a reliable transcript of the earlier choice. Johansson and colleagues pursued this gap between reasons and introspection in a subsequent analysis of participants' verbal reports. [2]

It is also important to preserve the observations that resist a sensational interpretation. Choice blindness is not universal. Whether a substitution is detected can depend on the procedure, the stimuli, the interval, and what counts as detection. Nor does failure to correct a switch imply that the participant would remain mistaken after an explicit comparison. To understand any percentage quoted about this effect, inspect the study's trial conditions and denominator first.

## The jam stand brought the question into a supermarket

In 2010, Lars Hall, Petter Johansson, and their colleagues reported a striking extension outside the face-choice task. At a supermarket, shoppers sampled varieties of jam and tea, chose a favorite, and were asked to sample their choice again. Researchers sometimes secretly switched the sample so that the item presented as the favorite was the alternative the shopper had rejected. Participants then explained their preference. [3]

This is a different sensory world from photographs. The experiment involved taste and smell, and included surprisingly divergent alternatives, such as cinnamon-apple and bitter grapefruit. The authors reported that no more than a third of manipulated trials were detected overall, although detection was higher for certain especially distinct alternatives. It would be incorrect to portray every participant as fooled or to turn a per-trial result into a universal percentage of people who cannot recognize what they taste.

The supermarket example is useful because it shows how an explanation can become attached to an outcome when the original sequence is no longer directly available. But it does not tell us how often surgeons misidentify instruments, how judges weigh evidence, or how buyers behave in a high-value purchase. Different settings have different stakes, opportunities to check, and procedures. A legitimate research connection is not permission to import a dramatic rate into unrelated situations.

## When the question became political

A later study asked participants about political issues during a Swedish election. Researchers covertly changed recorded survey responses and then asked participants to elaborate on the altered positions. The published paper reports limited corrections of manipulated replies in that setting and examines subsequent stated voting intentions. [4]

This work attracts bold claims because it involves convictions rather than pictures. But an experimentally altered survey response is not a mind-control procedure, and a subsequent statement of possible political openness is not proof of a durable change in party identification or a changed vote. The paper is evidence about its specific procedure and measurements. It also introduces ethical questions about deception in research and the need for consent and debriefing.

For our archive, the safest lesson is methodological rather than partisan. If an answer is shown back to you in a new form, that presentation can influence the reason you give next. A conscientious reader compares what was recorded first with what was explained later. An especially confident account may still be an account of the version of the evidence now in front of its speaker.

## A reason is not necessarily a record

People provide narratives all day: why a building looks familiar, why one product feels trustworthy, why they chose a route home. Many of those narratives are useful. The error lies in treating all explanations as transparent access to the mental process that produced an earlier choice. Psychology has long debated what introspection reveals and what people reconstruct. Choice-blindness studies put an independent record alongside the explanation, making a narrow but powerful comparison possible. [2]

Think of two statements. 'I prefer this photograph' can describe an actual judgment in the present. 'This is the photograph I selected before the experimenter moved the cards' describes a historical event. The second statement is testable only if the original action was recorded. When the experimenter returns the wrong photograph, the testimony can concern the wrong object without the speaker deciding to lie.

The distinction also changes how we approach eyewitness memory. Choice blindness is not interchangeable with the misinformation effect, change blindness, or hypnotic suggestibility. Each names a different experimental phenomenon with different methods and evidentiary limits. They may all invite curiosity about the reliability of experience, but none supplies a universal mechanism for every disputed witness account.

## Why this belongs beside Puységur's files

Final Vestige's published Puységur's Animal Magnetism Files examine eighteenth-century records of magnetic somnambulism, claimed cures, predictions, witness statements and the vocabulary of rapport. The archive makes its provenance explicit: 153 active source rows, 16 case dossiers, and a distinction between the original 1785 source role and later working verification material. Crucially, failed cures and failed predictions remain visible rather than being removed from the story. [5]

Choice blindness neither proves nor disproves those historical claims. There was no modern randomized face-choice protocol in Puységur's eighteenth-century cases, and it would be irresponsible to diagnose historical participants from a modern experiment. The connection lies in a shared standard of inquiry: separate the event from the later description and look for independently preserved evidence.

A historian needs the document, edition, date and witness context before endorsing an explanation. A scientist needs the actual choice, the manipulation and the report. Both are helped by keeping inconvenient evidence. If a later interpretation replaces the original observation without acknowledgment, the record becomes a story that can no longer be checked.

## An honest field exercise: preserve your own before and after

Try a simple reflection without tricking anyone. Put two different everyday objects in front of you: a smooth stone and a folded cloth, perhaps. Decide which one you would rather keep on your desk. Before you talk about it, write down which object you selected and one reason. Record the time.

Ten minutes later, look at the same objects and, without reading the first note, record your preference and the reason you give now. Only then compare the two entries. Perhaps your explanation has shifted. Perhaps it is nearly identical. Neither outcome reproduces the 2005 experiment. The value of the exercise is having a prior record instead of relying on a persuasive retrospective account.

For a better observation, note the lighting, any distractions, and whether you knew you would be asked to justify the selection. These details are not a clinical assessment, a test of suggestibility, or an invitation to manipulate someone else. They are the small habits by which a person learns not to let an appealing explanation erase what actually happened.

## The evidence survives the story

The photograph returned to the participant was not proof that the person lacked agency. It was an occasion when a mismatch between intention and presented outcome was not always noticed. That is surprising enough without offering it as a universal law of behavior.

When someone offers you a dazzling account of their own mind, of a historical witness, or of a commercial product's psychological power, there is one question worth carrying from the laboratory into the archive: what was recorded before anyone had a reason to explain it? A fluent story can make a claim memorable. The original record can make it testable.
