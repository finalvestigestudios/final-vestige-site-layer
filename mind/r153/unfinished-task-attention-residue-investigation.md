# The Unfinished Task That Followed You Into the Next Room

*Attention residue, interruptions, and the limits of a one-minute return plan*

A document lies open on a desk. Three sentences remain before the end of a difficult passage, and a note in the margin holds the beginning of an idea. Then a message arrives. A meeting is starting. The reader leaves the document, opens the meeting, and looks at the person speaking. Yet part of the mind remains at the desk, somewhere between the unfinished sentence and the note.

This is an imagined scene, not an archival case or a scientific trial. Its usefulness lies in how ordinary it is. We tend to describe distraction as an intruder: a notification, a voice in the hallway, a demand on our time. But the distraction can persist after the intruder is gone. Work left unfinished continues to make claims on attention while we are doing something else.

Psychologist Sophie Leroy gave this difficulty a research name: **attention residue**. The phrase has escaped the journal into productivity advice and commercials promising effortless concentration. Before becoming a slogan, it deserves an investigation. What did the researchers actually measure? Why might a task be finished yet remain mentally present? Can a simple written plan help without requiring a purchase, a recording or a theory of supernatural powers?

The evidence suggests a modest and interesting answer. Thoughts about one activity can interfere with performance on a later one under conditions studied in laboratories. In certain experiments, briefly noting how to resume interrupted work helped participants turn to the next task. Neither finding guarantees results for any individual, any workplace, or any commercial audio product. [1–4]

## Changing the rule is itself a task

Long before the phrase attention residue gained a following, researchers examined what it takes to switch between tasks. In four experiments published in 2001, Joshua Rubinstein, David Meyer and Jeffrey Evans asked participants to alternate between tasks or repeat them. Some trials required classifying geometric objects under different rules. Others used arithmetic problems with changing numerical operations. Performance was measured across conditions varying task familiarity, rule complexity and advance cues. [1]

Switching carried time costs. The costs grew with the complexity of the rules and declined when cues clarified what came next. These findings were compatible with a model involving shifts in goals and activation of task rules. The important point is not a universal number of seconds lost per interruption—these laboratory procedures cannot establish one—but that changing tasks may itself involve additional mental operations.

The obvious explanation is that a new task requires setup. What about the old task? Does it vanish when its window closes?

Leroy's 2009 study, **Why Is It So Hard to Do My Work? The Challenge of Attention Residue When Switching Between Work Tasks**, examined that question. Across two experiments, the paper explored how thoughts about earlier work could linger as participants moved into another activity. Its abstract reports an awkward result: simply finishing the earlier task did not always produce clean disengagement. Under the tested conditions, finishing while under time pressure assisted the transition. [2]

That observation must not turn into advice to manufacture anxiety. An experimental deadline is not proof that rushing is wise, stress is healthy or pressure improves judgment in every setting. The paper supports a narrower proposition: the *condition in which a prior task is left* can affect engagement with what follows.

Concentration is therefore not only about what we look at. It is also about what we have not fully put down.

## The interruption has an afterlife

In 2018, Leroy and Theresa Glomb investigated a more specific difficulty: someone must leave unfinished Task A to perform Task B, and expects pressure upon returning to A. Their study, published in *Organization Science*, considered whether concern about a pending return keeps attention attached to the original task. [3]

A contemporary University of Washington account describes four studies. One surveyed 202 working professionals; other studies included laboratory tasks. The researchers evaluated a brief **ready-to-resume plan** in which people reflected on where interrupted work stood and how they would return to it. In the studied conditions, this intervention reduced measured attention residue and improved performance on the interrupting work. [3–4]

One crucial limitation is easily lost in retelling: the researchers did **not** test whether the plan improved performance on the *original* task once participants returned. Their observed benefit concerned attention and performance while working on the intervening assignment. [4] A commercial claim that the technique will make you finish your report twice as fast would be an unsupported extrapolation.

The intervention is appealing because it does not demand mystical certainty. It leaves a trace of the unfinished task somewhere other than working attention.

It also is not a universal prescription. A controlled assignment does not replicate every emergency, classroom, disability, caregiving obligation or occupation. Sometimes an interruption is urgent and no time for a note exists. The cited studies cannot diagnose attention difficulties, determine whether someone has ADHD, or establish a therapeutic effect.

## A small field experiment, without a sales pitch

The following is a free personal observation exercise inspired by the work—not a clinical intervention, not a validated instrument and not a reproduction of the 2018 experiment.

**First:** before leaving a low-stakes task, write where you stopped. Be specific: "Page twelve, last paragraph" is more useful than "finish reading"; "Spreadsheet, row eighty-four" is better than "fix report."

**Second:** record the next concrete action. Not the entire ambition—just the first move: "Check the newspaper date against the scanned original" or "Reconcile the invoice number against the ledger."

**Third:** if there is one, write a genuinely unresolved question. Do not spend five minutes solving it. The purpose is to make it locatable on return.

Now do the interrupting task. When you come back, inspect whether the note made it easier to resume. If it did not, record that too.

You could try the note during one suitable interruption and your ordinary method at another. But the tasks, your expectations and circumstances will be different, so the comparison does not prove the intervention works. The honest outcome is a question you can actually check: **Did I know where to restart?**

This method is especially well-suited to an evidence-minded publication. You do not need to assert invisible changes to brain chemistry or perfect performance. You can preserve the next step and see if it helped you get back to it.

## The investigator's unfinished question

Consider an archivist examining conflicting historical accounts. One witness dates an event to Tuesday; another remembers Wednesday. A careful investigator leaves a note: "Compare each account with the original newspaper, not a modern retelling." The note does not settle the conflict. It makes the next act of verification explicit.

That archivist is an illustration, not a subject in a reported experiment. The parallel is nevertheless useful. An investigation survives interruptions because a question can remain open *without being lost*.

Final Vestige's published [Puységur's Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) deal with historical claims about suggestion and unusual experiences. Those records can tell us what people reported and how particular ideas circulated. They cannot independently show that a modern commercial hypnosis recording has a clinically validated effect.

Our published [Change Blindness investigation](https://finalvestige.com/2026/11/02/change-blindness/) likewise concerns a limit on what people notice. Missing a change in a visual scene does not establish that attention has failed everywhere, or that a consumer product can repair the limitation. A studied perceptual effect is not an advertisement in disguise.

These stories belong together because they challenge the reliability of intuitive certainty. A witness may be sincere and mistaken. A task may have ended and still be mentally present. A persuasive demonstration can make us more curious without making us more credulous.

## Does multitasking always mean failure?

No two combinations of activities impose identical demands. Walking while talking differs from doing mental arithmetic while processing a complex argument. Task familiarity, the rules involved and available cues all matter. The 2001 experiments found variations with rules and cues, rather than a single penalty that applied to all switching. [1]

"Never multitask" is attractive precisely because it contains no caveats. Research becomes more useful when it retains its caveats. Likewise, the ready-to-resume studies cannot prove that a minute of writing will fix every interruption, eliminate anxiety, boost grades, or double productivity.

The moral story about focus also needs scrutiny. Being interrupted by children, colleagues or someone needing help is not a character defect. The experiments are not an excuse to treat other people as mere interruptions. Where a note is feasible, it may help make a transition clearer; where help is urgent, the person comes first.

## Meditation, hypnosis audio and the boundary between evidence and promotion

Three claims tend to collapse together in online self-improvement promotions:

1. Switching between laboratory tasks can impose measurable costs.
2. A brief ready-to-resume plan helped participants in defined experimental conditions.
3. A specific paid audio recording therefore reliably produces extraordinary concentration.

The sources reviewed here support claims one and two in their stated circumstances. **They do not establish claim three.**

Inspire3 markets products aimed at meditation, focus and personal growth. Its merchant, Hypnosis Live, describes the exact **Total Focus** product as a single 33-minute, 47-MB recording written and recorded by J. A. Amos. The product page also contains dramatic seller assertions about mental reprogramming and guaranteed concentration. Those are *seller claims*, not evidence produced by Leroy's experiments. We have not used the paid track or independently verified product-specific efficacy. [5]

A reader may prefer guided audio as a form of entertainment or structure. Preference is a legitimate personal consideration, but it is not evidence that the track treats a health condition or produces measurable productivity gains. A buyer should confirm the actual format, present checkout total, download and refund terms. The seller page displayed varying prices in different November 5 retrievals, and no checkout was independently tested. [5]

This independent investigation does **not** endorse a paid product or provide an affiliate earning link; the merchant page is cited only as a factual source about the seller's offer. Any later optional purchasing guide belongs in a visibly separate commercial section. An approved earning link would require documented partner authorization and a clear disclosure beside that link. The FTC's endorsement and health-advertising guidance requires financial connections to be understandable and benefit claims to be adequately supported. [6]

The distinction is not a bureaucratic footnote. It is the difference between explaining a study and borrowing the study's credibility to sell something it never tested.

## When the solution becomes another task

Attention is attractive to marketers because almost everyone wants more of it. The promise of guaranteed focus is simple; the evidence is not. A technique meant to help can become another demanding ritual: a playlist to perfect, a complicated tracker, or a score to worry about. None of those measures proves the original work got done.

The return note asks for less. It does not demand that the mind become silent or that a subscriber transform. It gives unfinished work an address.

That is the established Jetpack pathway; this manuscript creates no additional list, opt-in form or analytics script. Anonymous signup and welcome-message delivery still require independent end-to-end verification before being reported as completed leads.

A document still lies open on the desk. The meeting ends. On the margin is one sentence: "Verify the date against the primary source." The inquiry can begin again—not because a product conquered the mind, but because the unfinished question was left somewhere it could be found.
