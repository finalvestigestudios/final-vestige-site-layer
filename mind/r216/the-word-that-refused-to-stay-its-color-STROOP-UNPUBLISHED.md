# The Word That Refused to Stay Its Color
## J. Ridley Stroop, the trained habit of reading, and the mistake of treating an attention test as a promise

**FINAL VESTIGE STUDIOS / MIND & PERCEPTION — UNPUBLISHED RESEARCH DRAFT — 8 November 2026**  
**Editorial status:** Research-first, independently sourced; not a medical test or commercial endorsement. This article must not receive an invented public WordPress URL.

Try a small act of disobedience: look at the word **BLUE** printed in red ink, and say *red* instead of *blue*. Nobody is ordering you to be mistaken. Nobody has switched the page. Yet something very familiar moves first. Your eyes deliver the ink; years of reading reach for the word. For a fraction of a second, the two are rivals.

That hesitation has a name, but it is more useful than the name suggests. Almost a century ago, a psychology researcher built an experiment around the fact that two properties of the same mark on paper could demand two different responses. It was an experiment about interference, not a demonstration that people are foolish, suggestible or incapable of controlling their thoughts. The important question was whether the practiced response could get in the way of the response the experimenter actually requested.

His name was J. Ridley Stroop. The paper appeared in December 1935 in the *Journal of Experimental Psychology*: *Studies of Interference in Serial Verbal Reactions*. The original article is available in transcription through York University's Classics in the History of Psychology archive [S1], and its bibliographic record can be checked through the DOI [S2].

What Stroop showed was simpler, harder and more interesting than the viral demonstration suggests. Reading is extraordinarily practiced. Naming an ink color while refusing to read a contradictory word is not quite the same task. When the two instructions collide, performance changes. But measuring that collision in an experiment is not the same as measuring someone's intelligence, diagnosing a disorder or proving that a paid audio recording strengthens the mind.

### Two facts printed on the same paper

The original materials were not a glowing app, an animated hypnotic spiral or a one-click score. Stroop prepared lists of color names and ink colors. A participant could be asked to read color words; alternatively, to name the ink in which words were printed. Consider the written word *green* in red ink. A reader can identify the word, or can identify the color; the physical stimulus makes both possible. The instruction determines which information matters.

In the main comparisons, the researcher set incongruent color words against controls. The important controls were not merely a page of matching color words. Stroop also used words printed in black and colors presented separately from conflicting words. His central comparison separated **the job of reading** from **the job of naming a printed color**.

The asymmetry is striking. In the paper's summary, reading one hundred incongruently colored words took only 2.3 seconds longer than reading the same words in black—an increase of 5.6 percent that Stroop did **not** regard as reliable. Naming the ink of one hundred conflicting color words, by contrast, added 47 seconds—74.3 percent relative to naming colors presented without those words. Those are figures from Stroop's particular tasks and historical sample, not modern universal constants [S1].

The difference helps explain why an everyday exercise can feel almost absurd. If you are told to read, the letters supply the expected answer. If you are told to name the ink, the same letters provide a competing answer you have spent years becoming exceptionally good at producing. A habitual response does not need an argument to intrude.

The published paper described assistance from roughly three hundred college students across its work. That should not be flattened into a claim that each experiment had exactly three hundred participants in every condition. The methods and tables differ. Nor should a historical college-student sample be treated as representative of every language, age or circumstance [S1].

### Eight days, two hundred reactions a day

Stroop did not stop when he found interference. He also examined practice. He reported eight days of practice with two hundred color-naming responses each day. Performance improved; interference decreased. But it was not eliminated. Repeated exposure changed the relationship between task and response without making the conflict vanish [S1].

It is tempting to turn that result into a modern promotional sentence: train your attention and become immune to distraction. That sentence reaches far past the experiment. The intervention was practice on a particular visual-verbal task. Improvement on that task does not automatically transfer to paying bills, reading a difficult chapter, resisting a notification, treating anxiety or responding better to a meditation app.

This is the first boundary an honest article needs. **Task learning is not the same as broad cognitive transformation.** The gap between them is precisely where careless marketing likes to set up shop.

It is also worth noticing how modest the lesson is. Even after repeated practice, the irrelevant word remained consequential. We can adapt to interference without becoming a person who never experiences it.

### What the modern literature actually says

By 1991, Colin M. MacLeod could review more than fifty years of experiments. His integrative review, published in *Psychological Bulletin*, considered an extensive literature and identified numerous regularities that an explanatory theory would have to accommodate [S3]. It did not reduce the effect to a single tidy switch labeled *automatic reading*. Different experimental designs can change the interference observed; a word may compete in multiple ways with the task-relevant color.

A familiar explanation says that reading is automatic while color naming is controlled. This captures a useful intuition but is incomplete. Skilled reading can intrude even when it is not the goal, yet the balance of conflict depends on what participants are asked to do, the stimuli they see, the language they know, practice, response method and timing. The surrounding task matters.

If the word is written in a language someone cannot read, the competition is no longer the same. If a person has difficulty distinguishing a color, the apparent result may say more about that perceptual limitation than about response control. If the display is slow, the participant has time to prepare an answer. If the task includes hundreds of trials with carefully measured reaction times, it is no longer the same as a six-word internet card.

This matters because colorful social demonstrations routinely borrow the legitimacy of laboratory studies while leaving their controls behind. They can convey the *idea* of interference. They should not be sold as equivalent clinical or cognitive assessments.

A related line of experimental work concerns conflict adaptation: whether the experience of recent interference changes performance on a later trial. One influential paper by Botvinick and colleagues proposed a framework linking conflict monitoring and cognitive control [S4]. That framework has generated substantial research, but a theory of task adjustments is not proof that every fleeting hesitation represents a single trait called “focus,” or that a supplement or audio course can correct it. The question of which mechanisms produce particular experimental effects remains more complicated than an animation can show.

### Try the phenomenon without claiming to measure a brain

Below is an **illustrative, nonstandard exercise**, not a replica of Stroop's laboratory procedure. It works with a printout or screen, but it has not been calibrated for reaction-time research. Do not use it to score a person, compare children, infer neurodivergence or judge fitness for any job.

**Round one:** On a screen or paper with good contrast, make four large color patches: red, blue, green and purple. Say the color of each patch. Start at a comfortable pace. These are colors without conflicting printed names.

**Round two:** Read the written words *RED*, *GREEN*, *BLUE*, *PURPLE* printed in ordinary dark text. This calls for reading, not ink naming.

**Round three:** If you can distinguish the colors comfortably, use the original studio's six incongruent cards shown in the companion design draft. Each printed word names one color while its ink is a different color. Say the **ink color** aloud. Do not read the letters. It is normal if the urge to read is noticeable.

For an illustration of what this means without relying on color perception, imagine a printed label reading “NORTH” placed on a physical object pointing south. The label and the thing it labels can disagree. That analogy conveys competing instructions but **does not replicate** the Stroop stimulus or its measured effect. Screen readers may announce the words without announcing the ink, and a color-vision difference can make a color-based task unusable. Neither circumstance makes anyone a poor observer.

Want to compare an attempt with your own later attempt? Keep the viewing conditions, word set, instructions and relative pacing consistent, but expect practice and order effects. A single subjective experience, whether you hesitated or not, is not a research result. This is a classroom demonstration of an idea. It cannot prove that meditation changed cognition.

The most honest result might be that nothing dramatic happened. That, too, is information about your particular run of a tiny unstandardized exercise—not grounds for a diagnosis or a claim that you possess extraordinary self-control.

### The history of suggestion is not the history of color naming

Why does an evidence-minded historical studio include this in the same broad archive as Mesmer and Puységur?

The connection is not that Stroop proved animal magnetism. He did not. It is that the **instruction** matters. A witness can look at an object under one question and report one property; asked a different question, the same witness may prioritize something else. In Stroop's task, the contrast is explicit and measurable: report the word or report the ink. The experimenter can separate the signals and repeat the procedure.

The eighteenth-century material is a different category of evidence. Puységur's Animal Magnetism Files document historical claims, observations, procedures and testimony. They show what people said, did and believed, not a controlled demonstration that an invisible magnetic fluid or modern hypnosis product produces the advertised outcomes. See Final Vestige's published archival collection [S5]. The difference between a surviving claim and a replicated observation is part of the story.

The same principle applies to perception articles: a video about change blindness can make a person curious enough to examine a research paper. It cannot turn a historical treatment claim into a modern clinical finding by association. Nor does the fact that a visual task demonstrates interference establish anything about the benefits of sound pulses, subliminal recordings or guided hypnosis.

The bridge is methodological rather than magical: **what were participants asked to report, what was actually observed, and what alternative explanation remains open?**

### When an experiment becomes a purchasing argument

The science above has been used in popular discussions of attention training and mental performance. A reader who notices the conflict may reasonably want a calmer or more deliberate way to begin work. But attention is not one switch with a single volume knob.

Inspire3 publicly markets audio products including Zen12, Hypnosis Live and Brainwave Shots [S6]. The precise Zen12 Premium sales page describes a twelve-level library with 48 MP3s in four listening styles; it also makes strong promotional claims about minutes of listening, relaxation and accelerated meditation [S7]. Those are seller descriptions. Nothing in Stroop's 1935 task or MacLeod's review establishes that Zen12 reduces interference, changes task-general executive functioning, treats a condition or recreates an hour of practiced meditation.

There is an important consumer distinction here. **A well-organized library of guided recordings may be worth buying for convenience** to someone who enjoys that form of routine. Such preference is not a therapeutic claim. The package features and any final price, rights to download, refund eligibility and support should be checked against the actual transaction terms. That workbook has not been clinically tested either. You can use a plain timer without buying anything. The lesson is to measure the thing you actually want done, rather than mistake a captivating demonstration for proof of a product.

### What the demonstration cannot tell you

A single page of color words does not establish baseline attention, broad intellectual ability, a diagnosis, treatment needs, injury, the state of a reader's “brainwaves,” or the effectiveness of any purchased meditation audio. It doesn't establish whether you will be more productive on Tuesday. The effect depends on the task, the materials, and the person performing it. It can be measured under controlled conditions, but casual online graphics are not those conditions.

A reputable claims guide must ask what kind of evidence is required for the **particular product claim**. The Federal Trade Commission says objective health-related claims need appropriate scientific substantiation, and material affiliate relationships must be clearly disclosed where relevant [S8–S9]. Even an attractive video cannot make missing evidence appear.

Stroop had a simpler discipline. Give people an instruction. Arrange a conflict. Record what changed. Report the limits. The word did not refuse to change color; the observer was asked to separate one feature of the mark from another. That is a small, sturdy experiment. It deserves better than becoming a false promise.
