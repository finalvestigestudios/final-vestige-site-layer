# When a Beat Becomes a Promise: What Brainwave Audio Can—and Cannot—Prove

**Final Vestige Studios | Mind & Perception Archive | Independent investigation — UNPUBLISHED draft | 5 November 2026**

*There is a sound you can hear without anyone playing it. There is also an enormous gap between hearing that sound and proving that it makes your brain do what an advertisement promises.*

## The tone between two tones

Picture a quiet room, a pair of headphones and two steady notes. A researcher sends a pure tone of 400 hertz to one ear and 410 hertz to the other. The listener may perceive a gentle rhythmic fluctuation at the ten-hertz difference. No third loudspeaker is broadcasting a ten-hertz tone. The apparent beat is a product of auditory processing across the two ears. This is the **binaural beat**, a genuine perceptual phenomenon. It is neither imaginary in the everyday sense nor proof that the listener's whole brain has begun oscillating at ten hertz.

That second inference is where an interesting science becomes a commercial claim. You can experience a beat, measure changes in an electroencephalogram (EEG), report feeling calmer, and perform better on a particular attention test. Those are four different observations. One does not automatically establish the others. More importantly, an experiment on specially generated binaural tones does not establish what happens when a buyer downloads a different product built around *isochronic* pulses, spoken hypnosis, ambient nature recordings or a proprietary combination.

The distinction matters because audio programs promise convenience. A buyer may reasonably prefer a timed listening session to assembling their own concentration ritual. Convenience is an intelligible reason to pay. Promised instant cognitive transformation, however, is an objective efficacy claim and requires a different standard of proof.

The unusual story begins not in a digital storefront but in the anatomy of the ear.

## A signal, a response, an outcome

Pitch names how fast a sound wave vibrates; a beat describes a fluctuation in perceived or physical intensity. When two different frequencies arrive separately at the ears, the neural system can create a fluctuating impression called a binaural beat. When a sound is actually pulsed on and off at a regular rate, that is a different auditory stimulus: an amplitude-modulated or isochronic pattern. The two are not interchangeable just because a product describes them with the broad phrase *brainwave sounds*.

EEG measures electrical activity at the scalp. Researchers ask whether some measured activity follows a stimulus rhythm. But a finding of auditory steady-state response, or local rhythmic tracking, would not by itself prove better sustained attention, stronger memory, less anxiety, pain reduction, or four hours of effortless focus. Those outcomes call for carefully controlled behavioral or clinical measurements.

This separation between **perception**, **neural response** and **meaningful performance** is a useful test for any study someone cites to market an audio download. Which stimulus was actually used? Were headphones required? Was the comparison silence, ordinary music, a similar non-beat sound or an active program? How long were people exposed? What cognitive task was measured? Was the result reproduced? Were participants unaware of the expected benefit? And was the actual commercial audio tested?

Without those answers, the phrase *scientifically proven* is doing more work than the evidence.

## Fourteen studies and an uncomfortable split

In 2023 Ruth Maria Ingendoh, Ella Posny and Angela Heine published a systematic review asking a focused question: do binaural beats produce reliable EEG evidence of brainwave entrainment? They found fourteen eligible studies. **Five** reported results consistent with the entrainment hypothesis; **eight** reported contradictory findings; **one** was mixed. The researchers emphasized major differences in stimulus design, experimental procedures and EEG analyses, limiting how confidently results could be combined.

The lesson is not that sound cannot affect a brain. Sound plainly can. It is that a particular, frequency-specific entrainment hypothesis had not been established consistently by the studies the reviewers examined.

Primary review: Ingendoh, Posny & Heine (2023), *PLOS ONE*, https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0286023 ; PubMed https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37205669/ .

For a commercial claim, this is a crucial junction. A seller could accurately say that researchers have investigated brain responses to rhythmic auditory stimulation. They could not validly leap from that sentence to an unconditional promise that their particular download switches on a desired cognitive state in every listener. Such a promise would need credible studies of the actual intervention and relevant outcome.

## The studies do not all say no

A second mistake would be treating inconsistent EEG findings as proof that every binaural-beat experiment fails. A 2019 meta-analysis by García-Argibay, Santed and Reales pooled **22 studies** and reported **35 effect sizes**, with an overall medium standardized effect (**Hedges' g = 0.45**) across mixed outcomes involving cognition, anxiety and pain. That is a summary of heterogeneous research on binaural beats, not a guarantee that any named product improves focus by a predictable amount.

The investigators also examined variation in listening timing and exposure. Different comparison conditions and designs can matter enormously. A pooled result is not a product endorsement, and an effect on one outcome cannot establish every mental or physiological benefit a vendor lists.

Review: García-Argibay et al., *Psychological Research* (2019), https://doi.org/10.1007/s00426-018-1066-8 ; PubMed https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30073406/ .

Another 2023 experiment tested gamma-frequency, forty-hertz binaural beats in **58 healthy adults** on an attention network task and self-reported anxiety. Its authors reported no significant advantage in reaction time, error rates, attention-network efficiency or measured anxiety compared with the control. The experiment does not settle every frequency or every context, but it directly cautions against universal claims.

Experiment: Leistiko and colleagues (2023), https://doi.org/10.1007/s12144-023-04681-3 ; PubMed https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37359672/ .

A 2024 systematic review examined 31 studies of different auditory interventions—including music, noise and binaural beats—and autonomic measures such as cardiac, skin-conductance and pupillary responses alongside cognitive performance. Chee and colleagues found results mixed or insufficient for firm conclusions about the proposed arousal–performance pathway. That is another reason to resist the claim that an enjoyable listening sensation demonstrates a useful brain-state change.

Review: Chee and colleagues, *International Journal of Psychophysiology* (2024), https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38458383/ .

Put the studies together and an intellectually honest answer emerges: the mechanisms and behavioral effects merit research, some studies find positive results, others find no meaningful effects, and the intervention types and measurement methods differ. **None of these papers directly tested the commercial Focus Shot download.**

## The product is not the experiment

Final Vestige examined the official Inspire3 catalog and the exact merchant page for **Brainwave Shots: Focus Shot** on 5 November 2026. The seller page describes a fifteen-minute audio, a thirty-minute extended version, additional “brainwave-only” versions, a quick-start video and lifetime support. It states that headphones are not required. Its technical description says the thirty-minute protocol alternates between **12 and 18 hertz** and mixes ambient mountain sounds with brown noise. The merchant's FAQ attributes its approach to **isochronic tones**.

Sources: https://inspire3.com/ ; https://brainwaveshots.com/focus-shot ; https://brainwaveshots.com/faq .

That detail changes which studies can support which claims. The prominent EEG review above studied *binaural beats*. The vendor's description points toward *isochronic* stimulation. They may share the broad idea of rhythmic sound, but they are different interventions. The fact that binaural beats have been studied does not validate the seller's specific isochronic protocol. Nor does evidence about a frequency band prove that a fifteen-minute commercial mix will induce focused productivity for hours.

The seller's language about rapid focus, effortless productivity and several hours of effects remains **marketing claims**. It is not an independently established outcome of the listed recording. We have not listened to the product, examined a proprietary audio file, performed a blinded consumer trial, verified its checkout or processed a refund. A published independent guide must not pretend otherwise.

The seller page displayed a promotional dollar amount during this review. That amount is not a verified future checkout total; taxes, location, bundles and changing promotions may alter the amount. The merchant also describes a one-year guarantee, and its FAQ adds conditions concerning repeated refunds and larger orders. Whether those policies are honored in individual cases was not tested. The buyer's task is to read the current checkout and terms, not assume an archived screenshot is a contract.

## An old story about influence—and a modern caution

Final Vestige's existing [Puységur Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) examines how observers in the eighteenth century recorded striking behaviors, interpreted them through available theories, and sometimes ventured beyond the evidence their observations could carry. That archive is a historical investigation, not an endorsement of “animal magnetism.”

Modern sound technology belongs to a different scientific era. EEG exists; controlled trials exist; sophisticated analysis exists. Yet the logic of overreach can look familiar. Someone witnesses a remarkable response, assigns a persuasive mechanism, and markets a general promise. The proper historical connection is about **how claims become credible**, not about declaring past magnetists the discoverers of today's neuroscience.

At Final Vestige, an artifact is not the same thing as its interpretation. A frequency label on an audio file is an artifact-like description. Evidence of sustained attention is an experimentally measured outcome. Selling one as though it were the other blurs the line.

## Try a simple, free comparison—not a clinical test

Here is a low-stakes exercise that requires no purchased audio, proprietary frequencies or signup. It cannot identify what your brainwaves are doing. It can help reveal how easily expectation influences a personal impression.

Choose a short, harmless task such as proofreading an unfamiliar paragraph for repeated words or sorting a list of simple categories. Before beginning, write down what you will count: missed repetitions, completed items or time to finish. Do two comparable rounds on different material, one in quiet and one with ordinary, comfortable background audio of your choosing. Keep task difficulty, time and distractions as similar as you can. Record the result before judging which round “felt” sharper.

If the audio round feels wonderful but your predefined measure stays unchanged, that is useful information. If the score changes, you still cannot conclude that the sound caused it: tasks may differ, fatigue may fluctuate and practice may help. Alternating order on another day can reveal how unstable such impressions are. No need to crank up volume, repeat a session if uncomfortable or listen while driving.

For a rigorous scientific answer you would need an appropriately powered, blinded and controlled study. The exercise is **a lesson in separating sensation from measurement**, not treatment, diagnosis or proof of any product claim.

One practical lesson survives either result: you can build focus-supporting routines for free. A specific next-task note, a quiet window, fewer alerts, and a defined return point after interruptions are accessible before buying an audio library. The existing [Seven Day Focus Fieldbook](https://finalvestige.com/focus/) offers a structured resource without requiring a purchase.

## A better question before paying

The intellectually useful buyer question is not “does brainwave sound exist?” It does. Nor is it “have scientists ever found positive effects from auditory beats?” Some have. The useful question is narrower: **what reliable evidence establishes that this exact product produces the precise benefit I care about, under conditions resembling mine?**

Ask for the intervention method, exact product ingredients or protocol to the extent disclosed, relevant independent trial evidence, appropriate controls, meaningful effects, limits, contraindications, current price and refund conditions. Be especially wary of phrases implying that all brains respond identically or that a complicated cognitive skill can be switched on reliably by one download.

A person may still decide that guided listening is worthwhile as an enjoyable, convenient routine. That is a preference, not a medical claim. A seller can offer a genuine digital recording without establishing every attached promise. Our responsibility as an evidence-first publisher is to keep that boundary visible.
