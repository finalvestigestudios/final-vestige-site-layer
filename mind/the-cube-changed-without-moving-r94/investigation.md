# The Cube Changed Without Moving
*Final Vestige Studios — Mind & Perception / Perception & Attention | Independent investigation | Draft, November 3, 2026*

## The evidence did not move. The room did.

Place a line drawing of a wireframe cube on a page. Do not shade the faces. Do not animate the drawing. Look at its center long enough to decide which square is nearer to you. Look again. On some occasions the nearer square will seem to move back, and the rear square will seem to come forward. If the reversal comes, nothing outside the observer has changed: the same strokes occupy the same pixels. The apparent orientation has changed.

That is why the Necker cube deserves a place in an archive about evidence. The apparent object is vivid enough to describe, but the drawing does not specify one uniquely correct three-dimensional interpretation. The mind supplies a plausible organization for ambiguous information. It may then supply another. The illusion is not evidence that your mind can change external reality. It is a demonstration of the more subtle point that perception includes interpretation.

The phenomenon takes its name from Louis Albert Necker, who wrote from Geneva to the physicist Sir David Brewster in 1832. Necker's published letter discusses several optical observations and includes the apparent reversal of a crystal or geometrical drawing. The bibliographic record identifies the piece as appearing in the *Philosophical Magazine*, series 3, volume 1, pages 329–337. Later writers often compress that into "the Necker cube was discovered in 1832." What the record more securely supports is a documented early description of the reversible figure, which subsequently acquired his name. [N1, N2]

### A drawing with two defensible readings

Imagine two squares of equal size, offset and joined at their corners by diagonal lines. The picture is commonly read as a transparent solid. But the intersections do not tell you which edges are in front. We do not have the ordinary depth information that would settle the question in a physical object, such as consistent occlusion, motion parallax from changing viewpoint, or binocular disparities. A viewer can interpret one square as the near face; a moment later the same lines may support the opposite reading.

This difference matters because it separates *the stimulus*—a particular arrangement of marks—from *the percept*—the three-dimensional interpretation the observer experiences. An observer who honestly says "the cube flipped" may be reporting a genuine perceptual reversal, even though the ink never moved. That is not a lie, malfunction or mystical feat. It is a case where a stable ambiguous stimulus admits more than one percept.

The cube is also not a universal test with a guaranteed effect. Some readers switch almost immediately; others do not notice a switch at first; others may need to understand the two alternatives before perceiving both. Screen size, visual acuity, viewing conditions and familiarity can influence what happens. Do not attach a diagnosis, a personality type or a score for intelligence or mindfulness to the speed of reversal.

### Necker's question did not end in 1832

A surprisingly persistent scientific question is whether movements of the eye *cause* a perceptual switch, follow it, or participate in a loop. In 1978 Stephen Ellis and Lawrence Stark recorded eye movements while participants viewed variants of Necker cubes and signaled perceptual reversals. They reported fixation patterns associated with reversals, particularly around the externally appearing corner. Their observation was a clue, not a decisive demonstration that eye movement alone creates the phenomenon. [N3]

In 2004 Wolfgang Einhäuser, Kevan Martin and Peter König revisited the problem using precise eye-position measurements, less intrusive instructions and biased versions of the cube that could help identify the viewer's interpretation. Their results indicated a close association between eye position and perceptual switching, compatible with *bidirectional coupling*: a new interpretation changes where the eyes move, while subsequent eye position may influence the odds of the next reversal. [N4]

That is an important distinction. When two things change together, the direction of causation is not automatically determined. An instruction to watch the near corner might itself change attention. An eye movement measured close to the reported reversal might be its consequence, its contributor, or both. The 2004 study narrowed the possibilities through experimental design, but it did not establish a single universal mechanism for every ambiguous image.

### Why a determined observer can still lose the image

People can sometimes influence which interpretation persists. Anyone who has looked deliberately for one face of a reversible drawing knows the urge to "hold" it in place. But deliberate attention is not an absolute switch.

Thomas Toppino's 2022 research, *Reversible-figure perception: Why is voluntary control limited?*, tested the degree to which observers could keep a chosen interpretation of the Necker cube. In two experiments, participants demonstrated meaningful but limited control. Manipulations of cube size and completeness changed reversal frequencies even when participants tried to maintain a percept. The paper interpreted these patterns as consistent with a combination of higher-level intention and stimulus-driven processes. [N5]

In plain language: what you ask your attention to do matters, but it does not control everything that your perceptual system does. That is almost the opposite of the more extravagant slogan that sufficient concentration can make anything appear or happen. A better lesson is that attention operates within constraints.

Earlier work also offered evidence that reversible-figure perception could be influenced intentionally. A 1985 experiment by Robert Liebert and Bonnie Burk investigated voluntary control using reversible figures. It found evidence of control but did not imply perfect elimination of reversals. Differences among tasks and designs make simplistic comparisons unwise. [N6]

A line drawing offers an unusually clean way to watch the boundary between deliberate interpretation and involuntary change. It does not prove what happens during meditation, hypnosis, therapy or any commercial audio intervention.

### The unusual historical bridge: suggestion and a visible cube

A limited 1988 study offers an intriguing, but easily abused, bridge to the history of hypnosis. Barbara Wallace compared groups categorized as susceptible or resistant to hypnotic suggestion, fifty participants in each, and asked them to report Necker-cube apparent reversals with different geometric surrounds. The paper reported more reversals in the suggestion-susceptible group and fewer reports in the presence of surrounding shapes. [N7]

Those are reports made under particular laboratory conditions. They do not establish that a recording can train someone's susceptibility, that more reversals are better, that hypnosis improves vision, or that a Necker cube reveals a person's mental health. Without stronger controls and independent replication, a result about group differences should remain exactly that.

This brings us to an older archive. In the eighteenth century, the Marquis de Puységur's animal-magnetism accounts described striking experiences interpreted through theories of magnetic influence. Final Vestige's published *Puységur's Animal Magnetism Files* preserves the historical trail. The connection is methodological, not physiological: an unusual experience can be accurately observed while the explanation attached to it remains an open question. A cube that apparently reverses while the ink stays still gives modern readers a gentle illustration of why that distinction matters.

The historical documents must not be turned into proof of magnetic fluids. Neither should a perceptual-illusion study be treated as a controlled test of a meditation product. A careful archive refuses both shortcuts.

### Try it yourself: the evidence-room observation

Use the **original unshaded wireframe cube** supplied with the draft HTML. If no image renders, draw two equally sized offset squares and connect corresponding corners. There is no need for a moving animation, a timer, headphones or a signup form.

First, look at the middle of the drawing and write down which square feels nearest. Second, try to see the opposite arrangement; if you cannot, shift your attention among the corners, rather than straining. Third, look away and return to the page. Notice whether the first reading returns. Finally, inspect the edges themselves. Ask which lines literally moved. The answer is none.

The exercise is an informal observation, **not an experiment** establishing your attention span or suggestibility. It has no controlled comparison group, random assignment or standardized outcome. It cannot demonstrate a benefit from any audio course. Its useful result is a distinction you can carry into other investigations: mark down what happened in the stimulus, what you experienced, and what explanation you are proposing.

For readers using screen readers, the verbal description is the demonstration: two displaced squares connected at their four corresponding corners can be imagined with either square nearer. There is no requirement to see a switch or report a particular interpretation.

### The evidence ledger

**What is observed:** A stable ambiguous drawing can support alternating apparent depth interpretations. Experimental researchers have measured relationships among perceptual switches, eye movements, attention and stimulus geometry. [N3–N5]

**What is disputed or limited:** The relative contribution of eye position, adaptation and intentional control depends on methods and explanatory models. A particular experimental result does not settle the mechanism in all conditions. Historical publication dates and bibliographic metadata do not themselves establish what Necker personally saw at every moment. [N1–N5]

**What is not established:** That seeing frequent reversals indicates intelligence, spiritual development, self-hypnosis skill, a medical condition, or any benefit from Zen12, Hypnosis Live or Brainwave Shots. The sources in this investigation do not test those products.

For a second kind of evidence check, read Final Vestige's published investigation of Franklin's 1784 commission into animal magnetism. It offers a parallel lesson from deliberate experimental controls: one can take a witness's experience seriously without adopting the witness's causal theory.
