# The Face You Didn't Choose: The Experiment That Made People Defend a Swapped Decision
**Final Vestige Studios | Mind & Perception Archive | Original investigation | 7 November 2026 | UNPUBLISHED — no CMS write**

*You select a photograph. The experimenter hands you the other one. Then asks why you picked it. You begin explaining.*

## A choice with a missing link

Picture two portraits on a table. You compare expressions, turn the possibilities over for a moment and point to one. You think the decision is yours, and in the ordinary sense it is. But the next object you receive isn't quite the object you selected. Unknown to you, someone has switched the outcome.

Now comes the uncomfortable part: asked to explain the choice, you may describe the very features of the picture you had just rejected. You might even sound confident. The sentence is coherent. The explanation has the grammar of a memory. Its link to the original decision is the part that has gone missing.

In a 2005 paper in *Science*, Petter Johansson, Lars Hall, Sverker Sikström and Andreas Olsson used a concealed card-switching procedure to study exactly this mismatch. Participants compared pairs of faces for attractiveness. On manipulated trials, the selected image was replaced by the alternative before participants explained why they had made their selection. Some detected the substitution. Others did not and nonetheless supplied reasons for the presented result. The authors named this phenomenon **choice blindness**. [1]

It is tempting to pull an enormous lesson from that result: *nobody knows why they do anything*. That conclusion goes well beyond the evidence. The more useful finding is smaller and sharper. Under particular conditions, an explanation of a decision can be constructed around an apparent outcome that is not the outcome the person intended. A sincere account need not be an accurate record of the steps that preceded it.

This difference matters wherever a polished explanation stands in for evidence—including a buyer's explanation of why a promotional claim suddenly feels true.

## What the scientists changed

The original procedure had three separate moments. First came a choice between faces. Then came an experimenter's intervention that changed which face the participant was shown as the chosen one. Finally came an explanation: *Why this one?* The intervention is critical. It creates an observable distinction between the participant's recorded selection and the object presented for justification.

This wasn't a mind-reading experiment. Nor was it a demonstration that all preferences can be reversed. Some participants noticed. The experiment depends on its timing, choice format and concealment method. The crucial evidence is the mismatch between the previously visible choice and the subsequent report—not an investigator's speculation about an invisible thought.

The result also does not mean the justifications were knowingly false. A person can report a reason that feels immediately available without knowingly deceiving anyone. The scientific challenge is to distinguish accurate access to a decision process from a plausible story that fits the information currently in front of us.

Johansson and colleagues framed the finding as a problem for simple assumptions about introspection. Human beings often act, then answer questions about their actions. We might expect an internal alarm whenever the outcome of an action does not match its intention. The experiment showed that this alarm is not automatic in every setting. [1]

That is one reason the effect is fascinating rather than merely embarrassing: it concerns a useful, everyday cognitive skill—constructing reasons—and how that skill sometimes outruns the underlying evidence.

## From two faces to a supermarket counter

If the original result had only involved face photographs, we would still have a provocative experiment. In 2010, Hall, Johansson, Betty Tärning, Sikström and Thérèse Deutgen carried the approach into a supermarket. Visitors tasted or smelled competing varieties of jam and tea, selected a preferred sample and were then invited to sample their selection again and explain why they liked it.

At the decisive moment, the contents were secretly switched. Participants sometimes justified a preference for the alternative they had not actually chosen. The researchers reported that **no more than one third of manipulated trials were detected** overall. With particularly distinct flavors or scents, detection was higher but still incomplete. [2]

The supermarket matters for two reasons. First, it moves the manipulation from portraits on cards to the taste and smell of familiar consumer goods. Second, it reveals how easily an explanation can attach itself to what is presented as an already-made choice. A statement about a flavor can be vivid, specific and earnest while referring to the wrong selection history.

But we must not pretend that this study measures every kind of purchasing decision. The task was immediate sensory preference in a designed field experiment; it was not a months-long financial decision, an actual digital-audio purchase or a study of affiliate marketing. Nor does it tell us what a person would do if given a written receipt, a side-by-side comparison or a chance to correct the record.

The proper takeaway is not that shoppers are helpless. It is that a buyer's post-hoc story is a different kind of evidence from a record of what the buyer selected and what actually arrived.

## A paper survey that changed its answers

The next experiment is more troubling because the stakes feel different. In 2012, Hall, Johansson and Thomas Strandberg published a study in *PLOS ONE* using a self-transforming paper survey of moral and political attitudes. A paper-handling manipulation exposed some participants to responses that reversed their earlier stated positions. Researchers then asked participants to account for the resulting answers.

The published abstract reports that **69% of participants failed to detect at least one of two reversals**. Participants sometimes offered arguments supporting the apparent position that contradicted their initial answer. [3] This is a study-specific statistic, not a percentage of the public that can be persuaded of anything.

Here the distinction between perception and interpretation grows important. In the paper survey, a concealed slip was removed to reveal wording with the opposite meaning while the participant's written rating stayed in place; the manipulation changed what that rating appeared to endorse, not the location of the respondent's mark. A respondent might remember the underlying issue quite well, yet take the transformed printed statement and unchanged mark as a trustworthy reminder of what they had answered. Once that displayed outcome is accepted as a premise, the search for reasons can begin in the wrong place.

These researchers suggest flexibility and retrospective justification in expressed attitudes. They did not show that every political belief is weak, that debate never changes minds, or that moral views can be rewritten at will. The survey involved particular participants, questions and manipulations. Its scope should stay attached to those methods.

Still, the sequence across three papers is striking: a swapped portrait, a swapped taste, a swapped written position. In each case the investigators separated what was initially chosen from what later appeared to be the chosen outcome.

## Why a sensible explanation can be the wrong evidence

An explanation can have at least three ingredients: a remembered decision, observations about the object now present, and assumptions about what sort of reason would justify a choice like this. Those ingredients do not always arrive with provenance labels.

Suppose someone is given the wrong photograph and asked what they liked about it. The face in hand supplies real visual features. The interviewer supplies the premise that this is the selected image. The participant supplies language that makes the premise feel coherent. Nothing in the fluency of the answer proves that the premise was correct.

This should make us careful with **confidence**. Confidence may be valuable when paired with records, but it isn't itself a duplicate of the original record. The experiments do not establish that a confident person is usually mistaken. They demonstrate a situation where confidence and accuracy can diverge.

Another useful distinction is between **a choice** and **a retrospective account of a choice**. Buying a short audio program, for example, may be a perfectly reasonable decision for someone who wants a convenient, structured listening routine. But after encountering repeated claims about concentration, brainwaves or effortless meditation, a buyer could easily remember their reason for purchase as more scientific than it was. That is an illustrative possibility, not an observed effect in these experiments or in any specific Inspire3 customers.

The protection isn't to stop making decisions. It is to keep the supporting evidence visible long enough that a later story can be checked against it.

## A historical echo, not a proof of hypnosis

Final Vestige's published **[Puységur's Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/)** examine a different age's efforts to make sense of suggestion, testimony and unusual behavior. Accounts of magnetic somnambulism repeatedly raise questions about how observers understood the person being observed—and how the expectations of the investigator shaped the questions asked. The archive is a historical source trail, not evidence that magnetic cures worked.

Modern choice-blindness experiments offer a different tool. Here investigators specify a testable mismatch, manipulate it deliberately and measure whether participants notice. The analogy is methodological: in both archives and laboratories, the question is *which part is a direct observation, and which part is a narrative built afterward?* It would be an error to claim that the choice-blindness work proves eighteenth-century theories of animal magnetism or validates a modern hypnosis recording.

The companion published account of **[Mesmerism](https://finalvestige.com/2026/11/02/mesmerism/)** supplies historical context for a practice once surrounded by extraordinary assertions. If the history teaches anything useful for today's product reader, it is to distinguish testimony, method and reproducible results before accepting a grand explanation.

## An honest experiment you can run without tricking anyone

You do not need to swap another person's answers to learn something useful. Try a **decision-receipt exercise** with two genuinely safe, low-stakes options: two articles to read, two music tracks you already own, or two free ways of structuring a twelve-minute quiet break.

Before choosing, write down both options, which one you selected and *two concrete reasons*. Put the note somewhere you can find it tomorrow. Use the option as planned, then write a fresh explanation without consulting the note. Finally compare the two accounts.

Did your reasons change? Did you emphasize a feature you never mentioned at the outset? Did new experience legitimately change your view? None of these differences by itself proves choice blindness: the exercise does **not** include the covert outcome substitution that defines the laboratory method, and it is not a validated cognitive test. It is simply a provenance habit. It distinguishes *what I intended at the time* from *what I now say about that decision*.

For a buyer considering a paid recording, use the same habit. Before visiting checkout, write a one-line reason: “I want a convenient library of twelve-minute sessions,” or “I need credible proof that this product improves my attention.” Those are very different purchase criteria. If your desired outcome is medical or psychological treatment, the evidence required is much stronger than a seller's promise.

There is no need to disclose private thoughts or health information to complete this exercise. The record can stay entirely offline.

## What this research says—and what it does not

**Supported:** the 2005 manipulated face-choice experiment demonstrated failures to detect some intention/outcome mismatches, sometimes followed by explanations; the 2010 supermarket experiment observed incomplete detection of swapped choices for taste and smell; the 2012 altered-survey experiment found undetected reversals and subsequent justifications under its conditions. [1–3]

**Not established:** that most ordinary purchases are involuntary; that every memory is fabricated; that all preferences are unstable; that any named seller's meditation or hypnosis file improves self-awareness; that a buyer can eliminate choice blindness by listening to a brainwave recording.

A recent marketing script may invoke a real psychological term and still provide no product-specific trial. The FTC's health-products guidance makes clear that objective health-related benefits require appropriately reliable scientific substantiation. It is not enough to borrow an effect demonstrated with face photographs and apply it to a commercial audio program. [4]

## The last card on the table

The most memorable object in the original experiment was not the photograph. It was the gap between a decision and the account that followed it.

We spend much of life explaining our actions, and often those explanations are useful. But a good explanation can be too well matched to the scene in front of us. When the scene has changed, fluency may hide the substitution.

A careful investigator returns to the original selection. A careful historian returns to the document. A careful consumer returns to the actual offer, its independent evidence and the terms at checkout. The point is not to become suspicious of every choice. It is to know which part of a story can still be checked.
