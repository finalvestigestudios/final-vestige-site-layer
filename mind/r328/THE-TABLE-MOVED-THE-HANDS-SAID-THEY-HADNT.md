# The Table Moved. The Hands Said They Hadn't.

**Mind & Perception Archive | Original investigation | UNPUBLISHED REVIEW DRAFT | 12 November 2026**

*What Michael Faraday measured in 1853, what a twenty-first-century motion-capture study found, and why feeling that an action was involuntary is not the same thing as knowing what caused it.*

## The hand on the table

In the middle of the nineteenth century, people gathered around tables and waited for something to happen. Their hands rested on the furniture. A movement, when it came, could be experienced as an event delivered by the table rather than produced by the people touching it. Several participants could quite sincerely insist that they were following an object, not pushing it. The most striking feature of this account is not that a table could move. A table is a physical object, and pressure can move it. It is that an ordinary physical movement could arrive with the *experience* of somebody else's agency.

In 1853, the chemist and experimentalist Michael Faraday addressed just such testimony. He was not content to dismiss participants as dishonest. In his published report, **Experimental Investigation of Table-Moving**, he explicitly described the table-turners with whom he had worked as sincere and capable. He asked a more demanding question: if the people sincerely felt that the table was moving their hands, could an instrument resolve which object started moving first? [F1]

This is the question that turns an entertaining mystery into an investigation. The experience was data. It was not an adequate explanation of the mechanism.

## Faraday's indicator

Faraday tried layers of materials placed between hands and table. His interest was in the direction of pressure and the order of movement. He devised an indicator in which small movements could shift a visible pointer. When people believed they were responding to the table's motion, the arrangement could reveal motion originating from their hands instead. In other words, his experiment was engineered to make a subtle causal sequence visible rather than asking witnesses to inspect their own feelings more carefully. [F1]

The crucial distinction is between *not intending to push* and *not physically pushing*. Those are different propositions. A person can perform a small action without deliberating about its timing, force or direction. If several people make small movements in the same situation, the common effect can be quite noticeable, even when the contributions are hard to feel individually.

Faraday did not thereby provide a complete neuroscience of unconscious movement. Modern researchers would be cautious about using a nineteenth-century apparatus to prove every mechanism of contemporary agency perception. What he did establish in his test was more modest, and more durable: **a genuine subjective experience of an external mover need not identify the source of the movement correctly**.

The claim also has a historical limitation. Faraday was investigating table-moving, not every claimed magnetic cure, hypnotic response, or spiritual encounter. Treating his results as a single universal refutation of every unusual experience would repeat the very error his apparatus was designed to avoid: deciding the answer before testing the particular claim.

## An older pendulum, revisited

The hand-held pendulum presents a quieter version of the same problem. Suspend a small weight from a string, keep the fingers apparently still, and picture motion in a particular direction. The suspended weight may begin to oscillate while the holder experiences little or no deliberate movement. The phenomenon is associated with the nineteenth-century chemist Michel Eugène Chevreul and is often described as the **Chevreul pendulum illusion**.

It needs neither a paranormal force nor a fictional participant to be interesting. The motion can be real. The misattribution can also be real. The holder's body is part of the mechanical system, yet the output can feel autonomous.

In 1975, Randolph Easton and Ronald Shor studied this effect experimentally. Their abstract describes photographic measurement and visual and auditory imagery prompts. Pendulum motion was enhanced when participants could see the movement and when spatially oscillating cues were present; in that study, visually presented spatial cues were stronger than auditory cues. [F2]

That is a result about the tested pendulum task and its conditions. It is not evidence that hearing a commercial sound recording can reorganize brainwaves or produce clinical benefits. It does show why expectations, sensation and movement can form a feedback loop: a little movement becomes visible, and what becomes visible can influence subsequent movement.

## The measurement that arrived much later

The subject did not remain in the nineteenth century. In a 2021 peer-reviewed paper in *Human Movement Science*, Débora Cantergi, Bhuvanesh Awasthi and Jason Friedman used motion capture to examine how subtle hand and finger movements produce larger pendulum oscillations. Their research linked success with small movements close to the pendulum's resonant frequency. They found that a 40-centimeter pendulum was more conducive to the tested illusion than an 80-centimeter one, and that participants who moved their fingers more tended to be more successful. [F3]

This is a surprisingly concrete result. The imagined difference between a stationary hand and a moving pendulum is not necessarily a difference between stillness and motion. It can be a difference in **scale**: the driving movement is so small that it is not noticed, while the pendulum makes it visible.

But this result must not be made to say too much. The investigators measured motion and its mechanical relationships. They did not measure a product like Zen12, did not test whether a listener could obtain a specific psychological outcome, and did not show that suggestions can reliably override another person's will.

The same caution applies to simple at-home demonstrations. The position of the fingers, fatigue, string length, airflow and anticipation can change the result. A pendulum that barely moves is not a failed person; a pendulum that swings is not a detector of hidden truth.

## The board that appeared to know

A related experiment carried the question of agency into a social setting. In 2018, Marc Andersen and colleagues published a field study involving participants at a paranormal conference using a Ouija board. Eye-tracking data showed that participants were less likely to visually anticipate individual letters during a Ouija session than when deliberately spelling words. The authors also described a collective pattern: meaningful responses emerged at the level of interacting pairs rather than as evidence of an external communicator. [F4]

The paper appeared online in 2018 and in the journal's 2019 volume. This dating matters when following the record. So does the field setting: participants were enthusiasts at a paranormal conference, not a representative sample of all people, and the task was not a medical or therapeutic trial.

Why is this a good piece of perception research? Because it separates three things often mixed together: where a physical pointer goes, what people predict it will do, and who they believe is responsible. Those variables can disagree. The board can move, the meaning can be socially assembled, and the movement can feel not entirely self-authored. Nothing in that sequence requires labeling participants either gullible or dishonest.

Researchers of the **sense of agency**—the experience of causing one's actions—have proposed multiple mechanisms for such discrepancies. Some accounts emphasize predictions of sensory consequences; others emphasize retrospective interpretation and situational cues. A 2018 scholarly review surveys these approaches and notes that conclusions extending from laboratory tasks to the nature of conscious will remain contested. [F5]

That last caveat is the difference between an investigation and a slogan. “People can misjudge the authorship of an action” is a defensible finding in relevant conditions. “Nobody ever chooses anything” is not what the pendulum study demonstrated.

## The historical echo in Puységur's files

There is a reason this belongs inside Final Vestige's historical archive rather than in an undifferentiated collection of psychological curiosities.

In the [published Puységur Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/), the central editorial question is not merely whether extraordinary things were reported. The archive follows records made to substantiate those reports: witnesses, remembered conversations, failures, written predictions, and a claimant's attempt to assemble evidence. It preserves counterexamples rather than quietly discarding them.

Faraday's response to table-moving offers a companion case in experimental method. The people observing the event were witnesses to the motion. They were not necessarily qualified instruments for identifying the force. Their sincerity did not settle causation. A later observer's confidence in an alternative explanation would not settle it either without appropriate evidence.

There is a further boundary. Mesmerism, magnetic somnambulism, suggestion, ideomotor behavior and modern hypnosis have overlapping histories and vocabulary, but they are not interchangeable mechanisms. An ideomotor explanation for a pendulum cannot retrospectively prove precisely what happened in any individual Puységur case. Nor may a seller invoke this body of history as support for a modern audio product without a relevant test of that product.

## Try a quiet, non-diagnostic observation

You do not have to reproduce a séance to see why measurement matters. Take an ordinary small washer tied to a short piece of string, if it is safe for you to handle, and sit at a stable table with the forearm supported. Keep the weight away from other people and breakable objects. If handling small suspended objects is difficult or uncomfortable, skip this exercise.

Before attempting anything, write down what you expect to happen. Then allow the weight to hang, notice whether it moves, and look for tiny changes in finger position. Try a second observation with different visual attention. Record what you actually noticed, including no movement. Do not infer anything about intelligence, suggestibility, diagnosis or a hidden message from the outcome.

A more controlled version would use an external camera and multiple conditions, but the point here is not to claim that a casual demonstration proves a scientific law. It is to notice how quickly observation becomes interpretation, and to put a little distance between the two.

The useful question is not **“Could my mind move an object?”** Your body is already capable of moving things. The better question is **“How much movement might be occurring before I notice I am producing it?”**

That last question has an honest commercial-adjacent application, but a very limited one. A reader interested in short meditation sessions or self-hypnosis may be curious about mental imagery, attention and the feeling of effort. Those interests are coherent. They do not establish that a specific paid recording reliably improves concentration, treats anxiety, changes mental health, or produces any promised brain state.

## What remains on the table

There is an irony in Faraday's experiment. The men and women around the table did not need to lie in order to be wrong about how it moved. The investigation became stronger precisely because it took their testimony seriously enough to test it.

The modern motion-capture result does something similar. It does not drain the subject of wonder. It makes a largely unnoticed movement visible. A finger shifts. A suspended weight swings. An apparently external intention acquires a mechanical history.

The most durable lesson of the moving table may be about a different kind of movement: the leap from a vivid experience to a confident conclusion. If the witness can be honest and mistaken, then the burden of proof falls on the experiment, the record and the instrument—not on how remarkable the experience feels.
