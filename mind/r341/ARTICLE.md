# The Frequency That Did Not Prove Focus
### What fourteen EEG studies tell us—and what they do not—about buying brainwave audio

*Final Vestige Studios · Independent Mind & Perception editorial draft · 13 November 2026 · UNPUBLISHED*

A few seconds into a piece of focus audio, there is nothing to see. No task completed, no changed memory, no independent measure of concentration. Only a sound. Yet on the page selling the recording, a physiological explanation may already have been supplied: particular pulses are said to guide the brain into the electrical pattern of attention. The explanation is attractive because it seems to skip the hard part of focusing. If a frequency can be delivered through headphones, perhaps focus can be delivered with it.

That sentence contains three different hypotheses. First, a listener hears—or their nervous system responds to—a patterned stimulus. Second, the pattern alters brain electrical activity in a relevant, measurable way. Third, a change in that activity improves a real-world outcome, such as finishing a difficult assignment or noticing a signal. **The third is not established by the first.** Nor is the second guaranteed merely because a product has a frequency printed on its packaging.

The distinction matters for Final Vestige because this is precisely the kind of chain of evidence we interrogate in archival investigations: a record of an observation is not automatically evidence for the mechanism that its author proposes. We apply the same rule to scientific-sounding commercial copy.

## A sound in the room is not a signal in the cortex

In a conventional binaural-beat demonstration, one tone is delivered to each ear at slightly different frequencies. A listener may perceive a beating sensation corresponding to the difference. The stimulus is real; the perception has been studied. But the perception alone does not demonstrate that widespread cortical oscillations lock reliably to that difference, much less that the listener becomes more productive.

The key published synthesis here is Ingendoh, Posny and Heine's 2023 *PLOS ONE* systematic review. They selected fourteen studies investigating EEG responses to binaural-beat stimulation. **Five** reported results consistent with their entrainment hypothesis, **eight** reported contradictory outcomes, and **one** reported mixed results. Their conclusion was not that binaural beats never work. It was that the heterogeneity of procedures and measures does not permit a confident general verdict about EEG entrainment. [Primary review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10198548/).

That is a more useful finding for a prospective buyer than either blanket promotion or dismissal. Even a perfectly replicated EEG change would still require a separate, appropriately controlled experiment to demonstrate a benefit on the buyer's actual task. "The brain responded" and "the person got meaningful work done" are not interchangeable endpoints.

A product-specific claim requires an even longer evidentiary bridge. The participants must have used the product or a genuinely equivalent tested protocol. A study using a different audio mix, headphones, duration, baseline population or outcome cannot be sold as proof that a named recording delivers its advertised result.

## Two clocks, neither of them a receipt

Zen12 markets a twelve-minute structured audio meditation program. Its official sales page says that one twelve-minute session provides the benefit of an hour of advanced meditation. That is a **seller assertion**, not an independently verified conversion ratio between different practices. The official page also describes three packages: Beginner covers the first six levels with 24 MP3s, Premium all twelve with 48, and Advanced the final six with 24. Package contents are a checkable description, whereas promised cognitive and health outcomes demand separate support. [Official package page](https://zen12.com/).

Now consider a legitimate scientific question about duration. Palmer, Roos, Vafaie and Kober randomized 372 remotely recruited adults among ten-minute mindfulness, twenty-minute mindfulness and matching recorded-control conditions. They assessed short-term changes in state mindfulness and affect. In their 2023 report, the ten- and twenty-minute mindfulness conditions did not differ on their main comparison, while an exploratory moderator suggested a difference in state anxiety for certain participants. [Study abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38001316/).

That is neither twelve minutes nor an hour; neither arm tested Zen12; the outcome was not sustained work performance; the sample and method impose limits. "No measured difference between ten and twenty minutes in this study" is not "twelve minutes equals sixty." Absence of a detected difference under a particular protocol is not proof that two interventions are interchangeable everywhere.

A person can still rationally choose a twelve-minute routine. A short, repeatable window may fit their day. They may like guided structure or background sound. Those are practical preferences to test, not medical promises.

## The other audio, and a different proposed mechanism

Inspire3's related brands describe distinct products that should not be collapsed into one vague category of "brain optimization." Hypnosis Live's **Total Focus** is a 33-minute, 47 MB guided hypnosis recording credited to J. A. Amos on the seller's page. Brainwave Shots' **Focus Shot** includes fifteen- and thirty-minute versions, plus additional brainwave-only versions and a short video. These are seller-described formats and may change. The websites also make stronger performance claims that we do not endorse.

Hypnosis and suggestion are not identical to entrainment. In a 2002 experiment, Raz and colleagues studied highly and less hypnotizable participants doing a Stroop color-word task. Among the highly suggestible participants, a posthypnotic suggestion to treat words differently reduced the usual interference; the less suggestible comparison group did not show the same significant effect. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12470132/).

In a 2006 paper, Raz, Kirsch and colleagues reported an important nuance: a suggestion to perceive words as meaningless symbols reduced interference in highly suggestible people whether or not it was accompanied by a hypnotic induction. [Primary abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16466414/). Those results complicate simplistic stories in which a special trance is the only active ingredient. They do not establish that a prerecorded commercial session makes an average listener more productive, and they do not validate proprietary marketing about "reprogramming" the brain.

Hypnosis research is not a license to disregard personal agency. A person can decide whether to participate, stop a recording or seek professional assistance where appropriate. No consumer audio should replace clinical assessment or treatment.

## The historical witness who made the wrong leap

The same logic is visible in our published [Puységur Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/). An eighteenth-century operator documented cases, predictions, witnesses, signed statements and failures. Some of those records are detailed enough to reconstruct what was said or done. None, by virtue of being detailed or notarized, establishes the proposed physical cause called animal magnetism.

The archive also preserves a darker problem: patients who asked to be left alone were sometimes overridden, and a system of obedience could be mistaken for reliable evidence. The research is not included here as a neat origin story for modern meditation or hypnosis. It is a warning against turning authority and expectation into proof. What the operator wanted the record to mean is not necessarily what the record proves.

The analogy with consumer focus audio has limits. Modern recordings are not historical medical procedures; our point is about inference, not equivalence of harms or technologies. If a seller supplies a persuasive scientific mechanism, it remains the buyer's job to ask what independently observed outcome connects that mechanism to their task.

## A twelve-minute test that costs nothing

You cannot reproduce a controlled EEG trial at home. You can make a more grounded purchasing decision by identifying what you are actually trying to change.

Choose one safe, ordinary task—sorting a short reading list, drafting a paragraph, or organizing five notes. Before starting, write the smallest observable output: "one paragraph drafted," "five labels applied," or "three questions answered." Silence notifications. Set a twelve-minute timer. Choose either ordinary silence or a neutral familiar background sound, not a seller's unverified brainwave protocol. Begin.

When the timer ends, record whether you produced the defined output, whether you switched away, and whether the session felt comfortable. Do not score yourself with imaginary neurological metrics. Repeat on another day, ideally with tasks of comparable difficulty, and note the differences. Expect noise: your energy, sleep, task novelty and mood can matter.

This is an **informal personal observation**, not a blinded experiment and not a demonstration that a product succeeds or fails. It measures whether a short working window fits *you*. If you later choose to try a commercial audio product, keep the same practical measures rather than using a vague feeling of mental transformation as the sole result.

## The sales-page claim ladder

Before paying, separate four kinds of statement.

**What is included?** Number and duration of recordings, supported formats, access model, requirements, support and documented returns terms. This information can generally be checked against the exact official offer page.

**What does the seller claim?** Promises of dramatic focus, immediate changes, hour-equivalence, brainwave synchronization or broader health improvement. These should be labeled as merchant advertising, not silently imported into editorial conclusions.

**What does independent research address?** A particular experimental stimulus, group, time frame and outcome. It may inform plausibility or limits without proving a branded product's results.

**What is not known?** Whether this exact recording works for a particular buyer, whether a promotional price appears at checkout, whether a refund condition applies to their order, and whether a publisher has legal authorization to earn a commission through a particular link.

On November 13, 2026, the public Zen12 page advertised a one-year refund promise; Hypnosis Live's Total Focus page advertised a fourteen-day satisfaction refund; Focus Shot displayed a one-year promise. These are **merchant displays observed on that date**, not a contract authenticated at checkout or a prediction that a refund will always be granted. Because product tiers and merchant cart terms can change, this draft does not publish a guaranteed price or promise of no financial risk.

## What remains useful even if you never buy

The most useful question is not "Can a sound change a brain?" Of course sensory stimulation interacts with the nervous system. The question is: **what, in this study, was actually measured—and is that the outcome the seller has promised me?**

A person who understands that difference is harder to mislead by an impressive frequency chart, a testimonial or a paper cited for a different result. They are also better equipped to notice when a product offers something modest but real: a structured experience they may enjoy and use, without expecting it to act as a neuroscientific shortcut.
