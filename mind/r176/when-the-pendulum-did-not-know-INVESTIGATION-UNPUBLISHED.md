# When the Pendulum Didn't Know: The Replication That Complicated a Famous Experiment

Evidence reviewed November 6, 2026. This investigation is an editorial work, not a purchase recommendation.

The pendulum offers a perfect little theater. A hand rests on a table; a weight sways beneath a string; the person holding it insists they did not choose the direction. What moved it? That is one question. Did it answer correctly? That is another. And if an experimental result seems to suggest that it knows what its holder cannot consciously recall, there is a third: will that result survive a different laboratory?

In 2012, a remarkable research paper appeared to give the ideomotor phenomenon something beyond movement: an information advantage. People who thought they were guessing at yes-or-no facts answered more accurately when using a Ouija board than when answering deliberately. Ten years later, a closer comparison using a pendulum did not find that advantage. Between those two results lies an instructive account of the difficulty of turning subjective experiences into reliable knowledge—and the ease with which a narrow effect can become a sweeping claim.

The point of this story is neither to mock the people who experience an uncanny motion nor to sell them a way to reproduce it. It is to ask what an answer can demonstrate once the familiar feeling of certainty is stripped away.

## Before the answer: movement without a strong feeling of agency

In the scientific literature, *ideomotor action* describes behavior influenced by an idea or anticipated consequence, often with limited awareness of the contributing movement. It is not necessarily fraudulent or wholly voluntary in the way a consciously raised hand is voluntary. Nor does the term by itself establish exactly which muscles, expectations or surrounding disturbances produced any one swing.

Imagine holding a light object on a string. Small movements can accumulate into a pronounced arc. A person noticing that arc may be surprised, even when their own hand supplies most of the movement. Their surprise is a valid report of experience; it does not tell us whether the emerging answer is correct.

That distinction haunted nineteenth-century debates over automatic writing, animal magnetism and hypnotic suggestion. The surviving records gathered in Final Vestige's [Puységur Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) show why apparently unbidden conduct could make a witness seem unusually authoritative. Historical testimony records what observers asserted. It does not, on its own, verify the proposed mechanism of magnetic somnambulism or establish the accuracy of a subject's pronouncements.

Before modern experiments, pendulums had already moved between physics, divination, medical fashion and demonstrations of suggestion. Each domain brought a different answer to the same question: if motion surprises the mover, whose information is it?

## 2012: the unexpected result

Hélène Gauchou, Ronald Rensink and Sidney Fels asked a testable version of that question in [*Expression of nonconscious knowledge via ideomotor actions*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22377138/), published in *Consciousness and Cognition* in 2012.

Participants first answered factual yes-or-no questions on a computer, recording whether they believed they knew or guessed each answer. They later answered selected questions using a Ouija-style planchette, while a person pretending to be another participant did not actually move it. In the experiment, the other person's role concealed that movement came from the participant's own hands.

Twenty-seven university students took part; six were excluded for motor-response issues, leaving twenty-one for the main analysis. The sample size matters. A study can be innovative and carefully described while still being too small to support sweeping claims about all people.

The eye-catching figure came from questions participants believed they were guessing: roughly **65% correct** with the ideomotor response, compared with **50%** for the ordinary response. The paper also examined other comparisons and proposed that some semantic information might be expressed by ideomotor action despite low subjective confidence.

But look closely at what was and was not being tested. These were general-knowledge questions with independently checkable answers. A university sample was measured under experimental constraints. There was no divination, treatment, memory-recovery service, meditation audio or test of whether any person could gain financially by trusting an involuntary-looking movement.

The experiment did not ask if the planchette *always* knew. It asked if a difference emerged, on average, under carefully selected conditions. A group-level finding is not a decision tool for an individual with a health concern, a relationship conflict or an investment choice.

Source trail: [Gauchou et al., bibliographic record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22377138/) and [author-hosted full study](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/~rensink/publications/download/Ouija-GRF.pdf). The latter distinguishes participants recruited from those analyzed.

## 2017: a pendulum changes the decision, not the truth

Jay Olson, Ewalina Jeyanesan and Amir Raz examined a related but different question in [*Ask the pendulum: personality predictors of ideomotor performance*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5858027/), published in 2017.

Instead of asking general-knowledge questions, they asked people to detect briefly presented visual targets. They compared verbal answers with responses made by mentally asking a hand-held pendulum whether the target had appeared.

Study 1 recruited eighty undergraduates; **sixty-three remained for the analysis** after exclusions. This distinction is important: eighty was the recruitment count, not the denominator behind the reported principal results. Participants detected targets with approximately 57% accuracy verbally and 53% with the pendulum. Those modest results were not evidence that the pendulum answered more accurately.

The researchers did, however, observe a difference in *response bias*. Verbal responses more often leaned toward saying a target had not appeared. Pendulum responses were less biased in that particular yes/no sense. Bias and accuracy are not synonyms. If a coin-flip rule produces an even balance of yes and no, the balance alone does not prove the answers are correct.

Study 2 increased the task difficulty. Forty additional participants were recruited and **thirty-four analyzed**; accuracy hovered around chance under both response methods, while the response-bias difference appeared again. Neither experiment provided a broad accuracy advantage for the pendulum.

Nor was this a clean replication of 2012. A briefly flashed letter and a known-or-guessed fact use different cognitive resources, different timing and different answer conditions. It would be wrong to claim that the 2017 paper disproved every interpretation of the 2012 finding. It would be equally wrong to cite the 2012 result as if the later visual-detection work had confirmed a special information channel.

What 2017 did expose was another tempting inference: someone can change *how* they choose yes or no without changing whether the choice matches reality.

## 2022: a closer question, a different result

The next useful turn came in February 2022, when Gyorgy Moga and Zoltan Dienes published [*Expressing unconscious general knowledge using Chevreul's pendulum*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35184700/) in the *American Journal of Clinical Hypnosis*.

This research returned to the territory of general-knowledge questions rather than another visual-detection task. The researchers adapted the earlier approach but substituted a pendulum for the Ouija board and introduced some additional response-confidence distinctions. It is a closer conceptual and procedural test of the information claim—but not an exact reproduction.

Twenty-eight participants were recruited; one was excluded because the researchers could not elicit the required ideomotor response, leaving **twenty-seven analyzed**. They answered general-knowledge questions, classified their certainty, then used a pendulum for a selection of items.

The key result was direct: when answers had been rated as guesses, **pendulum and verbal responses were similarly accurate and near chance**. The result did not reproduce the celebrated 65%-versus-50% advantage under the altered method. The paper's message was not that movement can never express information; it was that an ideomotor advantage should not be assumed from the fact of movement or from the earlier striking result.

The full [publisher article](https://doi.org/10.1080/00029157.2021.2010641) also reports the specific statistical test for guessed answers: t(26) = 0.39, p = .70. That p value is not a universal proof of no effect; it helps describe what this experiment observed. The authors also discussed a Bayes-factor analysis, with evidence near rather than decisively beyond a conventional threshold for supporting no advantage.

Differences in the response device, question-selection procedure, awareness and confidence scales limit conclusions about which component accounts for the changed result. A skeptical reader should not choose one study as unquestionable truth and discard the others. Better to ask what common finding survives across different designs—and what remains conditional.

Across the studies, one pattern is much safer than the supernatural conclusion: people can make genuine, sometimes surprising ideomotor movements, but *whether those movements reveal accurate information is an empirical matter*, not a property guaranteed by the experience.

## The seductive leap from “different response” to “better knowledge”

Consider a popular storytelling shortcut:

A researcher shows that a pendulum can move without a felt decision. Another researcher reports a narrow advantage on some guesses. A social clip compresses them into a secret: “Your unconscious knows more than your thinking mind.” Finally, a product page suggests that an audio recording can unlock that knowledge on demand.

Every transition adds a claim. And the final claim is not established by the original experiments.

The 2012 paper did not test commercial meditation recordings. Neither did the 2017 visual search or the 2022 general-knowledge comparison. Those studies do not establish that a product improves memory, delivers medical benefit, makes hidden truths available or improves financial decisions.

Even if future work replicates an ideomotor information advantage, marketers would still need independent evidence tying a *specific product* to a *specified outcome* under appropriate conditions. Scientific curiosity does not transfer automatically to product efficacy.

## A demonstration that cannot answer a question for you

There is a useful, limited thing a reader can do without purchasing anything.

Place a light object on a short string, rest your forearm on a steady surface, and hold it above an empty tabletop. Imagine a slow horizontal swing for twenty seconds, then a small circle for twenty seconds. Observe whether the motion changes. Stop at any point. This demonstration is not required and should be skipped if uncomfortable.

Before drawing a conclusion, think like a researcher: how steady was your hand? Was there air movement? Did you hold the string the same way? Would an observer who did not know the imagined direction have scored the motion consistently? Would the result recur across many randomized attempts? None of these questions can be resolved by one intuitive experience.

Do **not** ask a pendulum to identify a diagnosis, evaluate consent, authenticate a memory, judge someone's honesty or choose a financial action. The exercise does not test hidden knowledge. It simply helps expose the difference between movement and the story attached to it.

A useful alternative thought experiment requires no object at all. Write down two columns: “What I experienced” and “What I can independently check.” For the pendulum, the first might say, “The direction surprised me.” The second might say, “A camera could record the swing.” There is no need to erase the surprise; just do not mistake it for a verified answer.

## How the historical archive changes the stakes

In the Puységur files, a witness's posture, state of consciousness or apparent obedience could be regarded as evidence that a new form of truth-telling was taking place. Modern experiments remind us why that inference requires caution. A person may sincerely lack awareness of having contributed to a movement and still supply no more accurate information than an ordinary guess.

There is a second historical lesson: *consent and authority matter*. Claims of unusual suggestibility do not make testimony automatically more reliable or another person's agency irrelevant. The proper response to an extraordinary narrative about a subject's obedience is not fascination alone, but scrutiny of what the record actually documents.

The modern experiments supply neither a retroactive refutation of every historical account nor validation of the mechanisms their narrators believed. What they offer is a sharper standard for reading the record: observe a behavior, ask how its cause was determined, and check whether the claimed answer had an independent verification path.

That standard works beyond pendulums. Change blindness, visual illusions, confidence errors and attentional blink each demonstrate that internal certainty can diverge from what is objectively available. They do not show that perception is useless; they show why good records include procedures and counterchecks.

## What we can responsibly conclude

The 2012 result is genuinely interesting: among a small analyzed sample and specifically guessed general-knowledge answers, an advantage appeared under a Ouija procedure. The 2017 study used a different task and found lower answer bias with pendulum responses, not greater detection accuracy. The 2022 study used a closer general-knowledge task with a pendulum and found no corresponding guessed-answer advantage.

Taken together, those papers call for careful replication and sharper explanations, not either reflexive dismissal or promotional certainty. Small samples, participant exclusions, apparatus changes and task design all limit generalization. There is no basis for using these experiments as a guarantee about hypnosis, meditation or a purchasable audio file.
