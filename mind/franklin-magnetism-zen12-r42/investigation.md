# The Tree That Had Never Been Magnetized
## Franklin's orchard, a blindfold, and the problem of believing an effect explains its cause
**Final Vestige Studios | Mind & Perception / History of Suggestion | Unpublished editorial draft, 1 November 2026**

In the garden of a house at Passy, outside Paris, a physician prepared an apricot tree. A boy of about twelve waited indoors. He was supposed to be unusually receptive to a force that, according to its champions, moved invisibly through living bodies.

The physician, Charles Deslon, had selected the boy himself. Deslon was a practitioner of animal magnetism, the controversial system associated with Franz Anton Mesmer. The visiting commissioners did not ask the boy to make an abstract judgment about the existence of an invisible fluid. They arranged for him to walk, blindfolded, to trees and describe what happened to him. This was not a theatrical flourish. It was the essential feature of the test. [1]

At the first tree the boy reported perspiration, coughing and pain in his head. At the second, his discomfort grew. At the third, he believed he was approaching the prepared tree. At the fourth, his body stiffened and he fainted. The physician attended him. None of the four trees the boy had embraced was the apricot tree Deslon had magnetized. [1]

The episode is easy to recount as a triumphal story about science defeating superstition. It is more instructive—and more disquieting—as an account of how hard it can be to separate an event from the explanation attached to it. The boy's distress was not simply erased by learning that he was standing beneath the wrong tree. The commissioners' question was narrower: had the specific supposed external agent been demonstrated? On the evidence of this test, it had not.

### The treatment and the thing supposedly treating

Mesmer's animal magnetism was presented as a universal fluid linking animate bodies and the wider natural world. In Paris, sessions became famous for strong bodily reactions. People reported heat, trembling, relief, convulsions and other experiences. Mesmer's explanations invited observers to treat those experiences as evidence that a force had been transferred. But the observable result and the alleged mechanism were distinct propositions. A person could feel something without the proposed magnetic fluid existing. [1][2]

In March 1784, Louis XVI appointed medical and scientific commissioners to examine the practice. Benjamin Franklin and Antoine Lavoisier were among those involved. Mesmer himself was not the practitioner whose procedures they investigated; the inquiry concerned animal magnetism as practiced by Deslon, a former associate willing to cooperate. That distinction matters. The commission tested the claims and procedures made available to it, not every practice or theory that would subsequently acquire the name hypnosis. [1][2][3]

The initial question was whether a purported agent could be detected by its effects when a subject did not know whether it was being applied. If the fluid was the decisive cause, genuine application should matter even when expectations were controlled. If a person instead reacted to believing treatment was occurring, the reaction could not by itself establish the fluid's existence.

That design sounds familiar because modern experiments repeatedly face the same difficulty: people arrive with expectations, investigators communicate possibilities, and reports of sensations can change under suggestion. This does not mean all reports are fabricated. It means a subjective report is a measurement of experience, not automatic proof of a particular external mechanism.

### The blindfold was the argument

The commissioners documented substantial precautions. They devised a covering that blocked light without obstructing the nose. They wanted participants to be unaware of the precise movements of the purported magnetizer. During one sequence, a woman was blindfolded; after she had recovered, she was led to believe the operator was active when no operation was performed. She again described sensations. At another point the operator worked without producing the expected effect. [1]

In another trial, women were placed in separate rooms and given the impression that Deslon was conducting a procedure when he was not. Their reported reactions were vivid. The observation challenged the inference from intense experience to magnetic cause, rather than demonstrating that intensity must have been insincere. [1]

At Passy, Deslon was allowed to select a boy he considered especially susceptible. The commissioners chose an apricot tree sufficiently apart from the others. Deslon prepared it without the boy observing; the boy was then presented to four different trees for approximately two minutes each. The report gives distances from the magnetized tree of about twenty-seven, thirty-six, thirty-eight and twenty-four feet as the boy proceeded. The fourth tree was unprepared, yet it was there that he suffered what the report called a crisis. [1]

One feature complicates any simplistic notion that the experiment was an immaculate modern randomized trial: Deslon insisted on remaining in the garden and directing his cane and attention toward the prepared tree. The commissioners wrote that they would have preferred his absence; they instead placed him at a distance and put observers between him and the boy. They recorded the compromise. [1]

That transparency is as important as the dramatic result. A credible investigation identifies its weaknesses, including the concessions that made the experiment possible.

### What the commissioners concluded—and what they could not know

Their final conclusion was forceful. They attributed the reported phenomena to ordinary contact or pressure, imagination and imitation rather than the hypothesized fluid. The 1785 English translation makes the chain of reasoning unusually clear: they sought cases where participants expected an influence without receiving it, and cases where they received an alleged influence without knowing it. [1]

But “imagination” in an eighteenth-century report is not a precise synonym for a present-day neuroscience diagnosis, nor evidence that every experience was willed or counterfeit. Contemporary researchers might distinguish expectations, suggestion, attention, autonomic responses, social influence and many other pathways. The commissioners had neither modern measurement instruments nor a full model of these mechanisms. Their achievement was a disciplined distinction between the claimed cause and an observed effect.

Equally, one should avoid the attractive but historically careless formulation that this was the world's first blinded clinical trial. Controlled comparisons and efforts to mitigate bias have longer, complicated histories. The James Lind Library preserves the commission among foundational examples of fair testing, not as a one-person invention of a methodology. [2]

The record also has a material limitation: the 1785 English text is a translation accompanied by an introduction. Its language is valuable evidence of how the report circulated, but it is not a verbatim substitute for the French manuscript in every question of wording. Wellcome Collection catalogs a surviving 1785 edition, while the James Lind Library identifies the 1784 French commission report. [2][3] Details concerning precise motives, private feelings and unrecorded dialogue must not be invented simply to enliven a scene.

### Puységur enters a different chapter

It would be convenient to write: Franklin disproved Mesmer; therefore the story of hypnosis ended. It did not. In the same decade, Armand-Marie-Jacques de Chastenet, Marquis de Puységur, became associated with a different set of phenomena and descriptions, including what later writers called magnetic somnambulism. Final Vestige's existing *Puységur's Animal Magnetism Files* is the appropriate archive for those historical records. [5]

Chronology and subject matter both resist conflation. The 1784 royal inquiry addressed the proposed magnetic agent in the practices made available to its commissioners. Puységur's dossiers concern other testimony, episodes and interpretations. The blindfolded tree experiment cannot authenticate Puységur's claims; nor can a negative finding about a purported fluid, by itself, dispose of every later clinical or psychological observation filed under hypnosis.

Nineteenth-century physician James Braid later developed a physiological and psychological account of hypnotic phenomena. A historical analysis in the *American Journal of Psychiatry* describes his move away from Mesmer's fluid theory and toward the investigation of suggestion. [4] This is part of a genuine transition in explanations—not evidence that any contemporary commercial hypnosis product can deliver a specified result.

### A feeling is evidence of a feeling

Here is the deeper lesson. Suppose a person leaves a meditation session feeling calmer. That report deserves to be heard. It does not automatically identify the cause. The improvement might involve the practice, the environment, the expectations brought into the room, ordinary rest, or multiple interacting factors. To determine whether a particular audio method reliably causes a particular benefit, the method needs a fair comparison with appropriate controls and outcomes.

Likewise, evidence that an experimental suggestion changes what a participant reports does not establish that any recorded suggestion will permanently transform a listener's finances, personality or health. The form of a claim determines the evidence it requires. A claim about the presence of a twelve-minute audio file is answered by inspecting that file. A claim about enhanced brain function requires much more.

The commission's lasting relevance lies not in the humiliation of a frightened child but in its attempt to stop an explanation from borrowing certainty from an experience.

### A free, non-deceptive observation

Do not reproduce the orchard experiment or deceive someone into believing they are receiving a treatment. Instead, try a safe self-observation. Sit comfortably and notice the position of your hands for thirty seconds. Before beginning, write down one neutral expectation: perhaps that you may notice warmth, pressure, or no change. Afterward record what you actually noticed, leaving room for “uncertain.” Then consider which expectations may have directed your attention.

This is a reflection exercise, not a test of animal magnetism, a diagnostic method, a treatment, or a scientific demonstration of suggestion. Its value is that it forces two sentences to remain separate: “I noticed this,” and “I know what caused it.”

In the orchard at Passy, the boy's reaction had to be attended to. The magnetized apricot tree had to be located. Both facts mattered. The error began where one was used to prove the other.
