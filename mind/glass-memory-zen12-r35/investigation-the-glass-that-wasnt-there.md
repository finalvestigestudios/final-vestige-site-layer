# The Glass That Wasn't There

*A single word entered a description of a car crash. Seven days later, some witnesses reported an object the film never showed.*

**Final Vestige Studios | The Unreliable Witness | UNPUBLISHED editorial draft, October 31, 2026**

## I. The accident after the accident

The glass entered the story a week after the crash. No one had to manufacture shards, plant a photograph, or tamper with a car. There had been no glass in the film at all. It arrived as an answer to a question.

In 1974, psychologists Elizabeth F. Loftus and John C. Palmer reported two experiments about people viewing short films of automobile accidents. In the first, researchers asked viewers to estimate the speed of the vehicles. The crucial question changed just one verb. The cars might have *hit*, *contacted*, *bumped*, *collided with*, or *smashed into* one another. The footage remained the same; the language did not. Estimated speed changed with the wording. The question was no longer a neutral instrument of measurement. It was part of the experience. [1]

The second experiment asked something different. Another group of 150 students watched a film depicting a multiple-car accident. Fifty were asked a speed question containing *smashed*, fifty one containing *hit*, and fifty received no speed question. One week later, participants were asked whether they had seen broken glass. The film contained none. Sixteen of the fifty in the *smashed* group answered yes, compared with seven of fifty in the *hit* group and six of fifty in the control group. [1]

Those are outcomes from a particular laboratory procedure. They are not estimates of the number of real eyewitnesses who fabricate evidence. What the experiment demonstrates is more precise and more interesting: information introduced *after* an event can influence how the event is later reported.

## II. Where the word entered

Consider the timing. The participants had already watched the footage before they encountered the influential word. It could not change the photons that reached their eyes. Instead, it became post-event information, delivered while they were being asked to describe what they had seen.

A violent verb suggests a violent collision. If a viewer is unsure about speed, that suggestion may become a cue. The subject might consciously choose a larger number to match what seems to be expected. Alternatively, the later description may become entangled with the person's recollection of the original film. The authors considered both response bias and an alteration of the memory representation. The second experiment was intended to get beyond the speed estimate, but an answer on a later questionnaire does not directly reveal the precise internal mechanism by which that answer formed. [1]

This distinction gets lost when the result is retold as though one word simply overwrote everyone's memory. It did not. In the *smashed* group, thirty-four out of fifty did not report glass. The finding is a difference in reporting probabilities, not universal obedience to a suggestion.

Nor did the experiment recreate an emotionally traumatic real collision, a police lineup, or a trial. Students watched relatively short films. Questionnaires supplied tightly controlled language. This controlled design is its strength, because it isolates a factor that would otherwise be embedded in a mess of competing influences. It is also the reason the study's numerical results should not be imported wholesale into courtrooms, hospitals, or personal memory disputes.

## III. Confidence is not a source document

A remembered scene can feel complete long before it is complete. We retain useful patterns, people, consequences, and apparent explanations; later accounts are often constructed from several streams of information. That normally helps us function. It can also make it harder to separate something originally seen from something suggested afterward.

The literature since 1974 has examined false beliefs and memories induced through imagination, false feedback, and other procedures. A 2021 systematic review by Beate Muschalla and Fabian Schönborn covered fifty-nine experimental articles and emphasized substantial heterogeneity among methods. It did not establish a general daily-life risk that could be applied to every person, meditation session, or therapy appointment. [2]

Research on hypnotic suggestion raises related but distinct questions. Steven Jay Lynn and colleagues published a 2020 review of myths about hypnosis and suggestion, including myths about unusually reliable memory retrieval. It is neither a declaration that hypnosis is uniformly fraudulent nor proof that any commercial hypnosis recording treats a condition. It is a call to distinguish evidence from inherited cultural stories. [3]

There is no reason to discard a witness merely because perception and recall can be influenced. The more useful approach is to examine conditions: the earliest surviving account, the exact words of the questions, who supplied a detail first, which witnesses were independent, and which records existed before the disputed event was interpreted. Honesty and accuracy are different properties. Neither cancels the other.

## IV. Questions in an eighteenth-century archive

The connection to Final Vestige's published [Puységur's Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) is methodological, not diagnostic. Puységur's 1784 memoir and 1785 sequel assembled reported cures, states described as magnetic somnambulism, predictions, witness statements, repetitions, doubts, and failures into a case for animal magnetism. The published archive follows 153 master-source rows and sixteen case dossiers while distinguishing original editions from later verification reproductions. [4]

Loftus and Palmer did not test Puységur's subjects, and their 1974 experiment does not tell us which eighteenth-century claims were mistaken, fabricated, or accurate. It offers a question that archivists can carry into the historical record without pretending to solve the history: who supplied the wording before the witness supplied the answer?

When a practitioner asked about an unusual sensation or future event, did the idea originate with the subject? What did observers already expect? Did the source preserve the question or only the reply? Were unsuccessful predictions retained alongside the successes? Could a claim be cross-checked against a document created before its alleged fulfillment?

The Puységur archive retains failed cures and failed predictions, making its record richer rather than weaker. Preserving contrary evidence is how an account becomes suitable for investigation instead of publicity.

## V. Hypnosis is not an archaeological tool for buried memory

Because animal magnetism belongs to the prehistory of modern hypnosis, it is easy to collapse historical somnambulism, therapeutic hypnosis, self-hypnosis recordings, and ordinary meditation into one category. They are not interchangeable. They have different methods, claims, aims, and evidence bases.

In 1985, the American Medical Association's Council on Scientific Affairs cautioned that memories elicited through hypnosis can include confabulations and pseudomemories and may be unreliable for forensic purposes. An American Psychological Association professional overview later reiterated the difficulty of treating hypnosis as a truth-recovery technique. Those cautions concern memory retrieval under suggestion. They do not establish that every self-hypnosis audio recording is harmful, or that a guided relaxation exercise cannot feel useful. [5][6]

The ethical question changes with the claim being made. An audio program described as a collection of short guided sessions can be assessed for its format, accessibility, and terms. A claim to recover accurate repressed events, treat a health condition, or automatically reproduce the effects of a much longer intervention demands different, product-specific evidence.

Final Vestige's scientific reporting cannot be used as a backdoor endorsement of an affiliate product. Nor does criticism of poor historical evidence allow us to diagnose the people who made that evidence.

## VI. A free exercise in asking better questions

You need no prank, hidden image, or manipulated autobiographical memory to try the principle.

Choose a news photograph that you can reopen after a short interval. Study it for twenty seconds. Without viewing it again, write an account divided into three columns: **I saw**, **I inferred**, and **I learned afterward**. Put physical objects and visible text in the first column; causal interpretations in the second; anything received from captions, other people, or later reading in the third.

Now examine two *fictional* prompts: “Why did that person rush away?” and “Which direction was that person walking?” The first question presupposes a hurried departure; the second asks about direction without telling you why the person moved. Neither proves that the scene contains movement at all.

Reopen the image. Check what is visible and which inferences remain undecidable. This is an exercise in identifying loaded questions, not a demonstration that your memory has been experimentally modified. Do not apply it to distressing personal memories or attempt to induce false recollections. If memory problems or troubling recollections are affecting daily life, seek an appropriate qualified professional.

The procedure is especially useful to readers of historical records. When a newspaper says a ship *vanished*, ask whether the surviving evidence shows destruction, disappearance, or only lost contact. When a witness writes that someone *refused*, ask whether silence, confusion, or lack of opportunity is also possible. Words do useful work; they also bring assumptions along.

## VII. The second accident

One collision took place on film. A second, quieter collision occurred between a record of what had been watched and language introduced afterward. The two need not be identical for the influence to matter.

There is a temptation to turn this finding into a sweeping indictment of eyewitnesses. That would be another overstatement. Archives would be impoverished without testimony. Human reports become more useful when their circumstances of production remain visible: the questions, revisions, expectations, intervening stories, surviving documents, and failures.

A question may help a witness speak. It can also smuggle its own conclusion into the record. The craft of investigation is to keep those possibilities separate.

The glass was never in the film. The important thing is to remember where it entered the story.
