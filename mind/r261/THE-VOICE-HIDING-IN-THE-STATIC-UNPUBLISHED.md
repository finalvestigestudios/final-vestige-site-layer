# The Voice Hiding in the Static
## Why an unclear recording can sound like a message—and what that means for the audio we buy

**Final Vestige Studios / Mind & Perception — original research manuscript, UNPUBLISHED, 9 November 2026.**  
**Editorial status:** Source-reviewed at abstract/publication level; scientific and canonical uniqueness review still required. No commercial endorsement or product CTA.

Imagine someone hands you a recording. A thin hiss fills your headphones. They tell you that, around the seventh second, a voice says a sentence. They play it again. At first, you can make out only the rough rise and fall of noise. On the third listening, the sentence seems almost obvious. You can no longer remember exactly how the sound was before you were given the words.

Now change one feature of this scene. A second listener hears the very same recording, but receives no suggested sentence. What would that person hear? And if they hear *different* words, which listener should we trust?

This question sits at the crossroads of anomalous history, attention research, and a contemporary marketplace selling audio that supposedly works at the edge of awareness. Its answer is not that the senses are useless, or that every ambiguous recording is meaningless. It is that **hearing something and demonstrating what caused it are different achievements**.

## A laboratory studies the alleged voice

In research published online in August 2014, Michael A. Nees and Charlotte Phillips studied what happens when people listen to recordings of purported electronic voice phenomena—often called EVP. These are recordings interpreted by enthusiasts as containing otherwise unexplained voices. The researchers placed such clips alongside ordinary human speech, degraded speech, and acoustic noise. Their experimental question was not whether a particular haunted building was haunted. It was whether the *context provided to a listener* could influence the report of hearing a voice. [1]

Some participants were told the task concerned speech intelligibility; others were given a paranormal frame. That frame increased the proportion of trials in which participants reported hearing voices in EVP recordings and degraded speech. Importantly, when listeners detected an apparent voice in ambiguous stimuli, they showed low agreement about its supposed words. [1]

Those are two distinct observations. The first concerns the threshold for reporting a voice at all. The second concerns whether the content of the report is reproducible among independent observers. A listener may be sincerely confident without other people independently identifying the same phrase.

The distinction matters well beyond paranormal recordings. When a social video puts subtitles over an unintelligible sound, the caption becomes part of the viewer's perceptual context. A headline can tell a reader what to hear before a waveform ever reaches the ear. The research does not show that captions always create phantom speech. It shows that experimental context can matter under at least some ambiguous listening conditions.

## The replication that complicates the tidy story

A good investigation does not stop at a study that fits its opening. Jess M. Williams and Mark Blagrove conducted a later study, published in *PLOS ONE* in September 2022, involving 61 analyzed participants. The experiment used purported EVP, degraded speech, ordinary speech, and white noise. Thirty people were assigned to a paranormal prime and thirty-one to a more neutral description. [2]

The researchers did **not** replicate the earlier finding as a straightforward between-group increase: the primed group did not report a higher proportion of voices across EVP and degraded speech than the unprimed group. They did observe a narrower effect. Within the primed group, participants reported more voices in EVP than in degraded speech; that contrast was not present in the unprimed group. The paper calls the outcome a *partial replication*. [2]

This is the part that disappears when research becomes a viral slogan. One study suggests that contextual framing can increase voice reports; the later study finds a different pattern. Neither permits the broad statement that every unusual sound is caused by suggestion. Neither provides evidence that paranormal forces caused the reported speech. Instead, the discrepancy tells us to be precise about which sound, which instructions, which observer, and which measured result.

The 2022 paper also reported limited agreement on the *words* attributed to ambiguous stimuli. This is particularly informative when a recording is circulated with a single confident transcription. A transcription can be a hypothesis, not a property established by the recording.

## How does the brain make a word from fragments?

Spoken language is not delivered to us as a neat sequence of isolated dictionary entries. We hear a continuous stream in which sounds overlap, consonants are transformed by neighboring vowels, and background conditions interfere. Listeners recruit learned vocabulary and contextual expectations to make sense of those incomplete signals. That adaptive process helps us understand real people in difficult environments. It can also complicate the interpretation of noise.

William Ganong's 1980 experiments varied voice-onset timing in acoustic sequences for which one phonetic categorization yielded a real word and the other yielded a nonword. Participants tended to favor the word-consistent interpretation, with the effect strongest near the ambiguous phonetic boundary. [3] Ganong's work involved controlled speech continua—not ghost recordings or commercial meditation tracks. It nonetheless illustrates why the same small difference in sound can be interpreted differently depending on what linguistic possibilities the listener already knows.

This does **not** mean that listeners simply hallucinate everything they expect. Actual acoustic evidence constrains recognition; the real-word effect was smaller at the clearer ends of the continuum. The careful lesson is more interesting: perception integrates the incoming signal with a person's linguistic knowledge. To understand a reported word, we need to examine both.

A common mistake is to treat the phrase "top-down processing" as a universal explanation that eliminates the need to inspect a recording. It does not. Microphone artifacts, editing, compression, overlapping sources, room acoustics, and genuine speech all remain possible. A rigorous inquiry asks whether independent listeners can detect the same phrase under controlled conditions, whether the sound's origin can be traced, and whether an alternative transcription changes what people report.

## A practical demonstration that does not pretend to be a clinical test

You can examine this problem without downloading anyone's alleged paranormal audio, contacting strangers, or using manipulated third-party recordings. Create a simple, rights-cleared recording yourself: speak a short neutral sentence, then create a second version that is deliberately harder to understand by adding mild background sound or decreasing clarity. Do not distribute recordings of other people without consent, and avoid loud audio or prolonged exposure.

Give two willing adult listeners the unclear version separately. One sees only "Write down what, if anything, you hear." The other sees a proposed transcription of the sentence before listening. Neither should know which condition the other received. Record each person's initial report *before* playing the clear version. Then play the clear version and invite both to compare their first reports with the actual recording.

This is an **informal observation exercise**, not a randomized experiment, diagnosis, or proof that expectations created a perception. Two people are a tiny sample; one recording cannot establish a general effect. Your voice, editing choices, and the listeners' familiarity with your speech are confounds. The goal is to discover how easily an investigator can accidentally insert an interpretation into the method of collecting evidence.

Try the exercise again on another day with the proposed sentence withheld until after the first response. Keep the full sequence and original files. If you change the stimulus halfway through, document it. The most useful output is a record of what was heard *before* the explanation was offered.

## Three questions every claimed voice must survive

First, **what was physically recorded?** A file's provenance, duration, acoustic condition, and processing history help separate an unusual claim from an edited clip.

Second, **what did independent listeners report before being coached?** Comparing unprompted transcriptions is stronger than asking people to agree with a printed phrase. Agreement is not automatically proof of a supernatural or psychological mechanism, but disagreement can expose uncertainty.

Third, **does the account survive a plausible alternative explanation?** If subtitles, suggested phrases, or selective replays are necessary for the sound to become recognizable, that is part of the evidence. It does not mean the experience was insincere. It means the confidence of the witness and the identity of the source should be evaluated separately.

Notice what these questions do not require: accusing an observer of lying. An honest report can be incomplete. In research and archives alike, the challenge is often not dishonesty but the transformation of a vivid experience into a stronger causal claim than the record can support.

## The historical bridge: animal magnetism and the problem of proof

Long before anyone could make a digital recording, witnesses gathered around unusual mental states. The eighteenth-century accounts associated with Armand-Marie-Jacques de Chastenet, marquis de Puységur, describe magnetic somnambulism, responsiveness to suggestion, apparent changes of awareness, and accounts of healing. Final Vestige's published **Puységur Animal Magnetism Files** organize those records as a source trail rather than treating their most extraordinary interpretations as settled fact. [4]

The analogy has limits. A manuscript describing a person's behavior in 1785 is not equivalent to a controlled auditory experiment. The historical witnesses lived in different social and medical contexts, and the archive cannot retrospectively diagnose them. The worthwhile connection lies in the investigator's discipline: **what was observed, by whom, under which expectations, and what explanation was added afterward?**

In an auditory case the first layer may be a waveform and a spontaneous transcription. In an archival case it may be a dated witness account. In either setting, the interpretation is a second layer. Keeping those layers apart preserves rather than diminishes the mystery.

## The commercial temptation: "hidden" audio and invisible results

A person who hears an unintelligible phrase after being prompted to expect it has not demonstrated that a commercial self-help recording can change memory, confidence, income, or health. These are different questions, requiring different experiments.

That boundary matters because the official Inspire3 catalog promotes multiple audio-related products. **Subliminal Guru** is described by the seller as a library of hundreds of subliminal albums. **Brainwave Shots** is described as brief task-oriented audio; **Zen12** markets a structured meditation program. The descriptions explain what the seller offers or promises, not what independent controlled trials have established for those products. [5]

One particularly relevant experiment predates today's streaming market. In three double-blind replications reported in *Psychological Science* in 1991, Greenwald and colleagues studied commercially produced subliminal self-help tapes. In total, 237 participants who wanted the advertised memory or self-esteem effects used tapes under the manufacturers' recommended conditions. The researchers varied what the recordings actually contained and what the labels said they contained. After a month, the tapes did **not** produce the advertised improvements in memory or self-esteem, while a substantial fraction of listeners reported perceived improvement matching the *label*. [6]

That finding is striking because it separates objective outcomes from reported experience. But it is a study of those particular 1990s recordings and claims; it is **not** a direct trial of any current Inspire3 title. It does not establish that all guided audio is useless. The opposite overreach would be equally careless: taking the subjective improvement reports as proof that inaudible suggestions caused a measurable benefit.

A later 1994 experiment on commercial subliminal audiotapes likewise found no performance changes consistent with manufacturers' claims, while participants' perceived improvement tended to follow which kind of tape they believed they had received. [7] Again, the evidence concerns the tested tapes, not every present-day product.

## Three claims that must stay separated

"**The audio contains material that I cannot consciously distinguish**" is a claim about a recording's signal. It could be investigated with the source file and signal analysis.

"**A listener experiences something after playing it**" is a claim about an observation. It could be documented with careful measurement of experience, context, and expectation.

"**This particular product reliably improves a specified real-world outcome because of its hidden messages or frequencies**" is an efficacy and causal claim. To support it, researchers would need an appropriate comparison, a prespecified outcome, controls for expectancy, representative participants, and sufficiently strong replicated product-specific evidence.

Conflating these three categories is a shortcut from fascinating perception research to unjustified sales copy. The FTC's guidance on health-related claims requires marketers to consider both express and implied claims and to possess adequate substantiation before advertising them. Unrelated studies on a general mechanism cannot automatically substantiate a particular branded product's promises. [8]

No audio used for relaxation should be presented as a substitute for appropriate medical or mental-health care. Nor should this investigation be marketed as a technique for treating auditory hallucinations. Hearing ambiguous words in a laboratory task is not equivalent to a clinical symptom or diagnosis.

## A better decision than belief or dismissal

Suppose you are considering paying for meditation or suggestion audio. Ask what you receive **as a product**: recording count, listening format, access period, technical requirements, support, stated conditions, and documented refund process. Then ask what efficacy claims have actually been tested with that specific product. A recording can be enjoyable or useful as a routine without the merchant having demonstrated its stronger causal promises.

You can also compare a free, ordinary timer with a structured listening routine before paying. The comparison will not isolate a neuroscience mechanism, but it can help you assess personal fit and whether a purchased format is worth the cost. Keep the exercise optional; do not turn it into a diagnosis or a financial decision based on a supposedly proven brain effect.

This is the discipline behind the Mind & Perception Archive: collect what happened, examine how it was observed, expose where certainty entered the story, and allow an extraordinary experience to remain extraordinary without requiring an extraordinary explanation.

The next time a voice seems to emerge from static, the first question is not "Am I hearing things?" It is "What did I hear **before someone told me what it was supposed to say**?"
