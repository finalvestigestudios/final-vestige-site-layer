# The Claim That Became Familiar: Why Hearing It Again Can Make It Feel True

**Final Vestige Studios | Mind & Perception Archive | Independent investigation | UNPUBLISHED draft, 5 November 2026**

*An old experiment put ordinary statements in front of readers three times. Some statements were true, some false. The remarkable result was not that people ceased to know the difference. It was that repetition itself moved their judgments.*

## The third encounter

Imagine a sentence on a card: *A certain obscure mineral was first described in a particular century.* It has the orderly shape of a fact. You do not know whether it is true. You pass it, move on, and two weeks later meet it again. By the third encounter something has changed. The statement no longer feels like a stranger. It has acquired the faint texture of a memory.

Nothing about the mineral has changed. There has been no new evidence. Yet the sentence may now feel more credible.

This is the particular unease at the heart of the **illusory truth effect**: a familiar assertion can seem more true because it is familiar, not because fresh evidence supports it. It is not a theory that people are gullible in every circumstance, that repetition always wins, or that intelligence is irrelevant. It is a reproducible tendency in ratings of statements under studied conditions—with boundaries worth understanding.

The effect has an unusually relevant second life in the age of creator feeds, advertising, search summaries and algorithmic repeats. A phrase can appear in a video, on a sales page, in an email subject line and in the comments. Those repetitions do not become independent witnesses merely because they arrive from different screens.

## The finding from 1977

In 1977, Lynn Hasher, David Goldstein and Thomas Toppino published **“Frequency and the Conference of Referential Validity.”** Their participants evaluated the likely truth or falsity of sixty statements from areas such as arts, sports and politics. Some statements were repeated on three occasions separated by roughly two-week intervals, while others appeared only once. Ratings of repeated statements increased for true *and* false items; the never-repeated items did not show the same increase. The authors proposed that frequency can serve as one cue to apparent validity. This is a claim about a measured change in judgments—not a claim that participants permanently adopted every repeated falsehood.

Primary study: https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-5371(77)80012-1

Why was that interesting? Because most of us imagine that a truth judgment is a miniature trial: the mind summons relevant knowledge, weighs the evidence and issues a decision. Sometimes it does. Yet processing a familiar statement also becomes easier. That **fluency** can influence our feeling about its accuracy before deliberate inspection has begun.

Easy is not the same thing as correct. A practiced lie can be easy to read. A novel, true archival discovery can be awkward to understand. A well-designed font can make a sentence look authoritative without making the underlying claim sound. Familiarity is one cue in a larger process, and the cue can point in the wrong direction.

## The protection that sometimes fails

A second development complicates the story. In 2015, Lisa Fazio, Nadia Brashier, B. Keith Payne and Elizabeth Marsh studied whether prior knowledge shields readers from repetition. Their experiments included general-knowledge statements for which some participants possessed the information necessary to reject false assertions.

Repeated false statements could still receive a relative boost in perceived truth—even when relevant knowledge was available. The researchers called attention to **knowledge neglect**: people can rely on the experience of fluent processing instead of consulting knowledge they possess. This does not mean their knowledge vanished, nor that familiar falsehoods were judged as true as familiar facts. It means the increase associated with repetition was not confined to claims about unfamiliar subjects.

Primary paper: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26301795/ ; DOI: https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000098

That distinction matters to a buyer who has studied psychology or meditation research. Knowing that a benefit claim needs evidence does not guarantee that a repeatedly encountered line will *feel* unpersuasive. A confident tone, a polished design and a cluster of near-identical summaries can create the sensation of broad confirmation. None of those sensations supplies a clinical trial.

The effect is not an all-purpose explanation for belief. Studies compare conditions and average ratings. Individual readers differ; the content and its plausibility matter; warnings, context and the task participants are asked to perform can change the outcome. The right conclusion is not “you cannot trust your brain.” It is “one of the experiences your brain uses to judge truth may be unrelated to the evidence.”

## The largest synthesis—and the caveats

In February 2026, a systematic review and meta-analysis in *Nature Communications* assembled **182 studies, 366 effect sizes and 31,184 participants**, examining research published from 1977 through 2025. After correcting for small-study effects, the authors reported a small average illusory-truth effect (**Hedges’ g = 0.37**, 95% confidence interval **0.30–0.44**). They also reported substantial heterogeneity: results varied with items, first-exposure instructions, veracity cues and the duration of initial presentation.

Review: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13066098/ ; publication record: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41760614/

This is an unusually useful correction to the dramatic version of the story. The pooled effect does **not** mean that 37% of people believe a false statement, that repetition raises truth by 37 percentage points, or that every repeated claim becomes more believable. Hedges’ g is a standardized mean-difference measure. It characterizes effects across samples and methods, not an individual buyer’s inevitable response.

The synthesis also does not make any particular marketing asset, sales funnel or product more effective. It neither validates “subliminal” audio nor proves that a repeated commercial promise will change a real purchase. Laboratory truth judgments and settled online orders are different outcomes.

## Could a first question change the pattern?

A more constructive line of research asks what people can do at the first encounter. In four experiments published in 2020, Nadia Brashier, Emmaline Drew Eliseev and Elizabeth Marsh asked participants to focus on **accuracy at initial exposure**. In their tested conditions, that initial accuracy judgment prevented the later illusory-truth effect **when participants had relevant knowledge**. This is a bounded result, not a promise that a quick question defeats every manipulative message.

Primary record: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31473395/ ; DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2019.104054

The timing is the compelling part. By the time someone has seen an assertion five times, it may already be easy to process. Asking “How would I know whether this is true?” at first encounter invites a different operation: compare the claim with facts and sources rather than accepting the feel of familiarity as a substitute.

Another experiment helps explain the challenge of treating belief as a purely private matter. In 2023, Vellani and colleagues reported that prior exposure raised perceived accuracy and sharing intentions for repeated claims in their experimental tasks. A judgment can influence onward transmission, which creates further opportunities for exposure. Their tested design does not measure every social-media ecosystem, but it supplies a plausible mechanism worth investigating—without pretending an entire platform can be diagnosed from one paper.

Paper: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2023.105421

## When the archival record becomes a mirror

Final Vestige has already examined eighteenth- and nineteenth-century claims around animal magnetism and suggestion. The [Puységur Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) belong to a world of witnessed demonstrations, reported experiences, contested explanations and later reinterpretations.

The history should be handled as history. Puységur's accounts are evidence of what participants recorded or believed at the time; they do **not** independently establish magnetic fluids, modern brainwave entrainment or the efficacy of today's commercial audio. Likewise, the modern illusory-truth literature is not retroactive proof that a historical witness was dishonest, hypnotized or deceived. It supplies a precise question for a researcher: were later writers confirming the original observation, or merely repeating the earlier account?

A number of websites repeating the same paragraph do not constitute a number of independent sources. The researcher should trace the statement backward to its earliest available record and distinguish what the record says from what later paraphrases add.

That technique matters as much for a product page as for a century-old manuscript.

## A three-minute “first encounter” field test

This exercise is an editorial practice, **not** a clinical test, diagnostic tool or demonstration that your beliefs have been manipulated.

**Minute one — capture.** Write down one claim you encountered recently. It might concern a meditation recording, a historical mystery, an online gadget or a supposed productivity method. Record the exact wording rather than your memory of the promise. If the claim includes numbers, preserve the numbers. If the original page is inaccessible, mark the quotation as unverified.

**Minute two — separate.** Create three columns: *what is being asserted*, *who asserts it*, and *what would count as proof*. “Twelve minutes equals an hour of meditation” is an equivalence claim. “The program contains twelve levels” is a package-description claim. Those statements require fundamentally different evidence. A seller may directly document its own package features; supporting a precise brain or health outcome requires appropriate independent or product-specific testing.

**Minute three — inspect.** Find the closest primary source and write one sentence about the match—or mismatch—between the claim and its support. If the source is an abstract, note what you cannot inspect about methods, sample size and outcome definition. If the only source is the merchant, label it a *seller claim*. If you cannot resolve it, record “unknown” instead of silently guessing.

Repeat this practice later with a different claim. Do not expect a personal score or an instant change in cognition. The point is to create a record separating familiarity, assertion and independent support.

## The crucial difference between advertising and evidence

Consider three propositions:

1. A product is listed as a downloadable audio recording lasting thirty-three minutes.
2. A laboratory study found a difference in attention scores under a specified intervention.
3. That audio product will give its buyers unusually powerful concentration.

The first can be checked against an exact merchant listing, subject to the merchant being truthful about its inventory. The second requires reading the study and understanding its intervention and outcome. The third requires evidence that genuinely supports the **specific product and claimed benefit**; it does not follow automatically from either of the others.

The FTC's health-products guidance explains that objective health-related benefits and safety claims must be supported by competent and reliable scientific evidence, with attention to the particular product and claim. Its endorsement guidance also says a material affiliate relationship should be disclosed clearly near the recommendation or link, not hidden on a distant policy page.

FTC source: https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance ; https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/ftcs-endorsement-guides-what-people-are-asking

Here the archive draws a bright line. The public Inspire3 catalog and individual sales pages describe various meditation, hypnosis and audio products, including Zen12, Hypnosis Live and Brainwave Shots. Those descriptions tell a reader what the sellers advertise. They are **not** a cross-product body of independently verified clinical outcomes. Repeating the seller's description in a second voice would not change that.

There is no shortcut from “research on suggestion exists” to “this recording has been proven to change your life.” That leap should be visible to a reader, not smuggled into an elegantly designed purchase button.

## What this experiment cannot tell us

The illusory-truth effect is not the same as the mere-exposure effect, which concerns preference or liking; it is not the same as change blindness, in which an alteration to a scene may go unnoticed; and it is not a diagnosis of suggestibility or susceptibility to hypnosis.

The studies summarized here use heterogeneous items, exposure schedules, populations and rating measures. Laboratory settings do not perfectly recreate high-stakes, personalized online purchases. Meta-analytic averages cannot be used to forecast individual belief. Moreover, the effectiveness of accuracy prompts depends on context and available knowledge. Other interventions or settings may yield different results.

Nor is the scientifically interesting conclusion that all repetition is suspicious. Repetition can help teach accurate information. Archives rely on cross-checking and corroboration. The difference is whether the repeated claims have **independent, traceable support**.

## The reader's decision belongs to the reader

When a sentence returns for the fourth time, it has earned a place in your memory, not a place in the evidence column. Before spending money, subscribing to a claim or forwarding it, inspect what you have actually been shown.
