01 / The experiment

A sentence comes back. Evidence doesn't.

In 1977, Hasher, Goldstein and Toppino repeated some factual statements across three rating sessions. Repeated statements drew higher truth judgments, whether true or false. A 2026 synthesis of 182 studies found a small average illusory-truth effect after bias correction, with substantial variation across study designs.

That does not mean every repeated claim succeeds. It means familiarity can sometimes be mistaken for evidentiary strength. The safeguard is to examine the actual source, not the number of places that repeat it.

02 / A practical method

The three-column test

Take a familiar claim and write down: (1) the exact assertion, (2) who makes it, and (3) what primary or product-specific evidence would verify it. “A package contains 48 files” is a deliverable claim. “A twelve-minute session is equivalent to an hour of meditation” is an efficacy/equivalence claim. They require different proof.
