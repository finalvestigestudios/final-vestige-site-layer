# The Session That Worked in Both Groups

*Final Vestige Studios — Mind & Perception | Original investigation | Unpublished, 12 November 2026*

At first, the finding looks like a success story. People made time each day for a guided breathing recording. After four weeks, they reported feeling less stressed. Put that on a product page and the conclusion practically writes itself: the recording worked.

But the study's most important result lies in the comparison group.

In a randomized trial published on December 13, 2023, Guy Fincham, Clara Strauss and Kate Cavanagh assigned 400 participants to one of two practices. One group breathed at approximately 5.5 breaths per minute. The other practiced a different guided breathing pace: roughly 12 breaths per minute. Both were asked to devote around ten minutes a day for four weeks. Their audio guides used the same background sound; the essential difference was the breathing rate. The investigators also assessed how believable each intervention seemed to participants. [1]

By the main assessment, both conditions showed improvements over time in reported stress and other self-reported measures. The slower technique, however, did not show a significant advantage over the carefully matched comparison on the primary subjective-stress outcome: the paper reported a group-by-time interaction of F(1,377) = 0.089, p = .765. A similar lack of detectable between-group advantage appeared in the other outcomes and at follow-up. [1]

The experiment did not establish that breathing is useless. It made an altogether more specific point: *when two plausible practices produce similar reported improvements, the improvement alone does not identify which ingredient caused it.*

## The comparison that changes the story

Imagine testing a ten-minute focus recording against silence. The recording group gets a voice, a planned pause in the day, a schedule, an expectation of improvement and a repeatable ritual. The silence group may not get any of those things. If the recording group reports a change, which ingredient mattered? To answer that, a researcher needs a better comparator.

The breathing investigators attempted to keep many elements matched while changing the feature they wanted to test. The 12-breath-per-minute condition was an active, believable breathing practice. It is therefore not equivalent to doing nothing. That choice makes the experiment informative about whether the slower pace adds a measurable advantage under these conditions, but not about every conceivable benefit of breathing exercises relative to everyday life.

Participants were recruited online and did their practices remotely. Self-reported stress was an outcome, not a measurement of hidden neural changes. The study can neither establish identical effects in every person nor rule out improvements in unmeasured situations. Its strength is the unusually transparent control: the same background audio, the same apparent ritual, the same duration, a different pace. [1]

The important consumer question follows naturally: if a commercial audio page promises dramatic focus or relaxation, does its evidence compare the product with an equally plausible alternative—or merely with no practice at all?

## When the words on the recording matter

A second, different experiment offers a clue about the role of expectation. In a study published in *Pain* in 2022, 153 healthy adults were randomized in a balanced-placebo design that separated the intervention people actually received from the intervention they were told they were receiving. Researchers compared brief focused-attention mindfulness with sham practice, varied descriptions of the practices, and also included no-treatment participants. Across the intervention conditions, participants completed six twenty-minute sessions, and the outcome involved experimentally induced heat pain. [2]

Those receiving interventions generally improved on pain outcomes compared with no treatment. The expectation manipulation altered what participants anticipated, while factorial comparisons did not reveal clear main effects of actual intervention or instructions on the measured pain outcomes. Believing one had received mindfulness did predict aspects of tolerance and threshold. The researchers emphasized expectancy in this particular brief pain-study setting. [2]

That is not evidence that meditation in general is a placebo. The experiment was about a specific intervention duration, participants, instructions and experimentally induced heat pain. It does not test Zen12, Hypnosis Live or Brainwave Shots. More importantly, it demonstrates a useful distinction: the name of a technique can shape how it is experienced, without telling us everything about its biological or practical effects.

There is an ethical reason to care. When a purchaser hears that a recording will "automatically reprogram" their focus, they may scrutinize their subsequent experiences for confirmation of the phrase. This is a plausible expectation mechanism, not proof that the product has any particular effect. To demonstrate the effect of the specific recording, the test would need to separate the promise from the material delivered.

## A result that moved in the wrong direction

Not every apparent cognitive effect goes in the direction the advertisement predicts. A 2023 published abstract describes an experiment in which 1,000 people completed two parts of a fluid-intelligence task at home. During part two, some listened to binaural beats, others heard other sounds or silence, and the binaural groups received different explanations of what the audio might do. The investigators reported poorer results in the binaural-beat conditions regardless of expectation wording. [3]

That finding cannot be generalized into "all binaural beats harm intelligence." The outcome was a particular test under particular conditions, and the published indexed abstract does not stand in for a full independently audited methods assessment. Neither does it prove anything about one specific commercial track. It does show why the phrase "listen once and focus for hours" cannot be accepted as a scientific consequence of a purported sound-frequency mechanism.

A 2017 controlled study found no enhancement of corresponding EEG frequency bands under the binaural-beat conditions it tested. The authors cautioned about the implications of the methods, rather than claiming to have disproved all possible sound-based research. [4]

A 2023 systematic review pooled no single magic answer: among fourteen studies addressing EEG entrainment hypotheses, five were broadly supportive, eight contradictory, and one mixed. Their procedures differed substantially. The finding of an auditory perception, a measurable neural response, and improved performance are three separate propositions. [5]

This matters because commercial language often slides across those propositions as though establishing one automatically establishes the others. A perceptible beat is not necessarily entrainment. Entrainment is not necessarily a benefit. A benefit in another study is not necessarily a benefit from the branded product a visitor is considering.

## The older experiment hiding in the archive

In 1784, Franklin and fellow commissioners studied claims of animal magnetism as practiced by Charles Deslon and other demonstrators. Their experiments included subjects who reacted when they thought an operation was happening and who failed to react as expected when it was performed without their knowledge. The commission rejected evidence for the supposed fluid, while taking the reports of symptoms seriously enough to investigate them. [6]

Final Vestige's public investigation makes two things clear. The tests concerned particular eighteenth-century claims, not all subsequent treatments. And the commission's language of "imagination" should not be mistaken for a modern technical theory of placebo, sham controls or neural mechanisms.

Puységur's nearby historical archive complicates the story still further. It holds recorded cases, predictions, testimony, failed outcomes, vulnerable subjects and warnings about operator pressure. An archived statement can establish what someone was recorded as saying without demonstrating the truth of the speaker's explanation. [7]

Neither archive demonstrates whether contemporary meditation audio works. Together with modern controlled trials, however, they suggest a durable editorial habit: do not convert the sincerity of an experience into certainty about what produced it.

## The most useful twelve-minute experiment you can do

An everyday buyer cannot run a blinded clinical trial at home. You can nevertheless ask a much smaller and practical question: *Is this a routine I would actually use?*

Choose a safe, ordinary task with an observable endpoint: outline a paragraph, sort a set of notes, or plan tomorrow's first action. Before beginning, write down what finishing would look like. Work for twelve minutes with no purchased audio. Record interruptions and whether you completed what you intended. Another day, repeat with an ordinary background sound if you prefer. Avoid audio during driving or hazardous work.

This is not a cognitive diagnostic, a medical intervention, or a valid test of neural entrainment. It has no blinding, no reliable randomization, varying task difficulty and a sample too small for causal conclusions. But it separates "I felt different" from "I finished my planned work," which is useful when deciding what convenience is worth buying.

## Questions to keep beside any sales page

**Does feeling calmer prove the special sound caused it?** No. It demonstrates a report that needs an appropriate comparison before attributing cause.

**Does this trial prove slow breathing never helps?** No. It found no added effect over a credible active comparison in its tested measures, population and schedule.

**Does an EEG result automatically establish better concentration?** No. Neural measurements and practical cognitive outcomes are different, and a product needs product-specific evidence.

**Can I enjoy a recording even without proven clinical claims?** Yes. An audio format or pleasant routine may be worth considering as a preference, so long as the choice is not driven by false guarantees.

**Should a recommendation disclose commission?** Yes, clearly and close to the link, if the recommendation has a material earning relationship. That disclosure is separate from scientific substantiation. [11][12]

The answer is not to distrust every feeling. It is to ask a more careful question of every explanation: **What changed, compared with what, and who checked?**
