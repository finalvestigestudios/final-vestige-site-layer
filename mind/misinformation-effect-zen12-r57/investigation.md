# The Glass That Wasn't in the Film
## How one verb can follow a witness home

*Final Vestige Studios · The Mind & Perception Archive · Investigative manuscript · November 1, 2026 · DRAFT, NOT PUBLISHED*

**Evidence boundary.** This account examines controlled studies of memory reports. A laboratory result is not a diagnosis, a legal verdict, a reliable test of a particular individual, or proof of any commercial hypnosis or meditation product. No endorsement or merchant recommendation appears in this investigation.

There was no broken glass in the film. That small fact is the center of one of psychology's most consequential experiments.

In 1974, Elizabeth Loftus and John Palmer asked people to watch films of automobile accidents. The researchers did not need to change the images between one group of participants and another to discover a difference. They changed a word in a question asked afterward. To some viewers, the cars had “hit” each other; to others they had “smashed” into each other. Later, asked about broken glass, people in the latter condition were more likely to report seeing it. The study says no broken glass was present in the relevant film. The question had introduced a detail that the film itself could not supply.

The result makes a fine viral hook: one verb, one invented memory. But that shorthand is not what the original paper proves. What researchers observed were changed **answers** after changed questions. The causes could include how an event is represented, how information acquired afterward is incorporated into recall, and how participants interpret what the experimenter wants them to report. Those possibilities matter—especially when the experiment is used to make sweeping claims about witnesses, suggestion, hypnosis or truth.

### 01 / An accident in two experiments

The original paper was called *Reconstruction of automobile destruction: An example of the interaction between language and memory*. The title is specific in a way that popular retellings rarely are. It concerns a relationship between language **after an event** and a person's **later account** of it.

In one experiment, participants saw short filmed collisions and estimated the speeds of the vehicles. The critical question changed one verb: smashed, collided, bumped, hit, or contacted. People who received the more forceful wording, notably “smashed,” gave higher speed estimates than participants asked using less forceful verbs. A speed estimate is not a physical measurement of speed, and nothing in the word itself changes the movie. It changes the linguistic context in which a person makes a judgment.

In the second experiment, participants again watched an accident film and answered a question using either “smashed” or “hit”; a comparison group was not given that speed question. When questioned again a week later, viewers first asked about smashing were more likely to say they had seen broken glass. Yet the paper expressly says there had been no broken glass in the film. The between-group difference is the empirical finding. The memorable image of glass scattered across an intersection is a *mental illustration*, not a frame pulled from the source material.

The distinction between the two experiments is useful. A shift in an estimate made immediately after viewing is not the same outcome as a report of an absent detail one week later. The experiments together suggest post-event language can influence different kinds of responses, but they do not show that every eyewitness develops an irrevocable false memory.

Primary source: [Loftus & Palmer, 1974, *Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior*, 13(5), 585–589](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-5371(74)80011-3). Publisher abstract explicitly describes both experiments and the absent glass.

### 02 / The question becomes part of the record

Why might “smashed” matter? Its connotations are not neutral. It may imply a more violent collision, a higher speed, more damage, and the presence of debris. The participant now has two inputs: the watched scene and the words supplied by a questioner. When later asked what happened, the respondent must distinguish what came from the images from what came afterward.

That source distinction is the central problem. A person can remember a meaningful detail without reliably identifying where it came from. Was the stop sign seen in the original sequence? Was it suggested by a follow-up description? Was it inferred because the road looked like a place where a stop sign should be? A later answer can reflect a mixture of these sources.

In 1978, Loftus, David Miller and Helen Burns tested post-event information using slide sequences of a single auto–pedestrian accident. Across five experiments and a pilot study involving 1,242 participants, the investigators compared consistent, misleading and irrelevant information given after the slides. The PubMed abstract reports that misleading information reduced recognition accuracy on both yes/no and two-alternative forced-choice measures.

That design helped move the discussion beyond a single evocative verb. The question was no longer only whether a leading question influenced a numerical judgment; it was whether later verbal information could alter performance when witnesses were asked to identify visual information encountered before the misleading description.

Primary source: [Loftus, Miller & Burns, 1978, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Learning and Memory*, 4(1), 19–31](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/621467/); [DOI record](https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.4.1.19).

### 03 / Reporting an error is not proving an erased image

A false report is not a transparent window into the brain. Experimental psychologists can measure a participant's response; they cannot simply inspect the original memory trace and declare it physically overwritten. Researchers debate how much of a misinformation effect comes from genuine changes in remembered content versus response processes, retrieval competition, guessing and expectations created by the task.

Consider a participant who says a sign was a stop sign when it was actually a yield sign. One possibility is that the misleading statement has become part of what the person remembers. Another is that the person remembers uncertainty and relies on the most recently encountered description. A third is that the response format encourages a guess rather than allowing an admission of uncertainty. These possibilities are not interchangeable.

Even the term **false memory** covers different outcomes across research programs. Misremembering a word, saying that glass appeared in a film, and confidently recounting an entire fictitious life event are not identical phenomena. Responsible writing preserves the experimental measure instead of borrowing the most dramatic interpretation from a different study.

A 2024 systematic review by Maryanne Brassil, Cian O'Mahony and Ciara Greene examined whether cognitive abilities were associated with vulnerability to eyewitness misinformation. Nine studies met the criteria; the authors summarized 23 associations. Higher ability was associated with reduced susceptibility across several domains, but no single characteristic guaranteed immunity. This is not evidence that a reader can prevent every error by being clever, skeptical or well trained.

Review: [Brassil, O'Mahony & Greene, 2024, *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38696106/); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-024-02512-5).

### 04 / When a story starts borrowing scenes

The implication reaches beyond police interviews. News, family anecdotes, promotional testimonials and historical manuscripts all contain opportunities for one account to influence the next. A detail repeated in five interviews does not become five independent observations if four witnesses heard the first telling before they described what happened.

In 2024, Schincariol and colleagues published a meta-analysis of studies testing false memories and false beliefs after exposure to fabricated news stories. The paper included 13 articles and reported that substantial fractions of participants endorsed at least one false memory or belief under the study conditions. This is an estimate **within the included experimental tasks**, not a probability that a particular reader believes a specific news article, and certainly not a universal measure of human suggestibility.

The paper also distinguishes believing an event happened from recalling it autobiographically. That is a distinction worth protecting. “I think this is true,” “I remember reading it,” and “I remember being there” make different evidentiary claims. A headline that collapses them into one figure creates its own misleading impression.

Source: [Schincariol et al., 2024, *Memory, Mind & Media*](https://doi.org/10.1017/mem.2024.14).

### 05 / An eighteenth-century paper trail

Final Vestige's [Puységur's Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) examine a very different setting: late-eighteenth-century reports of magnetic somnambulism, cures, predictions, testimony and obedience. The archive keeps failures and provenance alongside remarkable claims. Its historical witnesses were people making reports within the beliefs and relationships of their time, not participants in Loftus and Palmer's controlled studies.

The comparison is methodological, not a retroactive diagnosis. A historical witness may have sincerely reported what seemed to happen. That sincerity alone does not establish the proposed cause. Conversely, demonstrating laboratory misinformation effects in some contexts does **not** prove that any named archival witness fabricated or misremembered an account. The archival task is to track who supplied information, when they supplied it, what they had heard already, and whether independent documents contradict or confirm the account.

This is also why careful editors resist the tempting sentence that suggestion “proves” modern hypnosis, or that hypnosis “explains” all historical episodes of mesmeric sleep. These claims leap across centuries, mechanisms and study designs. An analogy can sharpen a research question without becoming its answer.

### 06 / The quiet problem of the enthusiastic interviewer

An investigator can influence a record without intending to. A journalist who asks “How frightened were you when the guard shouted?” has already introduced a guard, a shout and a degree of fear. A better question is “What happened next?” followed by requests to separate direct observation from later conversations and interpretations.

That does not mean every specific question is improper; questions can be necessary for clarification. It means their wording and sequence are part of the evidence and should be retained wherever feasible. Recorded interviews, dated witness statements, original image files and contemporaneous notes can help later reviewers distinguish earlier observations from subsequent interpretations.

The same principle applies when someone evaluates a commercial audio program. A testimonial asking whether they “finally unlocked their hidden focus” supplies a theory of what happened. An experience log that asks “What, if anything, changed? What else did you do? What did you expect beforehand?” is less eager to make the product its own explanation. Neither log, by itself, demonstrates an effect.

### 07 / A free experiment in the wording of questions

**Try this with a willing partner; no deception about personal experiences is necessary.** Find a short, fictional, non-distressing video clip depicting an ordinary event. Keep the video identical for two volunteers. Ask one what happened using a neutral question. Ask the other a question that includes an evaluative word, such as “hurried” instead of “walked.” Have both write down what they actually observed, what was inferred, and how certain they feel. Then reveal that the questions differed and discuss the difference between reporting and interpretation.

This is **not** a scientifically valid replication of Loftus and Palmer. It lacks random assignment, enough participants, standardized stimuli and blinded scoring. Its value is educational: it makes the construction of a question visible. Do not use it to diagnose someone's memory, test a vulnerable person, suggest traumatic incidents, or adjudicate real disputes.

If you are doing historical research instead, run a simpler source exercise. Take three accounts of the same incident and build a chronology of publication. Highlight any detail appearing in all three. Then ask whether account B could have read A, and whether C could have read both. Corroboration is not just numerical agreement; it is about the independence of the routes by which information arrived.

### 08 / What remains true after the surprise fades

The experiment with the absent glass shows that words spoken **after** an event can affect later reports of what was seen. It does not show that every recollection is false, that memory works like a recorded video, or that a specific person can be made to believe anything. It does not certify any hypnosis program, “brainwave” audio, memory-recovery method or meditation package.

The paper's lasting force comes from its restraint: the film did not change; the question did; the answers were measured. The ethics of evidence begin with noticing which part of that sequence we know and which we are only imagining.
