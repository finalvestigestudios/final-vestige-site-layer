# The Hand Absent During the Sham Trial
### The 1784 experiment in expectation—and why a genuine sensation is not proof of an invisible mechanism
**Final Vestige Studios | Mind & Perception | Historical Evidence Investigation | UNPUBLISHED; corrected 9 November 2026 following independent primary-source review. Legacy file name is not the approved title.**

In the sham-condition trial, the operator was absent. The woman still reported heat.

In Paris in 1784, a woman reported warmth as physician Jumelin approached her with his hands. The commissioners covered her eyes: her reported sensations no longer followed the direction of his movements. **An intervening step cannot be erased:** they removed the blindfold, Jumelin **applied his hands to her upper abdomen**, and the woman became faint and fainted. Only after she recovered did they blindfold her again, remove Jumelin, request silence, and lead her to believe he was operating. She still reported symptoms when no operation took place. The commissioners' English translation printed this exact sequence on pages 56–57, and reported a later active-operation comparison. [1]

It is a wonderfully unsettling record—not because it proves that someone had imagined a physical feeling, and certainly not because it proves she was dishonest. It unsettles a different assumption: that the vividness of an experience establishes the truth of the explanation offered for it.

Today a recording might promise to control brainwaves, accelerate meditation or produce extraordinary focus. The same question stands between the claim and the evidence: does an outcome persist because of a special mechanism, or could timing, attention, expectation and ordinary experience explain it? This history cannot answer whether any contemporary product works. It can show why that answer requires independent testing.

## The invisible fluid people said they could feel

Franz Anton Mesmer's animal magnetism proposed an invisible fluid moving through living bodies and nature. Illness, in this account, was linked to disturbed circulation of the fluid; a magnetizer might correct it with gestures, touch or other instruments. Mesmer's Paris sessions were famously theatrical, often involving music, group treatment and intense bodily responses. Some participants trembled or collapsed. To believers, the responses signified the force at work.

To an investigator, however, **the effect and the proposed cause were different things**. A person trembling proves the observer saw somebody tremble. It does not, without additional controls, prove there is a magnetic fluid or that it caused the movement.

The French crown appointed a commission in March 1784 to inspect the practice of **Charles Deslon**, a physician associated with Mesmer. Benjamin Franklin, Antoine Lavoisier, Jean-Sylvain Bailly, Joseph-Ignace Guillotin and other members were involved. Their official findings were published in August; an English translation appeared in 1785. The inquiry studied Deslon and some experiments involving a different practitioner, **Jumelin**. It did not subject Mesmer himself to the exact set of experimental operations described here.

This precision matters. Summaries that say Franklin personally disproved every phenomenon of hypnosis or personally tested Mesmer overstate what the source documents show. They tested particular claims about the alleged fluid in particular circumstances. [1]

## The sick people in Franklin's house

At Franklin's home in Passy, seven ill patients underwent Deslon's treatment in the presence of the commissioners. Many said they felt nothing. A few reported symptoms. Deslon's proximity to existing pain, pressure, group expectations and spontaneous sensations complicated the interpretation.

The resulting account was not a modern randomized controlled trial. No preregistered outcome, independent assessor, power calculation or contemporary research-ethics review existed. Patients were not randomly recruited from the population. The observers also used language about class, illness and bodily response that deserves historically critical reading.

But the report did identify a scientific problem: **the signature responses were inconsistent**, and where a response occurred the commissioners still could not tell whether the claimed agent had been responsible. The next experiments tried to remove the information that might produce anticipation. [1]

## A woman who could feel the physician's approach

At a session with Jumelin, the commissioners observed a series of people receiving an alleged magnetic operation. One woman was especially responsive. With her eyes open, she described heat as the doctor's hands moved near her. The commissioners asked to blindfold her.

When the operation moved near her stomach or back, she instead spoke of symptoms in her head and eyes. Without knowing where the hands were, her sensations stopped tracking their location.

The blindfold was then **removed**. Jumelin **applied his hands to her upper abdomen** (called the “hypochonders” in the 1785 translation); she reported heat and then fainted. After her recovery, investigators **blindfolded her again, removed Jumelin and called for silence** while telling her the operation continued. She again reported heat and pain even though **no operation took place**. About fifteen minutes later he resumed operating near her stomach and back, without the matching increase of sensation that the alleged agent would predict. See printed pp. 56–58. [1]

The crucial comparison was not between feeling and not feeling; the feeling was reported in both conditions. It was between **actual procedure and believed procedure**. An explanation involving an invisible fluid had lost the correspondence on which it depended. The subject's belief could be manipulated without manipulating the supposed agent. [1]

We cannot interview the woman. We have the commissioners' selection and wording, the translator's presentation, and a historical account written to convince a public audience. That makes it evidence to read critically, not a transparent window into her private mind. Yet its specific arrangement remains instructive.

## A question that acts like an intervention

The investigators tried other blindfolded demonstrations, sometimes suggesting through their questions what the subjects should be feeling. Their record describes sensations reported while no magnetic action occurred and occasions when a real action did not elicit the anticipated response.

A question about whether a forehead tingles can make someone scrutinize that forehead. A questioner who expects heat can imply the answer even without ordering the witness to give it. Modern psychological research is more careful about experimenter effects, blinded assessors and demand characteristics, but the underlying problem was clearly visible in 1784.

The commission's report also describes the construction of a padded eye covering designed to prevent light from reaching the subject. Their concern was that the subject might see evidence of the operator's actions. The device was not modern double-blinding—experimenters often knew what was being done—but it removed one obvious channel of information. [1]

## The tree that had never been treated

One of the most memorable trials involved a young boy whom Deslon considered magnetically sensitive. In Franklin's orchard, Deslon allegedly magnetized an apricot tree. The blindfolded boy was taken to several other trees first and began exhibiting symptoms before arriving at the designated tree.

The episode is frequently retold as a simple exposure of trickery. It is better understood as a test of **specificity**. If the special fluid was localized to a particular treated tree, symptoms at untreated trees would not reliably identify the treated one.

It is not necessary to declare the boy a liar, or to conclude that every symptom in the historical literature was created through suggestion. It is enough to say that those symptoms failed to establish the claimed physical source. [1]

## What the commission decided, and what it could not decide

The royal report attributed the observed effects to imagination, imitation and pressure, rather than a special magnetic fluid. In the language of that period, "imagination" referred to powerful responses to ideas and expectations, not necessarily a fabricated complaint.

The commission was unusually explicit that it was not content with merely observing dramatic crises. It needed to isolate a proposed cause. It asked what happened when operators worked unseen, when they did not work at all, and when the participant expected to be acted upon.

But some limits remain: reports from a small, selected collection of demonstrations cannot settle every future clinical question. This was an inquiry into animal magnetism's claimed material cause. It was not a 21st-century test of hypnosis as a clinical adjunct or a modern treatment for pain or anxiety. The existence of suggestion does not establish miraculous healing. The rejection of magnetic fluid does not establish that every practice built around attention, language, or ritual is inert.

These distinctions matter because popular retellings often swing between extremes: either the commissioners explained away the whole mind, or the later survival of hypnotic methods vindicated Mesmer's fluid. Neither inference follows. [1,2]

## In the same year, a quieter case

Elsewhere in France, the Marquis de Puységur, influenced by Mesmer's methods, recorded events surrounding a young farmhand named **Victor Race**. According to Puységur's account, the young man entered a calm, communicative state during a procedure that the practitioner interpreted as magnetic. Puységur's descriptions helped establish what came to be called magnetic or artificial somnambulism.

The story is compelling because its central spectacle was less a convulsive crisis than a changed relationship: subject, experimenter and conversation. In historian Adam Crabtree's 2019 analysis, Puységur's contribution helped redirect attention toward the patient's inner life and the later history of psychological treatment. A Harvard Divinity School essay by Crabtree in July 2026 describes the case and its disputed extraordinary claims in more detail. [2,3]

A close reading must keep two columns separate. **Historical testimony:** Puységur said Victor spoke in an altered state and seemed to possess special insights. **Independent verification:** the source material does not establish clairvoyance, mind reading or reliable medical diagnosis. There are obvious difficulties arising from operator expectations, lack of independent measurement and status differences between the aristocratic practitioner and farm worker.

The useful historical question is not "was Victor secretly a supernatural diagnostician?" It is how strongly a description of unusual behavior can influence what other people conclude they have observed.

## An unexpected modern echo: the seller and the listener

Suppose two people listen to the same twelve-minute recording. One has first seen an advertisement claiming it will generate a deep meditative state automatically. The other has been told only that it is audio intended to accompany a short practice.

Imagine both later report feeling calmer. Their reports may be sincere, but they cannot by themselves reveal whether a special sound pattern caused the feeling, whether the pause in activity mattered, or whether expectations influenced what they noticed. A study designed to test the claimed mechanism would need appropriately comparable groups, independent outcomes, credible controls and a precise definition of the advertised benefit.

Zen12 Premium is a contemporary commercial meditation-audio product. Its seller describes twelve levels, 48 MP3 files and four audio styles and advertises deep meditation benefits in a twelve-minute session. The public presentation also contains broad claims about focus, creativity and health. Those latter statements are **seller assertions**, not conclusions of the 1784 commission, not findings from Puységur's writings, and not established merely by research on meditation or binaural beats generally. [5,6,7]

The official 2023 review of brainwave entrainment experiments found a mixed research picture. EEG responses are not the same as clinically meaningful performance outcomes, and findings with one experimental sound do not establish the advertised benefits of a different retail product. [7]

Readers might still value a structured, varied audio library. But that is a question of **format, preferences, cost, terms and personal utility**—not proof of an extraordinary mechanism.

## A twelve-minute field test that makes no medical promises

If a schedule is the difficult part, try a free ordinary observation.

Set a timer for twelve minutes. In the first two, name one small, nonclinical task you can actually begin. Spend the next eight minutes reading, sorting notes or working quietly; when attention wanders, notice it and resume. In the last two minutes, record what you finished, which interruptions occurred, and whether a defined end time made starting easier.

Repeat on another day if you find it useful. This is a personal log, **not a randomized or blinded experiment**, and it cannot demonstrate a treatment effect, special brainwave state or change in a diagnosed condition. It has the advantage of asking an observable question: what did I do during a defined interval?

## The evidence standard worth keeping

An effect is a beginning. A cause is a stronger claim. An advertised product benefit is stronger still when the benefit is medical, cognitive or comparative.

The eighteenth-century commissioners did not have modern neuroscientific instruments or ethical frameworks. They did understand a question that survives technological change: will the proposed cause still predict the result when the subject no longer knows the cue?

A woman reported heat when a hand was absent. What disappeared was not necessarily her sensation. What disappeared was the right to use that sensation alone as evidence of an invisible fluid.

That is the point of source-grounded investigation: not to deny an experience, but to insist that an explanation earns its place.
