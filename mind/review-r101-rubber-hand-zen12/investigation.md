# The Hand That Wasn't Yours
## What a rubber hand, a paintbrush, and a disputed illusion reveal about the body's most intimate certainty

*Final Vestige Studios · Mind & Perception Archive · Investigation draft · 3 November 2026*

**Editorial status:** Unpublished investigation. This is about research methods, perception and testimony, not a product review. No advertising, affiliate links or commercial recommendations belong within this investigation.

Picture a table with a lifelike rubber hand resting on it. Your actual hand is concealed behind a screen. You can feel a soft brush pass over a finger you cannot see. At nearly the same moment, you watch a second brush stroke the visible rubber finger. The strokes repeat. The fake hand is not yours, and you know that. But some people begin to *feel* as if it is.

The famous experiment is not proof that perception is a lie or that anyone can be talked into believing anything. It is stranger, and more useful, than either slogan. When sight, touch and the body's internal map deliver overlapping information, the sense of what belongs to the body can sometimes shift. And when scientists ask participants whether they felt the shift, the wording of the question and expectations about what should happen may matter too.

That second complication makes this experiment an unusual bridge between modern psychology and Final Vestige's historical archive of suggestion. It also offers a warning to anyone who promises to change your mind with a recording: a moving experience and an independently demonstrated explanation for the experience are not the same kind of evidence.

### The original hand on the table

In February 1998, Matthew Botvinick and Jonathan Cohen published a short report in *Nature* titled “Rubber hands ‘feel’ touch that eyes see.” The protocol placed a participant's real hand out of sight while a rubber hand lay in view. Researchers used coordinated touch to produce a striking mismatch between what participants *knew* and what they reported *feeling*.

In ordinary life, sight and touch often agree. When you see your hand grasp a cup, the pressure on your fingers and the movement of your arm occur in a coherent sequence. The brain has to integrate those sources of information constantly. It does not receive a single, pre-stamped message saying “this is my hand.” It infers where a limb is, what it touches, and how sight and sensation fit.

That inference can be challenged without changing the body's anatomy. A visible imitation hand, plausibly located and touched at roughly the same time as the concealed real hand, can become a contender for the felt location or ownership of a body part. The research introduced a way to study bodily self-identification rather than merely speculate about it.

**What the 1998 report supports:** under its particular experimental arrangement, some participants reported mislocalized tactile sensations associated with the rubber hand. **What it does not establish:** that all observers experience the effect, that the phenomenon demonstrates mind control, or that a consumer audio product can reproduce it. [1]

### Two things that sound like the same finding

Researchers often investigate the rubber-hand illusion with two measurements. One is a questionnaire: does the participant report a feeling of ownership, or as though touch occurred where the fake hand was stroked? The other is a spatial estimate: has the participant's judgment of where their hidden finger lies shifted toward the imitation? The latter is frequently called *proprioceptive drift*.

It is tempting to treat drift as a ruler for ownership. If the judged location shifts, perhaps the person must have adopted the imitation hand. But a study by Marieke Rohde, Massimiliano Di Luca and Marc Ernst in 2011 challenged that shortcut. The researchers varied how often they measured perceived hand position and compared simultaneous touch, mismatched touch, and conditions involving vision alone. Drift could occur under conditions in which participants did not report ownership. Their conclusion was that location recalibration and subjective ownership can come apart. [2]

Another investigation with rotated imitation hands reached a similar caution: changes in perceived location did not reliably imply an accompanying sensation of ownership. [3] What feels like one magical event in a short video becomes several distinct propositions when measured carefully. A participant might report a dramatic impression without a corresponding positional shift; another might show a shift without claiming the fake hand is theirs.

The distinction matters outside the laboratory too. A questionnaire measures an answer given under specific instructions. A spatial task measures performance on another kind of question. Neither is an all-purpose truth detector for the mind.

### The suggestion problem

Suppose a volunteer enters a psychology laboratory. They see a fake hand, brushes, a partition and a researcher carefully synchronizing movements. Even without being told the hypothesis, the arrangement may hint that something unusual is expected.

Peter Lush's 2020 paper, “Demand Characteristics Confound the Rubber Hand Illusion,” tested people's *expectations* about what an illusion procedure would produce after they viewed descriptions and video of it. The work argued that conventional questionnaire controls did not necessarily match participants' expectations in the experimental and comparison conditions. [4]

This is a serious measurement concern, but it does not justify saying the illusion is “only suggestion.” Reports might reflect genuine multisensory effects, expectations, differences in the way people interpret a question, or a mixture of those influences. Researchers need designs capable of separating those possibilities.

A 2022 paper by Mel Slater and Henrik Ehrsson directly pushed back against the strong suggestion-based interpretation. Its title—“Multisensory Integration Dominates Hypnotisability and Expectations in the Rubber Hand Illusion”—makes the disagreement plain. Their analysis argued that coordinated sensory evidence has an important role that cannot be reduced to hypnotic suggestibility. [5] The dispute is not whether people can be suggestible in a laboratory; it is how much that matters relative to the processes combining sight and touch in this particular experimental setup.

Then, on April 19, 2024, Nanami Tsuji and Shu Imaizumi reported a replication and extension of Lush's expectancy work. In two experiments, one preliminary study of 27 participants and another with 185, people expected stronger illusion-like experiences from synchronized than unsynchronized stimulation **even when judging a video of the procedure**. The researchers also observed an order effect: seeing one condition first altered expectations about the other. The authors explicitly warned about demand characteristics and order confounds in commonly used comparisons. [6]

This finding does not erase the 1998 phenomenon. It complicates the interpretation of testimony about that phenomenon. And that complication is exactly where strong investigation begins.

### Why an eighteenth-century magnetizer belongs in this conversation

Final Vestige's published *Puységur's Animal Magnetism Files* follows an older episode in the history of human observation. Practitioners in the late eighteenth century reported extraordinary states and behaviors in the language of animal magnetism; investigators contested whether the supposed magnetic mechanism had been established.

The rubber-hand literature is not evidence for animal magnetism, nor did Puységur anticipate modern neuroscience. The connection is methodological. In both settings, a reported experience could be sincere and interesting while leaving questions open about *why* it occurred.

There is a difference between three sentences: “The person said they felt the touch on the rubber hand”; “the participant's estimate of hand location shifted”; and “a specific physiological mechanism has been proven.” The first describes a report, the second a measured task outcome, and the third a causal interpretation. Readers deserve to know when an author has moved from one to the next.

This also protects participants from the easy accusation that an unusual report must be dishonest. An honest report may be influenced by attention, context, expectation and the structure of the question. The right response is a better experiment, not a sneer.

### A safe experiment in ordinary perception

No special props, physical contact, startling tricks or imitation limb are needed to investigate the underlying question.

Try this **observation exercise**, not a reproduction of the rubber-hand experiment: sit comfortably and look at one hand lying still on a table. Without moving it, close your eyes and point with the other hand toward where you think the tip of a finger rests. Open your eyes and compare the locations. Repeat after a short break, changing only the duration your eyes remain closed. You may notice no difference, and that is a valid outcome.

The exercise does not measure ownership, hypnosis or suggestibility. It asks a simpler question: how precisely do you know a limb's location when vision is absent? It is an informal reflection with no standardized controls or diagnostic value. Avoid it if pointing or looking at your hands causes discomfort.

For a second, purely intellectual exercise, imagine reading a transcript in which someone says, “I felt the brush on the visible hand.” What additional evidence would you want before concluding the subject had transferred a sense of ownership? Would a comparison condition help? Would it matter whether the questionnaire was written before the participant saw the setup? Would asking about the hand's location tell you something different?

The point is not that you can force an illusion. It is that you can learn to distinguish a compelling story from the evidence needed to explain it.

### The body as evidence, not a machine for testimonials

The rubber-hand experiment is memorable because it seems to press on an intimate fact: *these hands belong to me*. Its scientific value lies in turning that familiarity into questions researchers can test. When timing changes, what happens? When the hand is rotated, what happens? When the questionnaire changes, what happens? When participants know what is expected, what happens?

These questions remain alive because the original result was provocative, not because one paper settled every interpretation. A rigorous archive should hold the 1998 result beside later measurement disagreements, expectancy investigations and competing sensory-integration explanations.

A short video can show the premise. A source trail can show the argument. Those are different jobs. Final Vestige's work is to do both without pretending that an arresting image has resolved the dispute.

The hand was rubber. The experience, for some participants, was real. The mechanism is a scientific question, not an invitation to sell certainty.
