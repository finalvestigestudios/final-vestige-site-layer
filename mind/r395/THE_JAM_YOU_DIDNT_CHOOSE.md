# The Jam You Didn't Choose: A History of Choice Blindness, Suggestion, and the Right to Know

**Final Vestige Studios | Mind & Perception Archive | Investigation draft | 15 November 2026**
**Editorial status:** Unpublished / source-review draft. Independent research; no product endorsement.

*Imagine choosing one jar of jam and then explaining, with complete sincerity, why you preferred the other. The stranger across the counter has made a small switch. The larger switch is in the story your mind tells afterward.*

## I. The small reversal

At a supermarket tasting stand, there are two samples. You try both, settle on a favorite, and say so. Then the organizer asks you to taste the winner again and explain your choice. Unbeknownst to you, the containers have been exchanged.

The second sample is not the jam you just chose.

This scene is not a hypothetical account of a shopper secretly hypnotized by a clever salesperson. It reconstructs the procedure of a 2010 experiment in *Cognition*. Lars Hall, Petter Johansson, Betty Tärning, Sverker Sikström, and Thérèse Deutgen invited supermarket visitors to compare jams and teas. After visitors chose their preferred sample, researchers quietly changed which sample they were given back. According to the published abstract, no more than a third of manipulated trials were detected. Even some notably different tastes or scents escaped immediate recognition. [S2]

The result is arresting because a decision feels like something we own. *I tasted the choices. I selected the one I liked. I know why.* But the experiment placed a gap between intention and outcome. Within that gap, some participants supplied explanations for an outcome that did not match their earlier choice.

A fairer description than “the brain invents everything” is **choice blindness**: under certain tasks and conditions, people may fail to notice when a displayed outcome has been changed, and may still give sincere reasons for the outcome presented to them. The finding does not prove that ordinary consumers cannot tell two foods apart. It certainly does not make every purchasing decision an illusion.

It asks something narrower and more useful: *When an outcome is presented as ours, how carefully do we check whether it is actually the outcome we chose?*

## II. The photograph behind the trick

The supermarket work grew from a 2005 study by Petter Johansson, Lars Hall, Sverker Sikström, and Andreas Olsson, published in *Science*. People saw pairs of photographs of faces and selected the one they considered more attractive. In some trials, the experimenters used a manipulation to hand back the face that had **not** been selected, as though it were the winner. Participants were asked to explain their decision. Many did not flag the discrepancy, and some explained the substituted image in detail. [S1]

This is easy to exaggerate, so the boundaries matter. The task concerned comparative attractiveness of pictured strangers, not irreversible medical decisions, political identity, major family commitments, or purchases made with one's own money. Participants had not been instructed to monitor for sleight of hand. The central outcome was detection of a mismatch in a particular laboratory task. It was not a direct measurement of how many beliefs people possess falsely.

One often-repeated headline is that only 13 percent of swapped trials were noticed immediately in the original paradigm. The larger story is more complicated: studies use different tasks, procedures, detection windows, questions, and denominators. A figure from one experiment should not be passed off as a stable human trait. The original authors themselves framed their result as a challenge to a simple correspondence between choice, outcome, and retrospective explanation. [S1]

That formulation matters more than a sensational percentage. The volunteers were not necessarily lying. They were trying to explain what appeared to have happened. Their account could be fluent and still be attached to an outcome that had been changed.

Ordinary historical research faces a related problem. A witness's confidence can tell us something about the conviction of a report, without by itself settling the mechanism that produced the experience. That is not an accusation against witnesses. It is a reason to preserve the chain between what happened, what was recorded, and what was inferred.

## III. From image to appetite

The 2010 marketplace experiment was a deliberately practical extension. It swapped not merely photographs but familiar sensory products: jam and tea. Some contrasted samples were quite different, making the puzzle harder to dismiss as a mere issue of two nearly identical images. [S2]

Yet several qualifications survive. Participants were taking part in a tasting encounter set up by experimenters. They were not told in advance that a sample might be switched. They were not making ordinary repeated shopping purchases under the normal stakes of household choice. Moreover, “no more than a third detected” concerns manipulated trials under the experiment's counting rules, not the percentage of all shoppers who can never detect an unwanted substitute.

Taste, like vision, is affected by context and attention. A second bite is not simply a replay of the first. The label attached to a sample, the explanation being requested, and the social situation all influence what gets compared. The experimental manipulation did not need to transform a person's senses; it took advantage of a moment when people were not checking the correspondence between their prior selection and the present object.

This is the distinction Final Vestige should retain when discussing meditation or self-hypnosis products. An intriguing demonstration about attention or decision explanation is **not evidence that any named commercial audio product changes brain function, cures a condition, or reliably produces a measured improvement**.

## IV. The answer and the attitude

Researchers also investigated whether the effect could reach beyond simple sensory choices. A 2013 *PLOS ONE* study used altered survey feedback to examine political attitudes and voting intentions; it reported instances of changed responses going unnoticed and participants discussing the altered position as though it reflected their own. [S3]

That does not mean an experimenter can freely rewrite deeply held convictions with a questionnaire. Political identity, question wording, sampling, task context, the size of a change, and the way detection is measured all matter. The experiment is best understood as a warning about the reliability of some immediate self-explanations, not a recipe for mass persuasion or a guarantee of ideological conversion.

A 2014 paper in the *Journal of Behavioral Decision Making* explored a different downstream question: if someone accepts a substituted choice, can that episode affect later evaluations? In the studied face-choice setting, the researchers reported later preference differences involving initially rejected faces. The work raises the possibility that a presented outcome can influence subsequent choices, but it remains a particular experimental design rather than proof that any marketing tactic permanently changes preferences. [S4]

The thread running through these studies is not that people are fools. It is that *choice*, *outcome*, *explanation*, and *future preference* are different things. Research becomes more precise when it measures each of them separately. Advertising becomes more ethical when it does the same.

## V. An old problem in a new laboratory

Long before card tricks and supermarket samples, European debates over animal magnetism posed another question: could a powerful experienced effect establish the theory used to describe its cause?

Final Vestige's published [Puységur's Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) is the historical bridge for this inquiry. Its treatment of suggestion, reported trance and historical documentation should be consulted alongside original records before using any specific quotation or case as proof. On this review pass the live Puységur page was rate-limited; this draft therefore **does not quote or assert unseen details from its current body**. The intended editorial connection is methodological: describe testimony faithfully, then distinguish testimony from a causal explanation.

The same discipline helps when evaluating contemporary meditation claims. Someone can genuinely feel calmer after listening to audio; that experience alone does not identify why it occurred. Was it the quiet time, the expectation, the music, the instructions, the regular routine, natural change, or a specific audio component? A different experimental design is needed to answer that question.

Equally, a study of choice blindness does not demonstrate that meditation is useless. It does not study a meditation intervention at all. The crucial claim is about **how little follows from evidence that tested a different thing**.

## VI. A free twelve-minute decision ledger

Try this before you buy any focus recording, course, or self-development program. It is an *original decision-checking exercise*, not a validated clinical assessment, and it does not require tricking anyone.

**Minutes 0–2: Write the intended job.** Identify one task you actually want help with: for example, sitting down to draft a paragraph, beginning a difficult reading assignment, or taking a brief quiet break. Write a specific observable result that would count as useful. Avoid promising yourself “higher intelligence” or “a transformed mind.”

**Minutes 2–4: Record your choice before seeing the pitch.** Make two columns, “I want” and “I do not need.” A listener might value a clearly explained twelve-minute structure, audio file compatibility, or an accessible transcript; they might not need a huge library, mystical claims, or a subscription. Date the page.

**Minutes 4–6: Read the exact offer.** If considering Zen12, compare the named version to the actual seller's current sales and checkout pages. Write what is included, the displayed amount, the refund conditions presented, and what remains unanswered. Treat a time-limited countdown as a seller display, not as proven scarcity. Do not enter payment details for this exercise.

**Minutes 6–8: Compare the offer to the list you wrote first.** Did a feature genuinely answer the need you identified? Or did the presentation shift your attention toward a different desire? There is nothing wrong with discovering a new preference. The point is to recognize the change and choose deliberately.

**Minutes 8–10: Try a free alternative.** Sit comfortably, silence unnecessary notifications, select one small task, set a twelve-minute timer, and begin. If you prefer quiet reflection, simply notice your attention and let it return when it wanders. This is a personal observation exercise, not evidence that a medical or psychological condition has been treated.

**Minutes 10–12: Mark the result and the uncertainty.** Did you start? Did your attention move? Would you still value a paid audio structure after trying the free option? What evidence would change your mind? Keep your first answers beside your final ones instead of rewriting the first list to match the second.

In research, a preserved record helps detect a mismatch. In buying, the same practice can keep the offer from silently changing the question.

## VIII. The ethics of the next button

Choice blindness creates an awkward but useful challenge for publishers. A conversion funnel is designed to help people act. The same design could also obscure the difference between an editorial conclusion and a seller's pitch.

Final Vestige's answer should not be to eliminate every commercial recommendation. It should be to make the seams visible.

A reader arriving from a video about a switched face must first reach an article about the experiment, its limits, and its sources. A separate optional buyer guide may then explain what a particular product includes, whom its format might suit, and what remains unverified. The link to the free [Seven Day Focus Fieldbook](https://finalvestige.com/focus/) should remain available without buying anything and without adding a second email list. A commercial link, *if ever enabled*, should make its potential commission conspicuous beside the specific recommendation. The FTC's endorsement guidance expects transparent material connections, and its health-product guidance calls for adequate support before express or implied health claims are made. [S6][S7]

A productive editorial question is: if the publisher did not earn money when a reader clicked, would the article still be worth reading? Here it should. The experimental story, research limits, and free decision ledger are the core. A recommendation is a separate, conditional appendix.

For the moment, no Final Vestige-specific Inspire3 approval, permitted product/channel list, issued earning link, referred-checkout terms or paid settlement has been authenticated. The purchase button in the unpublished companion is therefore **disabled**. No affiliate click and certainly no paid sale is implied by the existence of this manuscript.

## IX. What the experiment did not prove

Choice blindness is not the discovery that free will is an illusion. It is not a reliable method to hypnotize unwilling people, nor evidence that consumers are helpless in the face of marketing. It does not tell us that all past-life memories, visions, reports of trance, or eyewitness recollections are false. And it cannot validate a specific audio product because no such product was tested.

The work is instead a lesson in documentary hygiene: keep the original response, check the presented outcome, inspect the change, and ask whether the explanation was elicited before or after the switch.

A good archive protects the original. A good experiment protects the measurement. A good commercial guide should protect the buyer's ability to recognize what they are choosing.

When the jam comes back across the counter, the most valuable question may be the simplest: **is this still the thing I picked?**
