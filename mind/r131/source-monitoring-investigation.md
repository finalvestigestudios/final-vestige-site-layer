# The Memory That Borrowed Its Source
**An investigation into source monitoring, imagination, and the history of suggestion**  
**Editorial status:** unpublished draft • November 4, 2026  
**Final Vestige Studios / Mind & Perception**

*You can remember a sentence clearly, know exactly what it said, and still be wrong about where you heard it.*

Suppose a friend tells you, over coffee, that a once-famous experiment demonstrated a remarkable mental power. Later, you read a sober explanation of why that experiment did not establish what the friend believed. Months pass. The explanation fades, but the vivid anecdote remains. When the claim turns up again, it seems familiar; you can almost hear the words. There is one question the feeling of familiarity cannot answer: **where did this belief come from?**

Memory does more than retain information. It supplies a story about the information's origin. Was that detail in the original newspaper? In the later documentary? In something you imagined? Or in an explanation you made up to fill a gap? Such judgments are usually useful. They also fail, sometimes in predictable ways.

This is not evidence that memory is useless. It is an argument for distinguishing what a person remembers from how a person *attributes* that memory.

## An archive with an invisible label

A book pulled from a library shelf carries a call number. A recollection usually does not. We may remember the taste of something, the cadence of a sentence, or the outline of a face without retaining a reliable receipt for where the impression originated.

Psychologists Marcia Johnson, Shahin Hashtroudi, and D. Stephen Lindsay offered a foundational synthesis of **source monitoring** in 1993. Their framework treats source judgments as inferences drawn from properties of remembered experiences: sensory details, context, emotional tone, and indications of the mental operations that produced an impression [1]. A memory rich in sights and sounds may seem externally experienced; an impression marked by conscious effort may be categorized as imagined. But the cues overlap.

Notice the subtlety: this is not an on/off switch between a genuine and a false memory. A correctly remembered phrase might be credited to the wrong speaker; a reliable description might be remembered as a firsthand experience when it was secondhand. The words can survive while their provenance drifts.

Our habits of explanation can aggravate this problem. A fluent sentence sounds as though it arrived with authority. Online, a repeated statement can wear the visual costume of a source—screenshots, confident captions, a reused historical photograph—without acquiring any independent evidence.

## The action that was only imagined

Lyn Goff and Henry Roediger reported two experiments on what researchers call **imagination inflation** in 1998 [2]. Participants encountered simple descriptions of actions. At a later phase, some of those actions were repeatedly *imagined*. During the final memory test, participants sometimes reported that they had performed actions in the original phase when they had not. More repeated imaginings increased this kind of mistaken recollection under the study conditions.

That is a narrow but consequential finding. It does not demonstrate that imagining anything can manufacture a complete autobiographical life. It does establish that rehearsal may alter later source attribution: a simulated event can pick up some of the subjective qualities that normally accompany a performed event.

Two years earlier, Maryanne Garry, Charles Manning, Elizabeth Loftus, and Steven Sherman had investigated whether imagining childhood events could increase confidence that those events occurred [3]. Their results supported an imagination-inflation effect for certain items and procedures. Again, neither study permits the sweeping conclusion that imagination invariably becomes false memory. Effects depend on the task, the question, the event, and the individual.

A later study by Matthew Dobson and Roslyn Markham explored source monitoring after participants viewed a crime film and read a written account that introduced additional details [4]. Participants with more vivid self-reported imagery were not simply worse at recognizing all items; the interesting distinction appeared in attributing items to the film or to the written description. **Recognition and source identification are different judgments.**

These studies explain one reason to be careful when a persuasive speaker asks an audience to picture an event repeatedly and then treats the resulting vividness as verification.

## An experiment you should not overinterpret

Try a deliberately ordinary demonstration with two volunteers. The first person writes six commonplace statements on paper, each beside a source label such as “newspaper,” “friend,” or “advertisement.” The second reads the list once. After several minutes, the second person tries two tasks separately: first, recall the statements; second, assign each to the original label. Compare the two columns and record mistakes.

This is an **informal teaching demonstration**, not a validated psychological test. Do not use it to diagnose anyone, manipulate a real memory, or infer that a person's account of a serious event must be wrong. It reveals the *question* source-monitoring researchers ask, not the magnitude of the effect in a particular population.

If the second person remembers a statement but confuses its label, the distinction becomes palpable. The information can feel retrieved without its provenance being secure. A more careful experiment would counterbalance the lists, control the delay, randomize stimuli, and compare results across multiple people. Our kitchen-table version does none of those things.

## From mesmerism to the modern recording

Final Vestige's [Puységur Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) examine a historical world in which attention, expectation, authority, and suggestion occupied the center of remarkable claims. That historical record is valuable precisely because the witnesses and practitioners took what they saw seriously. Their sincerity does not, by itself, establish the mechanisms they proposed.

Modern popular culture often links hypnosis, guided meditation, and subliminal recordings under the vast umbrella of “the subconscious.” But those are not interchangeable interventions. The existence of research on source monitoring does not prove that a commercial hypnotic audio file implant memories. Equally, historic testimony that someone experienced an unusual state does not demonstrate that the fluid theories associated with animal magnetism were physically correct.

There is a temptation to turn each new study into a key for every old mystery. Resist it. The responsible connection is more modest: when people describe extraordinary mental events, investigators should separate **what was observed**, **what participants reported**, and **what later interpreters inferred**. A historical source and a scientific paper can illuminate each other without one being used to manufacture conclusions for the other.

If you encounter a confident modern seller claiming to “rewrite the subconscious,” ask what was actually measured. Was it a randomized study of that exact product? A survey? A testimonial? An EEG reading? A collection of studies about different practices? The presence of scientific vocabulary is not itself scientific substantiation.

## The two receipts of a claim

Dougherty and Franco-Watkins reported four experiments in 2003 showing that frequency judgments about one list could be contaminated by information from another; manipulations that improved source monitoring could improve accuracy under tested conditions [5]. Their findings matter in an age of screenshots, stitched videos, and near-identical posts.

Consider a claim that appears on seven accounts. Seven appearances may represent seven independent researchers. Or they may represent a single marketing sentence copied six times. The number of encounters is not the number of sources.

A practical provenance audit can begin with four questions:

1. **First encounter:** Where did I originally encounter this statement, as best I can tell?
2. **Primary record:** Is there an original experiment, archival document, firsthand transcript, or official product description?
3. **Independent replication:** Are later writers citing independent evidence, or repeating a shared paragraph?
4. **Claim boundary:** Does the source establish the specific conclusion being sold, or only a narrower observation?

Write down the answers before watching another promotional clip. A link trail is imperfect, but it is better than relying on how naturally a statement rings in the mind.

## What the evidence does not say

Source-monitoring research does not imply that firsthand accounts are always unreliable or that all suggestion is harmful. Nor does it show that all meditation, imaginative exercises, or guided audio experiences are manipulative. Many people use quiet listening as a personally valued routine. Such subjective experience is a different claim from a seller's assertion of scientifically demonstrated effects.

A person can find a recording pleasant without its underlying marketing theory being true. And a researcher can find a measurable cognitive phenomenon without knowing whether it transfers to a real-life goal.

This distinction is essential because people searching for relief or greater focus are often encountering sales copy at the exact point of vulnerability. Making a choice requires knowing which benefits have been observed, in whom, under what conditions, and with what limitations.

## A more durable kind of certainty

An archive is not merely a storehouse of interesting claims. Its job is to preserve the labels: who said what, when, using which evidence, and what the record could reasonably support.

The next time an idea returns with the confidence of something long known, don't immediately dismiss it or accept it. Ask for its provenance. The most powerful part of the recollection may not be its content, but the question that follows: **how did I come to know this?**
