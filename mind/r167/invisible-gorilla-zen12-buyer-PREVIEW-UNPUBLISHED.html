01 / What we miss

The gorilla wasn't invisible.

A task can organize what you notice—and what you never report. The surprise isn't proof that a meditation recording will fix it.

Research desk

What the experiment actually established

Task-dependent surprises

Simons and Chabris (1999) investigated unexpected events during dynamic selective-attention tasks. Detection depended in part on similarity to attended objects and task difficulty—not on a universal inability to see.

Primary paper

The expert-observer caveat

In 2013, researchers inserted an artificial gorilla into a CT search task. 83% of 24 radiologists did not report it. This is not the same as a cancer miss rate or a clinical outcome.

Primary paper

The 2010 follow-up found that people who knew to look for a gorilla were not necessarily better at noticing a changed curtain or a player leaving the scene. Familiarity with one trick isn't general-purpose training. Read the original report.

Try a harmless observational prompt: Choose your own non-sensitive photo, count circles, then do a second pass for written words. Different instructions yield different inventories. This is a reflective exercise—not a replication of the gorilla study or a diagnostic assessment.

Potential value
