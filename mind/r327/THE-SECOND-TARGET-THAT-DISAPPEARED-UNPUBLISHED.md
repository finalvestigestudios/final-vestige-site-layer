# The Second Target That Disappeared: How Attention Can Lose a Fraction of a Second

*Final Vestige Studios · Mind & Perception Archive · Original investigation · Unpublished editorial draft · 12 November 2026*

**The question:** If a second visual target appears in plain sight, a few tenths of a second after the first, why might an attentive observer fail to report it?

Imagine a conveyor belt of letters on a screen. They are not moving across the room. Each replaces the last in exactly the same position. You have been told to identify the first target and then catch the second. Both are there. The computer keeps a record of the order. You may identify the first correctly and still report that the second never appeared.

Nothing went wrong with the display. The second item did not necessarily disappear from your eyes. The intriguing failure occurs between incoming information, attention, memory, and the eventual answer. Researchers call it **the attentional blink**. The term is vivid, but misleading if taken literally: it is not an eyelid blink, and the relevant deficit does not always happen.

This distinction matters at Final Vestige, where a witnessed event, a recorded event, and an observer's account are three different kinds of evidence. It also matters to the commerce side of the site. A laboratory deficit in recognizing two rapid targets is not proof that any meditation soundtrack will enhance concentration. [S1][S2]

## A letter appears. Then another. One is easier to lose.

In August 1992, Jane Raymond, Kimron Shapiro, and Karen Arnell reported an experiment using *rapid serial visual presentation*, or RSVP. Items appeared quickly, one after another, at a shared location. A participant had to identify a partly specified target letter and then detect a probe letter. This arrangement produced an unexpected temporal profile.

The first target could be identified accurately, while detection of the later probe suffered over a window that the authors described as **270 milliseconds beginning 180 milliseconds after the target**. Probes arriving immediately after the target, or later in the stream, were more accurately detected. Removing the demand to identify the initial target, or inserting a brief blank interval after it, altered the effect. The researchers proposed interference at a letter-recognition stage while initial target processing continued. [S1]

Consider the exact nature of that result. It does not mean that vision shuts down for everyone for a universal 270 milliseconds, or that attention becomes blind to everything. It means that under particular stimulus, response, and timing conditions, the probability of *reporting a second target* was depressed. The first target, the probe, and intervening items were precisely controlled. The task was about identifiable letters, not a claim about all observation.

That is already a more interesting story than the familiar shorthand that the brain 'misses the next half-second.' The original experiment's dependence on task instructions and a blank interval implies that the system is sensitive to how information is grouped and processed.

## Seven experiments complicated the story

Two years later, Shapiro, Raymond, and Arnell reported seven further experiments. Their central finding was that the blink appeared under tasks requiring attention to an object, whereas a task based on identifying a temporal interval did not produce the same deficit. Their results led them to offer a later-stage selection account instead of the earlier explanation. [S2]

This is what good experimental history looks like: an influential result becomes a more carefully delimited result after its inventors and colleagues try different task designs.

The 1995 experiments of Marvin Chun and Mary Potter added a second important layer. When first and second targets appeared among distracting items, the second often went unreported when it followed roughly **200–500 milliseconds** after the first. The authors examined immediate post-target interference, discriminability, and the effects of distractors. Their proposed two-stage account separated relatively rapid analysis from a more limited process by which a target becomes stable enough for explicit report. [S3]

One useful analogy is a newsroom. Reports arrive continuously, but only certain ones are selected for verification and filing. If one report is being processed, the next can arrive and still fail to make it into the register. This is a metaphor, not a statement that brains literally have reporters or a single fixed filing desk. It helps separate *something being presented* from *something being available to report afterward*.

## The strange exception: the very next item

A simplistic bottleneck would predict that the closer the second target appears to the first, the worse recognition must always become. Yet the literature documents an important exception called **lag-1 sparing**. In some RSVP arrangements, the target immediately following the first is reported relatively well; detection or identification worsens for items that come shortly afterward, and recovers at longer lags. This temporal dip is central to the question of what a 'blink' actually explains. [S3][S4]

Lag-1 sparing is not a universal trick. It depends on such features as target location and the structure of the stream. It also makes a practical point about design: a clip in which the second item is omitted at one particular interval is not an accurate general-purpose self-test of whether your attention is 'good.'

A broad review by Brad Wyble and colleagues traces competing explanations of selection, suppression, and consolidation and emphasizes that a pattern of reported targets alone does not uniquely identify a neural mechanism. Researchers can agree on a robust behavioral effect while disagreeing about how to explain it. [S4]

That is a distinction worth holding onto. A theory describes why the results might occur. The observed accuracy curves are the evidence to be explained.

## Some participants barely blink

In 2006, Sander Martens and colleagues described the differences between people who displayed a pronounced attentional blink and people who exhibited little or none under the studied conditions. Their work combined behavior and electrophysiology to examine how selection varied. [S5]

This is a direct reason not to diagnose strangers from a social-media demo. Even an excellent demonstration can reflect screen refresh, item size, timing, expectations, eyesight, prior familiarity, device rendering, and the design of the task. A single observer's report may be informative about that one attempt; it is not a valid test of intelligence, neurological status, creativity, suggestibility, or health.

Nor is it a stable commercial promise. The fact that performance varies does not establish that a downloadable sound recording, a particular technique, or a short course reliably improves the real-world ability to focus.

## Can training change what gets through?

Here the question becomes more delicate. In a 2007 PLOS Biology study, Heleen Slagter and colleagues investigated an intensive meditation-training intervention and the attentional blink. They reported that **three months of intensive mental training** was associated with a smaller attentional blink and a change in the P3b brain-potential response to the first target. The researchers interpreted that pattern as evidence of altered allocation of limited processing resources. [S6]

There are at least four differences between that study and a claim on a product checkout page.

First, the intervention involved intensive training over months, not listening once to a twelve-minute audio file. Second, the tested outcome was performance on a specific laboratory task, together with scalp electrical measurements; it was not a general measure of day-long productivity or medical improvement. Third, interpreting the mechanism involves theoretical assumptions about what P3b indexes. Fourth, a study of meditation training is not a clinical or commercial trial of Zen12, Hypnosis Live, or Brainwave Shots.

The study is valuable because it asks an answerable question and provides observable measurements. It is not a certificate for unrelated products. If future research establishes durable improvements from particular interventions, the claims should cite those interventions and populations precisely.

## A demonstration you can actually learn from

You do not need a purchasable app, a hypnotic recording, or a flashing-light challenge to understand the method. Here is a gentler **reading-level reconstruction** of the logic, not a validated attentional-blink test.

Ask a friend to prepare three short printed lists. In each, they place two starred target symbols among ordinary symbols. Put the two targets close together on one list, farther apart on the second, and adjacent on the third. Cover each list and briefly reveal it, then ask the reader to report the two targets. Note whether they read both correctly. Repeat in a different order. The reveal time, visual spacing and motor behavior won't mimic laboratory RSVP, so this exercise cannot demonstrate the classic temporal effect by itself. Its purpose is to show how changes in **task structure** change the reliability of reports.

For a genuinely timed RSVP test, researchers precisely control presentation duration, target spacing, distractor identity, exposure, randomization, and the scoring rule (often T2 accuracy conditional on T1 being correct). Screen refresh and accessibility matter. A self-selected internet demo cannot be read like a clinical instrument.

An even simpler observation costs nothing: when trying to read a dense paragraph, mark what you intended to look for, then separately mark what you actually noticed. This is a record of a reading task, not a diagnostic score. The useful habit is not to punish yourself for a miss; it is to ask whether the search instructions made certain evidence more salient than other evidence.

## What this has to do with a witness in 1784

The Final Vestige Library's **[Puységur’s Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/)** catalogues how an eighteenth-century system of magnetic somnambulism presented observations, operator instructions, cure claims, obedience, testimony, and source disputes. The archive contains its own historical documents and their context. [S7]

The attentional blink does not prove that Puységur's subjects experienced a modern selective-attention deficit. It cannot retroactively establish what one person perceived in a particular room in the 1780s. It can, however, give us a methodological question with which to approach a record: what was the observer asked to attend to, how quickly did events unfold, which details were recorded contemporaneously, and what was inferred after the fact?

That is a narrower and stronger connection between cognitive science and history than a sweeping claim that 'the brain invents reality.' A historically documented report remains evidence that someone *made the report*. Whether it supports the reported mechanism is a different question.

A similar principle applies to Final Vestige's [Change Blindness investigation](https://finalvestige.com/2026/11/02/change-blindness/). Change blindness concerns failures to notice changes in scenes across interruptions; attentional blink involves short-lived difficulty reporting successive targets in rapid streams. Both concern limits on reported awareness, but they are not interchangeable phenomena. [S8]

## What the science does—and does not—say about audio

The Mind & Perception Archive makes room for independent product research because some readers ask how to establish a routine of listening, guided reflection, or meditation. That is an authentic consumer question, not a license to turn cognitive studies into product testimonials.

Zen12's own site describes a twelve-level audio-meditation program with twelve-minute sessions and four styles of each level, while its checkout describes a complete 48-MP3 package. Those are **seller-described format features**. The site's assertion that one twelve-minute session brings the benefit of an hour of conventional meditation is a **seller efficacy claim**, not a conclusion supported by the attentional-blink papers cited here. [S9][S10]

Total Focus on Hypnosis Live is a distinct thirty-three-minute self-hypnosis MP3 according to its product page. Brainwave Shots' Focus Shot is described as offering fifteen- and thirty-minute versions; its dramatic immediate and prolonged concentration promises are also seller claims. They should not be imported into this investigation as research findings. [S11][S12]

An ordinary meditation routine might be appealing because a listener finds structured time, an audio guide, or a choice of ambient sounds convenient. Convenience and preference can matter even when performance-enhancement claims remain unproved. The ethical question for a buyer guide is: what exactly is supplied, at what current checkout price, under what actual terms, and with what independently supported outcomes? Until approval and verifiable terms exist, there is no active Final Vestige earning link.

The existing [Seven Day Focus Fieldbook](https://finalvestige.com/focus/) offers a free alternative: a 52-page edition with a twelve-minute task-start routine, practice log, and questions to ask before buying meditation audio. The existing signup and delivery mechanism remains the only intended lead-capture path. [S13]

## What a missing second target actually tells us

A participant may correctly identify the first letter and fail to report a second one visible fractions of a second later. In the original task, that deficit had a measurable time window. Follow-up experiments showed that distractors, task requirements, target timing, and individual differences shape the outcome. A later meditation-training study investigated whether a prolonged practice regimen could modify that pattern. These are substantial findings.

They do **not** demonstrate that witnesses are universally unreliable; that every omission reflects a brain malfunction; that a social-media stunt measures your memory; or that a particular branded audio file improves cognition.

The enduring lesson is more exacting. When testimony and a physical record disagree, do not assume the observer is lying or the equipment is perfect. Reconstruct the observation conditions. Ask how the task directed attention. Keep the original result distinct from the explanation, and both distinct from the promise somebody wants to sell you.

The second target was on the screen. Whether it survived into a person's report is another question. That gap is where the investigation begins.
