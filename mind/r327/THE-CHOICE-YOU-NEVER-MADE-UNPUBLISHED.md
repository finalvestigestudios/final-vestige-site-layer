# The Choice You Never Made: When People Defend a Decision That Was Switched

**Final Vestige Studios · Mind & Perception · Original investigation · Unpublished draft · November 12, 2026**

*The face in the researcher's hand was not the one the volunteer had selected. Yet the volunteer began explaining what was attractive about it. Two decades later, another laboratory found an important complication: some people had noticed a switch and simply hadn't said so.*

## A choice, a photograph, and the missing objection

Imagine being shown two portraits and asked which person you find more attractive. It is a small, private judgment, the kind of preference most of us expect to know. You point. The researcher hands you your selected portrait and asks why you chose it.

Except the image has been switched.

In Petter Johansson, Lars Hall, Sverker Sikström and Andreas Olsson's 2005 study, a concealed manipulation let an experimenter present a participant with the face they had *not* chosen. The participant was then invited to explain the supposed choice. The research team reported that many participants did not report the mismatch and nevertheless supplied reasons. They named the phenomenon **choice blindness**. The research was published in *Science*, volume 310, pages 116–119. [S1]

The immediately arresting version of the story is that people cannot even tell what they want. But that is much more than the experiment proves. The more precise question is whether a person will **report** an unexpected mismatch between an intended decision and the outcome shown to them—and what their explanation means when the displayed outcome has been tampered with. The distinction sounds technical until a later experiment makes it impossible to ignore.

## What the original experiment actually established

In the 2005 paradigm, the researcher controlled the presentation and used a physical switch. People had just made a choice; the researcher presented an alternative as though it were the choice. The study concerned preferences between particular face photographs, under the conditions of an experiment designed around a surprise and a social interaction.

This was not a study of whether people ordinarily remember every face. It was not a randomized trial of therapy, persuasion or meditation. It was not evidence that an operator could implant a stable belief at will. And a participant's elaborate explanation was not a reliable window into the psychological mechanism behind their initial preference.

The original authors interpreted the findings as evidence that an apparent connection between choice and introspection can sometimes be disrupted. That is the memorable discovery: an explanation can sound coherent even when the outcome being explained is not the one a person picked. [S1]

A coherent story is not the same thing as a verified cause. A subject might explain a displayed photo because they have accepted the researcher's premise, because they do not wish to interrupt, because they misunderstood, or because the mismatch really escaped conscious awareness. One exchange does not separate those possibilities.

Nor would it be correct to say that everyone failed. The 2005 abstract states that participants failed to detect *some* conspicuous mismatches. A later paper summarizes the original study as having **13% immediate verbal detection of manipulated trials**, a figure specific to that paradigm rather than a general measure of human decision-making. [S4]

## The second question: what if the memory was intact?

For years, the most intuitive explanation was simple forgetting. Maybe the original choice evaporates from memory before the false outcome appears.

Anna Sagana, Melanie Sauerland, Harald Merckelbach and colleagues examined the relationship between choice blindness and memory. Their 2014 paper, titled *Memory impairment is not sufficient for choice blindness to occur*, offers a crucial distinction: a person may remember the original selection reasonably accurately and still fail to flag the substitution when it appears. [S2]

The authors considered more than one account. An unexpected outcome might interfere with recognition of the mismatch. A participant might accept the given result and generate a plausible explanation while still retaining information about what they originally selected. The study did not settle which account is correct in every situation. It did, however, undercut the lazy shorthand that choice blindness must simply be a bad memory.

The paper also acknowledged constraints in its own retrospective detection measurement, including the use of descriptions rather than the original photographs in a post-test. Those details are not weaknesses to bury. They determine how much the result can bear.

It helps to distinguish three questions that everyday conversation lumps together: Did you actually choose the item? Did you notice it had changed? Did you decide to speak up? None is a perfect proxy for the others.

## The 2025 complication: silence may hide detection

In 2025, researchers publishing in *Frontiers in Psychology* used behavioral measures and pupillometry in **two computerized choice-blindness experiments**. They investigated whether not reporting a substituted outcome should automatically be classified as not detecting it. Their title announced the question plainly: *Restoring sight in choice blindness: pupillometry and behavioral evidence of covert detection*. [S3]

The result complicates an easy headline. Across these two experiments, the authors estimated that **135 of 206 unreported manipulated trials (65.5%) represented covert detection**. Put differently, in their tasks some participants gave no immediate verbal warning yet showed evidence of detecting the mismatch.

This is not the same as saying that 65.5% of the general public secretly notices every switch. The denominator was a specific set of *unreported manipulated trials* in these experiments; the estimate depends on the authors' operational definition of covert detection. Pupillary changes and later responses require interpretation. The paper's authors themselves cautioned that computerized tasks do not map perfectly onto physical sleight-of-hand experiments.

There is a social problem inside the measurement. To object, the participant may have to challenge the task or imply that the researcher or computer has made an error. People differ in how readily they do that. In the 2025 report, some apparently withheld objection, waited for more information, or treated the switch as a feature of the experiment. [S3]

This offers a richer account than the claim that a brain simply fails to see. At least under some conditions, an experimenter may mistake **unreported awareness** for **absence of awareness**. The act of asking and the social situation become part of the phenomenon.

The original 2005 result remains important: explanations can be produced for outcomes that do not match a prior selection. But it is less defensible to describe every silent participant as unaware of the manipulation.

## A demonstration that does not deceive anyone

You can examine the distinction without borrowing the researchers' deception.

Take two slips of paper and write the names of two neutral activities—say, “walk outside for ten minutes” and “read a short article.” Decide privately which you prefer for this afternoon. Before writing a reason, ask yourself three separate questions:

1. Which one did I choose?
2. What concrete evidence would let me verify that choice later?
3. If someone handed me a card naming the other activity, would I correct them immediately, hesitate, or ask why they had the other card?

Record your answers. There is no hidden switch, no other person manipulating the outcome and no performance score. This is a **reflection exercise**, not a replication of choice blindness and not a diagnostic assessment. It illustrates why remembering, detecting and reporting are separate events.

A stronger classroom demonstration would make its rules explicit and obtain informed consent before any experimental substitution. A reproducible study would need preregistered detection criteria, appropriate controls, meaningful sample size, and care in distinguishing immediate objections from later reports. The spectacle of a card trick alone is not the evidence.

## From a swapped portrait to an eighteenth-century witness file

Final Vestige's [Puységur Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) asks a related methodological question in a different century. Puységur gathered case narratives, witnesses, spoken responses, predictions, notarial records and failures as he built an argument for a disputed therapeutic doctrine. The archive preserves the conditions around the testimony—including hierarchy, vulnerable patients, the operator's expectations and explicit warnings about suggestible or pressured answers. [S5]

A notarized event may be better documented than an undocumented rumor. It does not follow that the proposed magnetic mechanism caused it. Likewise, a participant's explanation of a swapped portrait can be faithfully transcribed without proving the participant lacked all awareness that the portrait was wrong.

The comparison concerns **how evidence is made**, not an alleged shared force across centuries. Nothing in the 2005, 2014 or 2025 choice-blindness experiments demonstrates animal magnetism, thought transmission, modern hypnosis efficacy, repressed memory recovery or control over unwilling people.

The ethical thread matters. In a research room, the person who controls the materials and frames the question can influence what a respondent feels permitted to say. In the archive, the person who conducts the treatment may also record the response. In both settings, separating the operator's interpretation from the subject's observed words helps protect the record.

## Why polished explanations can mislead

People often expect a personal explanation to function like a receipt: it proves the decision that came before it. Choice-blindness studies show why the analogy breaks down. A reason can be supplied *after* an outcome is displayed, and a person can speak persuasively about the displayed result.

This does not mean reasons are useless. It means the evidence for what happened should include the initial choice record, the manner of presentation, the potential mismatch and the conditions under which objections were invited or discouraged.

In practical terms, three safeguards travel well beyond a psychology lab.

**Record the original decision.** Before reviewing a recommendation or purchase, jot down what you intended and why. That creates a comparison point independent of how an option is later framed.

**Separate observation from explanation.** “I was handed this photo” is not “I chose this photo.” “I didn't object aloud” is not “I didn't notice.”

**Make correction socially easy.** If you ask a customer, learner or witness whether an outcome was what they wanted, give them permission to say no without embarrassment. The 2025 work makes that seemingly small design choice scientifically interesting.

None of these safeguards guarantees immunity from suggestion or decision errors. They improve the quality of the questions we ask.

## And what about meditation and commercial focus audio?

There is a tempting jump from “people can misread their decisions” to “a guided recording can change what people believe” to “this particular meditation program produces measurable brain changes.” Each link in that chain requires evidence of its own.

The first claim concerns experimental reports of choice manipulation. The second concerns particular mechanisms of suggestion and learning. The third concerns a branded product, a defined outcome, and comparable, independently checked tests. There is no such bridge in these choice-blindness papers.

Final Vestige's [Seven Day Focus Fieldbook](https://finalvestige.com/focus/) is a free, 52-page practical tool for planning a twelve-minute work session and keeping a record of what happened. Its own page notes that the supporting behavioral research did not test this exact fieldbook. It also offers an audio-claims checklist. It is a useful alternative to buying any recording simply because its marketing sounds scientific. [S6]

Our separate, **unpublished** Zen12 evaluation describes what the seller actually provides and which efficacy claims remain unsupported. No approved earning link is active. Readers do not need to subscribe or buy anything to read the underlying evidence.

The most useful lesson from the swapped portrait is not that the mind is hopelessly unreliable. It is that a convincing explanation is a kind of evidence—but **not evidence for every claim we want it to carry**. When a response is ambiguous, the right answer is not to write a grander story. It is to improve the record.
