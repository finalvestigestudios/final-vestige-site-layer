# The Question That Changed the Crash: How Suggestion Enters Memory
**Final Vestige Studios · Mind & Perception / History of Suggestion · UNPUBLISHED research draft · 6 November 2026**

*An eyewitness watches a car crash. The film does not change. The question does. A week later, a detail that never appeared in the film begins to show up in the answers.*

## A word, a week, and glass that was never there

Imagine a person leaving a screening room with a reasonably clear recollection of a traffic accident. They saw the vehicles; they heard no new evidence after the film ended. The researcher asks a question about the speed at which the cars collided. The only adjustment between groups is a verb: perhaps the cars *hit* one another, perhaps they *smashed* into each other. Those are not interchangeable pieces of description. One suggests a harder collision.

A week passes. The researchers ask if the participants saw broken glass. The film contained none. Yet among people who previously heard the *smashed* question, sixteen of fifty said yes; among those who heard *hit*, seven of fifty said yes. Six of fifty in a group not asked the speed question said yes. These are the results of the second experiment reported by Elizabeth Loftus and John Palmer in 1974—not a contemporary social-media demonstration. [1]

The result is not that a question can always plant a rich, permanent autobiographical event. It is narrower, stranger and more useful: the wording of a post-event question affected later reports about a particular visual detail. Even the control group was not immune to error. A memory report can be sincerely produced and still fail to preserve exactly what appeared on screen.

The uncomfortable part is that no one needed to tell the participants to lie. A suggestion entered through ordinary interview language.

## The first experiment: a collision described five ways

Loftus and Palmer's original paper reported two experiments. In the first, 45 students watched films of motor-vehicle accidents and estimated the vehicles' speed after hearing questions that used different impact verbs. The *smashed* wording elicited higher estimates than verbs such as *contacted* or *hit*. [1]

What can that prove? It establishes that framing in a question can influence an estimate. On its own it cannot show whether the underlying memory changed, whether a participant interpreted the verb as an indication of severity, or whether the person selected an answer thought appropriate for the question. Those are competing explanations that matter to anyone reconstructing an event.

The second experiment added the delayed broken-glass question because the researchers wanted to examine whether the effect could extend beyond immediate speed estimates. That move made the finding important, but it did not transform a laboratory report into a universal theory of every recollection.

The usual retelling says that memory is a video recorder that gets overwritten. It is a useful image until it stops being accurate. Remembering involves reconstructing an event from partial traces, expectations and later information. Researchers still debate the precise processes behind any one error: changes in the stored representation, accessibility, source confusion and test-specific response tendencies can contribute in different circumstances. An experiment showing a false report is not direct observation of a memory trace being erased.

## The stop sign that entered after the fact

The next chapter did not require moving pictures. In a 1978 research program, Loftus, David Miller and Helen Burns showed slides of an auto–pedestrian accident to 1,242 people across five experiments and a pilot study. After the slides, some encountered consistent descriptions; others encountered misleading verbal information. The misleading material impaired accuracy on both yes/no tests and forced-choice recognition. The effect was larger when misinformation was introduced closer to the final test than immediately after the original event. [2]

The study is commonly illustrated with an intersection sign that a later question describes differently from the sign originally shown. What matters is not the familiar classroom image of a stop sign morphing into a yield sign; it is the experimentally varied order of seeing, reading and remembering.

That research makes one further mistake harder to sustain: that people must consciously agree with a misleading account for it to affect their answers. The abstract reports that the findings were not adequately explained by a simple demand-characteristics account. But that conclusion does not exclude every kind of response bias or settle the mechanism for all misinformation paradigms.

Why does the delay matter? A remembered episode does not arrive with a perfect receipt listing which details came from the original scene and which arrived in a later conversation. Often we have to reconstruct that receipt.

## Whose voice supplied the detail?

Marcia Johnson, Shahin Hashtroudi and D. Stephen Lindsay called this problem **source monitoring** in a major 1993 review. [3] When a person evaluates whether a recollection came from perception, imagination, conversation or reading, they are making a source judgment. Familiarity is not the same as accurate provenance.

Think of an archival photograph. The date handwritten on its envelope may come from the person who took the picture, an archivist decades later or a relative guessing from clothing. A skilled investigator does not discard the photograph because its label could be mistaken. They distinguish the artifact from the later caption and investigate the chain of custody.

Memory deserves the same discipline. 'I can picture it' describes an experience. 'I saw it occur' is a claim about origin. The first does not automatically verify the second.

More recent work complicates any simple story in which repeated testimony from different witnesses necessarily adds separate persuasive force. In 2023, O'Donnell and colleagues reported two experiments and meta-analyses: repetition of misinformation increased its influence, but varying the apparent number of misinformation sources did not show an independent increase in their designs. [4] This is evidence for a particular mechanism in particular tasks, not a license to ignore corroboration in real investigations. Genuine independent records remain important; copied or repeated claims should not be mistaken for independent support.

## The forgotten link to animal magnetism

Final Vestige's [Puységur Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) traces a much older history of suggestion, authority, expectation and altered experience. In late-eighteenth-century accounts of magnetic somnambulism, claims about extraordinary perception were entangled with the settings in which practitioners questioned their subjects. Those accounts are historical evidence that people made and interpreted such claims. They are not controlled demonstrations that suggestion produces supernatural knowledge.

The connection to modern misinformation experiments is a methodological one, not a declaration that two centuries of practice were secretly the same phenomenon. What does the examiner know? What is the participant told? What possibilities are supplied before an answer? Can the final account be checked against evidence that existed before the interview?

These questions are equally relevant to archives, the history of hypnosis and a viral claim about remembering a supposedly hidden past.

The caution should not be flipped into its opposite. Suggestion is not always harmful, and laboratory findings about misleading questions do not imply that all hypnosis experiences are false. A 1997 review by John Kihlstrom cautioned specifically against treating hypnotically enhanced recall as a more accurate reconstruction of the past: higher confidence and additional recollections need not mean better truth. [5] Separately, Lynn and colleagues' 2020 review of hypnosis misconceptions emphasizes nuance about hypnotic experience and responsiveness. [6]

The practical boundary is sharp: do not use a recording, exercise or guided suggestion as a tool for certifying historical facts about yourself or someone else. An emotionally vivid image is not provenance.

## A safe demonstration: the two-caption exercise

You can try an original observation exercise without inducing memories or misleading another person.

**Step one.** Find an ordinary image you own—a desk, garden, street or still life. Study it for thirty seconds, then hide it.

**Step two.** On paper, list five concrete things you remember. Mark each entry 'saw', 'inferred' or 'unsure'. Make this classification before checking.

**Step three.** Write two alternative captions about the same image: a neutral caption ('The table has objects on it') and an interpretive caption ('The cluttered table suggests someone left in a hurry'). Do not pretend the interpretive caption is a fact; notice which words imply information the picture alone does not contain.

**Step four.** Return to the image. Circle the observations actually supported by it. Ask whether the second caption would have steered your recollection if you had heard it first.

This is a reflective exercise, not a scientific recreation of Loftus and Palmer. There is no control group, random assignment, blinded scoring or inference about anyone else's memory reliability. Its purpose is to separate observation from interpretation in a tangible way.

For the next seven days, a useful practice is simply to annotate a claim with its origin: *I saw*, *someone said*, *I inferred*, *I checked*. That habit transfers to screenshots, family stories, archival evidence and the 'surely I remember' moments of ordinary life.

## What the experiments do not establish

These studies did not test the effectiveness of any particular commercial meditation MP3, self-hypnosis recording, brainwave-synchronization package or mindfulness course. They do not show that buying audio reliably improves memory, focus, psychological health or wealth. They also do not show that nonclinical exercises are useless. Those are separate claims requiring separate evidence.

A seller can describe what an audio library contains; that is a product-feature question. Demonstrating that the library changes recall accuracy or cognition is an empirical efficacy question. The difference is easy to lose when promotional language places the words *brain*, *science* and *proven* next to one another.

The same separation governs historical investigation. Puységur's documents can establish what a participant recorded in a particular century. They cannot, without more evidence, verify the powers attributed to a subject. The crash experiments can establish systematic differences in responses in particular laboratory conditions. They cannot prove that every witness will become unreliable if given a leading question.

There is another limit: eyewitness performance depends on attention at encoding, intervening information, time, questioning, the kind of target detail and many other variables. None of these studies supplies an individual lie detector. It would be an abuse of the literature to dismiss a particular victim, witness or person's account solely because memory is reconstructive.

## The important question is not whether you remember

The most consequential word in this body of research may not be *smashed*. It may be *source*.

A responsible historian asks which document supports the claim. A responsible researcher asks which result supports the inference. And a responsible reader can ask of a memory: where did that detail come from?

In 1974, the cars in the film were fixed. The interviewing language was not. More than fifty years later, when people argue about what a photograph shows, a witness said or a recording proves, that difference still matters. Evidence is what lets us notice when the explanation has acquired a detail the original record never contained.
