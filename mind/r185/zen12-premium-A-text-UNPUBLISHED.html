02 / A SMALL EXPERIMENT IN LANGUAGE

A question can change what people say they remember. That is not an audio trial.

In 1974, Loftus and Palmer varied just one verb while asking viewers about a filmed accident. In a follow-up, the group that heard a more forceful verb reported nonexistent broken glass more often than comparison groups. Memory researchers did not test Zen12, headphones, meditation equivalence or purchase decisions. The lesson for this page is about careful claims: dramatic vocabulary can shape an expectation even when the underlying evidence does not change.

03 / TRY BEFORE YOU COMMIT

Your three-session, twelve-minute fit test

Before considering a purchase, give yourself three ordinary twelve-minute blocks on different days. Use a basic timer and either quiet or everyday background sound you already have access to. Choose a neutral activity—sit comfortably, notice the breath, or simply observe when the mind wanders. No special state must occur.

Session one: friction

Write down what made starting difficult: time, location, volume, or interruptions. Avoid grading your mental performance. You are testing whether the schedule is sustainable.

Sessions two and three: preference

Repeat the routine. Did you prefer instructions, ambient sound or quiet? Would having 48 recordings make you more likely to return—or simply give you more choices than you want?

This is a personal preference check, not a controlled psychological experiment or test of Zen12 effectiveness. Do not use a guided track to recover, verify or diagnose personal memories.
