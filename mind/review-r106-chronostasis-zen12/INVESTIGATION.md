# The Second That Would Not End
*Mind & Perception · Original investigation · Editorial draft, not published*

**Deck:** On a clock face, a second sometimes appears to last too long. In a laboratory, that small discrepancy opened a much larger question: how does the mind make the visual world feel continuous when the eyes keep jumping?

## The clock on the wall

Imagine entering a room and turning toward a silent analog clock. The second hand seems frozen. For an oddly extended moment it refuses to move; then the clock resumes its perfectly ordinary rhythm. Nothing in the mechanism needed repairing. Nothing in the clock's measurement of time changed. The strange interval belonged to the observer.

This ordinary experience has a technical name, **chronostasis**, sometimes described as the stopped-clock illusion. It offers an unusually economical entry into a large subject: the gap between when an event happens and when the brain treats that event as having begun.

A story about a clock might sound like a story about time perception alone. It is also a story about the movement of the eyes. We do not take in a scene like a stationary camera. We make rapid jumps, called *saccades*, from one selected point to another. Those jumps alter the sensory input reaching our visual systems. Yet the room does not seem to spin each time we look across it. A reasonably stable world is experienced despite an unstable stream of retinal images.

The important question is not why a clock sometimes fools us. It is what that little illusion reveals—and what it does not.

## A laboratory version of a familiar moment

In November 2001, Kielan Yarrow, Patrick Haggard, Ron Heal, Peter Brown and John Rothwell published an experiment in *Nature* with the meticulous title *Illusory perceptions of space and time preserve cross-saccadic perceptual continuity*. The researchers did not have to rely on stories about broken clocks. They constructed a controlled task in which a participant moved their eyes to a target and compared the apparent duration of the first post-movement interval with subsequent intervals.

The critical manipulation was simple in principle. The physical duration of a displayed digit could be varied while people reported whether it seemed longer or shorter than a reference interval. A shift of gaze toward the target could make that initial interval seem extended. The researchers' account was that perception of the target was effectively *backdated* across a brief portion of the eye movement, as if the percept had started before the information was fully available in its new location. [1]

That is an inference supported by their experimental pattern, not a film of a clock inside a person's head. The distinction matters. An experiment measures judgments and manipulates the conditions under which those judgments change. It does not give direct access to the precise texture of a person's experience.

An unusually instructive control involved the apparent position of the target. When the target moved unexpectedly during the saccade so that its spatial continuity was broken, the chronostasis illusion diminished or disappeared under the conditions reported. This mattered because a general story such as “eye movements always slow time” would not explain the dependence on perceived object stability. The researchers' interpretation tied the temporal distortion to the visual system's efforts to maintain a coherent external world. [1]

A 2002 discussion by Kai Thilo and Vincent Walsh gave the findings an accessible framing: the brain appears to make a short-lived adjustment to the perceived beginning of an event after a rapid shift of gaze. The discussion described why controls that separated eye movements from merely shifting attention were important. It also spelled out the limits of what the experiments could demonstrate. [2]

The sensation of a stopped hand is persuasive because we do not normally observe the machinery that makes perception continuous. We see the output, and then assume the output is a direct measurement.

## What happens during a saccade?

Try moving your gaze quickly between two opposite corners of the room. You do not ordinarily experience the blur you might expect from a video camera sweeping across the same distance. Part of the explanation involves reduced visual sensitivity during saccades, often called *saccadic suppression*, alongside mechanisms that help match objects across successive fixations.

It would be a mistake to say that consciousness “switches off” between eye movements. The processes are more complicated, and a familiar shorthand can oversell a mechanism. What matters for chronostasis is that the visible world remains subjectively stable even though its incoming visual information is discontinuous and actively sampled.

Nor should the word *backdating* be interpreted as a literal time machine. Yarrow and colleagues inferred a change in perceived onset from patterns of duration judgment. The physical second does not expand. The experiment does not show that an individual's memories of every event are retroactively rewritten. It identifies a particular distortion in a particular task, with a specific relationship to eye movement and apparent target continuity. [1]

The stopped-clock effect is a useful example of why investigators distinguish **stimulus duration**, **reported duration** and **the explanation for a difference between them**. Those are three different claims. A measured difference in reported duration does not automatically decide which theoretical account is complete.

## Then the telephone complicated the story

If the illusion depended entirely on visual mechanisms and moving eyes, would it vanish in other senses?

Not quite. In October 2002, Iona Hodinott-Hill, Kai Thilo, Alan Cowey and Vincent Walsh published a study titled *Auditory chronostasis: hanging on the telephone*. Rather than asking participants to stare at a clock, they asked people to judge a silent interval between sounds delivered through headphones. The crucial comparison involved shifts of auditory attention from one ear to the other. Their findings suggested an analogous distortion in the auditory domain. [3]

This was a meaningful complication. It undermined the broad claim that chronostasis must be unique to the movement of the eyes. The authors considered the result compatible with a more general account of temporal estimation, although the auditory and visual experiments were not identical in their demands. The existence of an analogous effect is not proof of one unified neural clock.

There is a temptation to compress the entire history into “scientists discovered the brain lies about time.” That sentence gets the drama and loses the research. A careful account is more interesting: one group identified a reproducible discrepancy in timing judgments linked to saccades and visual stability; another found a comparable timing distortion during shifts in auditory attention. Both results constrain theories, and neither licenses a universal claim that every experience of an unexpectedly long second shares one cause.

The telephone study also exposes a trap in popular neuroscience. Similar subjective descriptions do not prove identical mechanisms. A sound that seems late, a clock that appears stopped and a long wait in a checkout queue can all be described as time stretching. Their physical settings, attentional demands and experimental explanations are not interchangeable.

## An honest demonstration

A gentle observation exercise is possible without special software. Find a quiet analog clock with a clear second hand. Stand where you can see it easily when you look directly at it, but initially fix your gaze on a point a little to one side. Shift your eyes to the clock and notice whether the first movement of the second hand seems slower than the next few. Repeat only if comfortable.

Do not stare at intense light, force your eyes or drive while trying this. If the effect does not occur, that is an ordinary result. A clock with a sweeping hand may feel different from one that advances in discrete steps, and expectations can influence what you report. A single home observation without controlled timing is **not** a reproduction of Yarrow's experiment, a diagnosis, or a measure of concentration.

For a more meaningful personal record, write down what type of clock you used and whether you noticed the effect *before* reading an explanation. You can also note occasions where you did **not** notice anything unusual. This exercise has not been validated as a clinical or research test.

Its value is not in “proving your brain can be fooled.” Its value is learning to ask which part of a story comes from the stimulus, which comes from a report, and which comes from a proposed mechanism.

## The historical echo: observation is not explanation

The Final Vestige Library's *Puységur's Animal Magnetism Files* preserves a very different story of remarkable experiences and efforts to explain them. In the 1780s, observers reported unusual behavior under the banner of animal magnetism. Those accounts belong to their own historical setting. They should not be casually translated into twenty-first-century neuroscience.

Yet there is a productive methodological parallel. When a witness reports that an event felt extraordinary, a historian should ask what was actually recorded, who made the observation, what alternative explanations existed and which later claims outran the evidence. When a volunteer reports that the clock stopped, an experimental psychologist asks how the visual target, eye movement and duration comparison were arranged. In neither case does the vividness of a report, by itself, establish its proposed cause.

The comparison is about **standards of evidence**, not an assertion that animal magnetism and chronostasis share a mechanism. Puységur's manuscripts do not predict a modern saccade experiment; modern saccade experiments do not confirm magnetic cures. The connection lies in the discipline of resisting an appealing explanation until the evidence supports it.

For another example closer to familiar media, Final Vestige's published *Change Blindness* investigation examines a different way in which visual experience can be confidently incomplete. Change blindness concerns failures to notice alterations in a scene; chronostasis concerns duration judgments associated with shifts in gaze. The two should not be presented as one unified “glitch.” They instead show how many kinds of questions can hide behind our sense of immediate certainty.

## What the stopped clock can actually tell us

The most defensible conclusion is bounded: **under studied conditions, the apparent duration of an initial event after a shift of gaze can exceed its physical duration, and the effect depends on how the perceptual system treats target continuity**. A related auditory finding suggests that explanations restricted solely to eye movement are incomplete. [1–3]

That modest conclusion is enough. It gives us a source-backed demonstration for a TikTok video, a vivid doorway into perceptual science and a reason to treat confident descriptions of subjective experience as data rather than finished explanations.

It does not tell us that attention-training products can slow time. It does not establish a meditation benefit, a treatment for anxiety, a route to productivity, or a guaranteed way of changing consciousness. Chronostasis should not be enlisted as evidence for unrelated commercial audio products merely because both involve the mind.

A clock's hand moves again. The lesson lasts longer: what feels immediate can be assembled after the fact, and the right experiment can tell us **where** that construction becomes visible without pretending to tell us everything.
