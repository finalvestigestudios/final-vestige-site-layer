# The Choice You Did Not Make
## How a card trick, a jar of jam, and a confident explanation exposed a blind spot in introspection

**Final Vestige Studios | Mind & Perception Archive | Original unpublished editorial manuscript | 17 November 2026**

*Editorial status: source-controlled proposal, not a published WordPress post. This is a historical and scientific investigation, not treatment advice, a product endorsement, or proof for any recording sold by Inspire3.*

Imagine that you choose a face from two photographs. The experimenter turns the chosen picture toward you and asks for your reasons. You study it. You say the smile seems warm; perhaps the eyes look expressive. You are articulate, unhurried, and sincere. Except the photograph in your hand is not the one you chose.

The unsettling thing is not merely that someone failed to spot a swap. It is that, when asked to explain, a person can produce a convincing account of a decision that never occurred. What sounds like direct access to a private preference may instead be a story built around the result in front of them. That is an extraordinary claim, and it deserves the kind of precise source trail we would demand from any historical case file.

The researchers called the effect **choice blindness**. It is related to change blindness and inattentional blindness, but it is not identical to either. It concerns our capacity to notice a mismatch between **what we selected** and **what we are presented as having selected**, and the stories we tell after the mismatch. The result should make us more careful listeners to our own explanations. It does not entitle us to assume that everyone's choices are unreal, that memories are uniformly false, or that a commercial audio program can repair the problem.

### 1. A decision made in full view

In a paper published in *Science* on October 7, 2005, Petter Johansson, Lars Hall, Sverker Sikström, and Andreas Olsson investigated a basic expectation of rational choice: if the outcome of a choice does not match what we intended, will we notice? Participants saw pairs of photographs of women's faces and indicated which person they found more attractive. On selected trials the experimenters covertly manipulated the presentation so that the person was handed the photograph they had **not** selected, then asked them why they had made that supposed choice.

The paper's central finding was that people often failed to report the discrepancy and supplied explanations for the presented photograph. The scientific claim is narrower than the viral version. It was a specific face-preference task, with covert switching and immediate explanation, under a particular laboratory protocol; it was not a measure of every participant's entire capacity to know their motives. The authors' published abstract stresses that participants could miss conspicuous mismatches between choice and outcome and offer introspective reasons anyway. [S1]

That last phrase matters. These were not necessarily deliberate lies. A participant may have been trying to answer the researcher's question in good faith, explaining visible qualities of a photograph without realizing that the question contained a false premise. We do not need to imagine a mysterious mind-control force or an invisible second personality. Ordinary information processing and social expectations are enough to make the finding troubling.

The hidden manipulation was central to the experiment, but it also sets an ethical boundary. The documented procedure included debriefing. A public demonstration that secretly changes someone's answer, films embarrassment, or extracts a confession without permission is not a responsible translation of this research. Final Vestige can explain the method without turning visitors into unwitting subjects.

### 2. The explanation arrived after the evidence had changed

The choice-blindness effect is often described as if participants first knew a preference and then suddenly lost it. The data do not force that reconstruction. A person's original selection may have been weak, transient, or context dependent. The alternative photograph may also have been sufficiently acceptable to justify. Some manipulation attempts were caught. Those details resist the easy narrative that a person's desires can be rewritten at will.

Consider the sequence carefully. First comes a comparison between two photographs. Next comes an outcome, quietly altered. Only then comes the demand for a verbal explanation. By the time the participant speaks, the visible outcome has become fresh evidence. A sincere explanation can be constructed from the wrong object because the mind is answering a present question with the present scene. Confidence in a reason is not the same as verification of its history.

This is a useful point for historians. A witness who confidently describes **why** something happened may give us important testimony about their experience. It does not mean they have measured every step between event, recollection, expectation, and explanation. In an investigation, the original choice, the manipulated outcome, and the later narrative should be three separate records, not a single merged account.

There is an especially strong temptation to collapse them when a story feels coherent. Clarity can persuade the storyteller as much as the audience. One of the archive's editorial obligations is to ask what was recorded *before* the explanation took hold.

### 3. The supermarket version

A five-year-later follow-up replaced faces with ordinary goods. For a study published in *Cognition* in October 2010, Hall, Johansson, Betty Tärning, Sikström, and Thérèse Deutgen set up a tasting stand in a supermarket. Passersby sampled two jams or teas, stated a preference, and were invited to taste their chosen option again and explain why. The experimenters covertly switched sample containers, so the second taste or smell belonged to the unchosen alternative.

The published abstract reports that **no more than one third of manipulated trials were detected overall**. Even with markedly different pairs, detection remained incomplete. [S2] This was a stronger test of the general idea than simply repeating a photograph trick. Taste and smell are vivid, personal and strongly associated with preference. The result showed that a mismatch between a recent decision and a presented outcome could go unnoticed outside the original visual-choice task.

But the supermarket did not prove that taste is imaginary. Nor did it show that any producer can persuade anyone to like anything. The researchers controlled the choices and swaps within the tasting setup. Different samples, motivations, familiarity, and degrees of perceptual difference could change the result. The finding is precisely that a person's report about a choice can be less reliable than the real sequence of recorded events. That is a reason to keep records, not to sneer at consumers.

### 4. Why this is not a trick for selling something

Choice blindness has an unavoidable commercial implication. Marketing asks people to form intentions, compare alternatives, and explain purchases. If our accounts of why we choose are sometimes reconstructed, a sales page can take advantage of that uncertainty by manufacturing urgency, social proof, or a premature sense that a product has already solved a problem.

This is where Final Vestige draws a bright line. We can publish the science of choice blindness without using the science as a covert persuasion technique. We should never alter a reader's expressed selection, stage a false checkout preference, claim a product is the reader's inevitable choice, or make an anxious person feel that reluctance is evidence they need hypnosis.

In the Mind Archive, the ethical use of this work is instead to improve the reader's **decision procedure**. Pause. Name the problem you are trying to solve. Write down what each candidate promises. Identify which facts are verified by a source other than its seller. Compare alternatives before looking at discounts or testimonials. Return to the original notes after reading the offer page. These actions are not experimentally proven cures for choice blindness. They are a transparent way to preserve a record of what you actually thought.

We should also be wary of turning one strange laboratory result into a blanket argument that people cannot make informed choices. That would overstate the paper and betray the very principle it teaches.

### 5. When a choice becomes an attitude

A later 2013 study by Hall, Thomas Strandberg, Philip Pärnamets, Andreas Lind, Tärning, and Johansson applied choice-blindness methods to political attitudes and voter intentions. The study demonstrates that some respondents could accept manipulated responses and then discuss them as though they reflected their own earlier position. [S3] Here the stakes are not a preference between photographs or two jams but statements about public issues.

This extension raises questions about survey methods and persuasion. Yet a laboratory manipulation of answers is not a vote count, a long-term electoral forecast, or proof that people's political identities are easily overwritten. Studies differ in contexts and measures. The crucial insight is that the **recorded response** and the **explanation after a response is presented** can diverge. We should not leap from that finding to conspiratorial claims of generalized mental programming.

The practical recommendation is modest and actionable: when accuracy matters, preserve your first answer, explain any change openly, and label what is observation versus reconstruction. Ethical research gets stronger when it does not need deception to be recast as a superpower.

### 6. The Puységur connection: a witness can be sincere and still be incomplete

Final Vestige's existing [Puységur's Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) studies the documents built around magnetic somnambulism in the late eighteenth century. Puységur assembled repeated experiences, testimony, written predictions, case histories, signed statements and notarial acts into an argument about a purported magnetic force. The archive keeps the original-edition reference separate from its later working scan and makes failures and ethical conflicts visible. [S4]

Choice blindness does **not** explain Puységur's historical subjects, diagnose their motives, or show that any specific report was fabricated. The two bodies of evidence are centuries apart, use different designs, and concern different claims. Their point of contact is methodological: a detailed and sincere account of experience can carry valuable information without establishing the claimed mechanism. A notarized statement supports the provenance of the statement. It does not, by itself, demonstrate the existence of magnetism, supernatural sight or an irresistible power over another person's will.

The historical archive also foregrounds consent. In some case dossiers, operator influence and patients' stated wishes are in tension; in the late notes, Puységur himself worries about abuse of power and compliant answers. Modern interpretation cannot simply declare an altered-state voice more authentic than an ordinary-state refusal. The uncomfortable evidence needs to stay on the page.

That caution applies to modern hypnotherapy marketing too. A visitor describing relaxation after listening is reporting an experience; they have not thereby validated a universal neuroscience mechanism or a guarantee of therapeutic benefit.

### 7. Four distinctions before the claim becomes a headline

There are four different propositions that a careless headline can compress into one.

**Observation:** an individual failed to detect a secretly altered choice in an experimental task.

**Explanation:** an interview elicited a coherent reason for the outcome shown.

**Generalization:** similar manipulations can sometimes work with other kinds of decisions, as in the supermarket extension.

**Commercial leap:** a particular recording can therefore reprogram preferences, cure distraction, create wealth, or guarantee focus.

The first three are topics of empirical research, with important limits. The fourth does **not** follow. Neither the 2005 paper, the 2010 study, nor the 2013 extension evaluated Zen12, Hypnosis Live Total Focus or Brainwave Shots Focus Shot. No chain of citations can convert a perception experiment into a brand-specific efficacy trial.

FTC guidance requires material affiliate relationships to be disclosed clearly and conspicuously near recommendations, while health-related representations need adequate relevant evidence. [S5] [S6] If the evidence comes from a seller's own sales page, we label the statement as a seller's claim and evaluate it separately. Price, discounts, guarantees and refunds must be checked for the exact product and referred checkout; a general catalog banner is not reliable proof of a specific affiliate offer.

### 8. An honest, consent-based demonstration

This is a **free twelve-minute reflection activity**, not a recreation of the covert experimental manipulation. No one needs to record another person or expose a private preference.

**Minutes 0–2 — Make a choice.** Pick between two ordinary options, such as a quiet break or a short walk, or two activities you might do later. Write the option you initially favor and the actual reason for that choice. Date the note.

**Minutes 2–4 — Separate observation from hope.** Under the reason, write what you know to be true. In a second column, mark which claims are expectations or guesses. If this is a purchase, include the exact seller page or independent source for each factual assertion.

**Minutes 4–7 — Review the alternative.** Consider one drawback and one advantage of the other option. Avoid trying to force a changed decision. You are not proving an effect; you are making the alternatives legible.

**Minutes 7–9 — Choose again.** Make your second choice without erasing the first. If you changed your mind, note what new information mattered.

**Minutes 9–11 — Read the two accounts.** Does your second explanation accurately describe the change, or does it read as though you always believed the later choice? Either is possible. The written record is more informative than a retrospective guess alone.

**Minute 12 — Name the limitation.** This private activity cannot measure the experimental choice-blindness rate or establish that meditation changes cognition. It makes a trail you can inspect. That is all it promises.

Unlike the original experiments, you are not deceived about what you chose. That reduces drama and protects consent. It also changes the phenomenon under study, which is why we do **not** call it a scientific replication.

### 9. When an audio product enters the picture

A reader who struggles to begin a quiet practice may reasonably want a structured recording. The official Zen12 description presents twelve-minute listening sessions, twelve levels and four listening styles (music, guided, white noise and nature). Its separate promotional sales page describes a full-course bundle containing 48 MP3 files. Those are descriptions of a seller's advertised format, not proof of their broader claims. [S7] [S8]

A direct purchase from an audio seller may be useful to someone who prefers prompts and a predictable format. It can also be unnecessary. An unbranded timer, a quiet room, or the free [Seven Day Focus Fieldbook](https://finalvestige.com/focus/) offers a first step without requiring a paid recording. The Fieldbook is a 52-page planning and reflection resource, not a clinically validated treatment. [S9]

A trustworthy comparison should help readers answer questions the seller's excitement can obscure: Is this the exact file format I want? Does it require headphones? What is the actual package being sold at checkout? What is the effective refund rule? Can I try a free alternative first? Does any independent product-specific research support the mental or health benefit I care about? Those questions are the bridge from a mind investigation to ethical commerce.

**Editorial boundary:** This investigation is not itself an affiliate recommendation. Any future separately labeled buyer guide must disclose a material earning relationship adjacent to an authorized earning link. No such link appears here, and no affiliate commission or sale is assumed.

### 10. The evidence that survives the trick

An experimenter once used a concealed swap to show that a person might not detect a conflict between choice and outcome. That finding is valuable because the researchers **recorded the conflict**. It is the recorded sequence, not a flourish of language, that makes the result meaningful.

History offers the same lesson. A vivid case, a respectable witness, a signed document, and a confident theory are different kinds of evidence. None should be erased, but they should not be made interchangeable. The final measure of a Mind Archive investigation is not how uncanny it feels. It is whether a reader can follow the source, identify the inference, spot the boundary, and leave better equipped to make their own decision.

If you remember only one line, remember this: **a persuasive explanation is not a substitute for the original record.**
