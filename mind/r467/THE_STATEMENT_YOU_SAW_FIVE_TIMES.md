# The Statement That Became Familiar Enough to Believe
*Mind & Perception Archive | Original investigation | Editorial draft, unpublished | 18 November 2026*

**A claim does not become evidence merely because you have seen it five times. Yet repeated exposure can change how true it feels.**

The first time you encounter a striking claim, you may ask who found it, when, and by what method. The fifth time may arrive as a clipped video, an influencer's caption, a search snippet, a newsletter headline, and an advertisement—each apparently confirming the others. The factual evidence may not have changed. Your experience of the sentence has.

That gap between *familiarity* and *proof* is the subject of the **illusory truth effect**. It belongs in Final Vestige's Mind & Perception Archive for a practical reason: if a historical rumor or a product claim becomes more believable simply through circulation, the job of the evidence-led investigator is not to repeat it more confidently. It is to trace the statement back to a testable source.

## Three sessions, one uncomfortable result

In 1977, Lynn Hasher, David Goldstein and Thomas Toppino reported an experiment with sixty plausible statements of general knowledge. Participants judged how likely the statements were to be true on three occasions, spaced two weeks apart. Some statements reappeared; others were new. Truth ratings rose for the repeated statements, **both true and false**, while new statements did not show the same increase. The researchers called attention to frequency as a cue to referential validity.

This finding is frequently compressed into the slogan that repeating anything makes people believe it. That goes too far. The experiment assessed **ratings of plausibly true factual statements under particular laboratory conditions**. It did not show that every claim overcomes contrary evidence, that all listeners are persuaded, or that repetition creates an irreversible belief. More important, it did not demonstrate that *repetition is a source of truth*. It showed that people sometimes treat it as if it were.

**Original source:** Hasher, Goldstein & Toppino (1977), *Frequency and the conference of referential validity*, *Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior* 16(1), 107–112. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-5371(77)80012-1

Imagine a handwritten 1784 case report, a later popular retelling of it, a modern documentary quoting that retelling, and a clipped social video repeating the documentary. Four sightings can feel like four witnesses. In fact, it may be **one witness copied four times**. That is an analogy for source provenance, not a replication of the 1977 experiment.

## Knowledge helps, but is not an automatic shield

In 2015, Lisa Fazio, Nadia Brashier, B. Keith Payne and Elizabeth Marsh tested a sharper question: what happens when people have knowledge that should allow them to reject a repeated statement? Across their experiments, repeated statements sometimes gained higher truth ratings **even when participants had the relevant knowledge**. Their analysis proposed that the ease of processing a familiar assertion can compete with retrieval of known facts.

The distinction matters. To say 'knowledge does not protect' in a headline is too categorical if readers infer that facts never help. The actual finding is a vulnerability in the way some truth judgments are made, not proof that knowledge is useless. The observed effect depends on the task and materials.

A reputable-looking number is an especially efficient vehicle for this mistake. Imagine a creator says an audio recording can deliver the benefit of an hour of meditation in twelve minutes. Viewers later encounter the same number on a sales page, in comments, and in an emailed promotion. Familiarity is now high. **Product-specific comparative efficacy has not been established by the repetition itself.** The statement still needs a clearly defined outcome, a valid comparison, controls, and research on the exact product and conditions. The hypothetical sequence is illustrative, not a finding about any individual consumer.

**Original source:** Fazio et al. (2015), *Knowledge does not protect against illusory truth*, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General* 144, 993–1002. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26301795/

## The second encounter often matters more than the twenty-seventh

A 2021 paper, *The effects of repetition frequency on the illusory truth effect*, tested many more repetitions than the usual few exposures. One experiment presented statements as many as nine times; a second went as high as twenty-seven. In both, perceived truth increased with repetition, but the increase followed a **logarithmic**, rather than endlessly linear, pattern. The largest jump was associated with the move from first exposure to second; additional repetition yielded smaller incremental increases.

This is a useful corrective to two tempting exaggerations: that repetition stops mattering after one encounter, and that every extra repost carries the same persuasive force. The experiments measured subjective judgments, not real merchant sales, votes or durable behavior.

**Original source:** *The effects of repetition frequency on the illusory truth effect* (2021), *Cognitive Research: Principles and Implications*. https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s41235-021-00301-5

Another registered longitudinal investigation asked whether time alone makes the familiarity effect disappear. Henderson, Simons and Barr tested intervals of immediate exposure, one day, one week and one month, with 567 participants analyzed. Repeated statements were rated more true than new ones at all intervals, but the effect **diminished as delay increased**. So 'repetition always persists unchanged' is as misleading as 'sleep on it and the effect disappears.'

**Original source:** Henderson, Simons & Barr (2021), *The Trajectory of Truth: A Longitudinal Study of the Illusory Truth Effect*, registered report. https://doi.org/10.5334/joc.161

## Does calling out the trick work?

There is better news than the simplified claim that the mind is helpless. In a 2020 laboratory study of 167 participants, Calio, Nadarevic and Musch tested explicit warnings about the truth effect. Their modeling suggested that warnings could **reduce** reliance on fluent processing, particularly when participants possessed relevant knowledge, without eliminating the effect. Warning is not a universal antidote.

In 2024, a separate set of three preregistered social-media experiments in the UK, Italy and Germany found an important complication. Corrections aimed at false news lowered its perceived accuracy and engagement, but **incorrect corrections on true posts also reduced perceived accuracy of those true claims**. That is an essential warning for an editorial brand tempted to treat every debunk as evidence. A correction's own source quality matters.

**Original sources:** Calio, Nadarevic & Musch (2020), *How explicit warnings reduce the truth effect*. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.actpsy.2020.103185 ; Stoeckel et al. (2024), *Social corrections act as a double-edged sword*. https://doi.org/10.1038/s44271-024-00057-w

A 2024 survey experiment with 636 participants reported improved verification behaviors and truth discernment from using a fact-checking guide. This does not establish that the exact Final Vestige method below has been experimentally validated. It simply supports making **independent checking an action**, not a slogan.

**Original source:** Tu (2024), *Empowering social media users: nudge toward self-engaged verification for improved truth and sharing discernment*. https://doi.org/10.1093/joc/jqae007

## The archival test: when the number of witnesses is an illusion

Final Vestige's [Puységur Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) provide a striking place to apply the same discipline.

Puységur gathered patient reports, witness statements, notarial documents, predictions and repeated observations into a case for animal magnetism in the late eighteenth century. The modern archive distinguishes an original 1785 edition reference from the later 1813 scan used for page-image verification. It preserves failed predictions, failed cures, operator doubts, and difficult testimony about refusal and authority. Its editorial boundary is explicit: a witnessed claim establishes what was reported, **not** the invisible mechanism said to have caused it.

Repetition is one reason to be precise about the word *independent*. A historical account repeated in multiple books may have only one original claimant. Conversely, a genuine second witness or separately recorded result is meaningful evidence and should not be discounted solely because it resembles an earlier report. To distinguish those cases, identify the earliest surviving record, its date and page, the source of each later citation, the conditions under which statements were recorded, and the outcome that could have disproved the claim.

The modern psychological studies here are **not** experimental tests of Puységur's subjects. They do not diagnose eighteenth-century participants or prove that animal magnetism was a cognitive illusion. They tell us why a twenty-first-century reader must not count repeated sentences as independent documentation.

## The twelve-minute evidence audit

This original exercise needs no app, purchase, account, trance, or special audio. It is not a diagnostic instrument or a tested treatment. Its purpose is to make the difference between *recognizing* and *verifying* a claim visible in your notes.

**Minutes 0–2: Write one claim accurately.** Choose a claim encountered in an online clip, a history article, or a consumer-audio product description. Copy the precise wording, including the promised outcome and time frame, into a notebook. Avoid selecting a claim involving your immediate medical decisions.

**Minutes 2–4: Count appearances, not sources.** List where you saw it: video, repost, sales page, search summary, creator comment. Put a mark beside each if it appears to quote, paraphrase, or link to an earlier item.

**Minutes 4–7: Find the first testable source.** Follow citations backward. Ask whether the source is a primary experiment, a catalog description, an anecdote, an advertising claim, or an independent review. Record publication date and methods. If no original source is accessible, say *source not established*, not *false*.

**Minutes 7–10: Set a counterfactual.** What result would count against the claim? If the outcome is 'feel better', how was it measured? If a vendor claims faster meditation, compared with which practice, under what conditions and for which participants? Distinguish a trial of a broad technique from a study of the particular marketed product.

**Minutes 10–12: Give it a bounded label.** 'Independent trial supports this exact outcome', 'seller description only', 'plausible but untested here', 'conflicting studies', or 'not substantiated'. Record one question you would need answered before sharing or buying.

A finding of uncertainty is useful. It tells you where the evidence stops.

## When the product page repeats the evidence language

The current official Inspire3 catalog describes Zen12, Hypnosis Live and Brainwave Shots using mental-performance language. The official Zen12 About page describes a structured MP3 program with twelve levels, twelve-minute sessions and four listening formats. It also makes much larger promises about automatic relaxation, brainwave states and time-equivalence. Those are **the seller's assertions**, not conclusions reached by this article.

A 2023 systematic review of fourteen binaural-beat EEG studies found five studies broadly consistent with the entrainment hypothesis, eight contradictory and one mixed; their methods were too heterogeneous to establish a consistent effect. It was **not a trial of Zen12**. The 1977, 2015 and 2021 repetition studies above cannot be recruited to prove Zen12 works either: they address how claims can come to *seem* true, not whether the audio causes a beneficial outcome.

**Original source:** Ingendoh, Posny & Heine (2023), *Binaural beats to entrain the brain?*, *PLOS ONE*. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37205669/ ; seller description https://zen12.com/about ; separate seller promotional page https://zen12.com/secret/

The reader deserves two separate experiences. This investigation is free of paid links and arrives at its conclusion from evidence. An **optional, independently labeled tools-under-review guide** can explain what a seller actually provides, what it charges at a verified checkout, what its terms say, what it does not establish, and whether Final Vestige may later receive a commission. The existence of that guide must not change what the investigation concludes.

## What survives the repetition

A credible investigation does not ask you to trust it because you have heard it before. It shows the original claim, the best surviving evidence, the most material disagreement and the boundary that the evidence cannot cross.

Repeated exposure can increase a statement's *felt* truth. It cannot furnish missing controls, authenticate a witness, validate an advertisement, or turn a sales slogan into an experimental result. For an archive built to find what others miss, the most valuable question may be deceptively simple: **Are these five sources—or the same sentence five times?**
