# The Word That Stopped Sounding Like a Word
**Final Vestige Studios · Mind & Perception Archive · UNPUBLISHED research draft · November 10, 2026**

*Why repeating a familiar word can make it feel strangely unfamiliar—and why this ordinary laboratory result cannot validate a self-help sales pitch.*

Imagine a word you have known since childhood: **window**. It refers to a pane, a frame, a view, and perhaps a particular room. You can read it almost without noticing that you are reading. Now say it, slowly, perhaps ten times. Then twenty. In some people, something odd happens. The sounds still arrive; the letters remain legible. Yet the word begins to feel assembled from unfamiliar pieces. Is *window* really spelled that way? How can those syllables possibly belong to the glass in the wall?

Nothing has happened to the window. Nothing has happened to the dictionary. What changed, temporarily, was the reader's experience of a perfectly ordinary sign. Researchers have investigated related phenomena under the label **semantic satiation**: a temporary weakening of the apparent meaning of a repeatedly presented word. It is not a reliable trick that works identically for everyone, and it is not the loss of language ability. It is one of several reasons to be careful when translating an intense subjective experience into an extravagant theory about what produced it.

That distinction matters to Final Vestige. Historical investigators confronted people who described striking alterations of experience in the presence of an operator. Modern product sellers describe audio that allegedly produces dramatic changes in the mind. In both settings, the observation can be worth documenting without granting the explanation that someone else attaches to it.

## The unsettling part is how little has changed

We often imagine language as a pipeline: hear a sound, retrieve its fixed meaning, proceed. But word recognition recruits several processes. We identify a visual or auditory pattern, distinguish it from alternatives, draw on context, and connect it to concepts. Familiarity usually makes those stages seem effortless, as if the word and the thing it names were one object. Repetition can expose that feeling of automaticity.

The sensation has a family resemblance to **jamais vu**, the unsettling experience of a familiar object or event feeling unfamiliar. These labels are not interchangeable. Semantic satiation concerns an experimentally studied response to repetition and meaning; jamais vu is a broader phenomenological description. A 2020-published research report on repeated word-copying investigated an experimentally induced word-alienation experience and framed it in relation to jamais vu. The researchers reported peculiar experiences in a substantial fraction of trials, but their particular sample and instructions should not become an assurance that everyone can produce the experience on command. [S5]

What readers often take from a quick internet demonstration—"I just broke my brain"—is precisely what the experiments do not show. There is no reason to infer damage, hypnosis, an altered permanent belief, a reprogrammed personality, or an unusual external influence. One can change how meaning *feels* without changing what a word *means*.

## An older experiment, before the viral video

The research did not begin with vertical video or a phone screen. In 1962, Leon A. Jakobovits and Wallace E. Lambert published studies of verbal repetition and satiation, including *Semantic satiation in an addition task* and *Mediated satiation in verbal transfer*. Their original papers can be identified through the National Library of Medicine's bibliographic records. Those records establish the authors, dates, and research questions; they do **not** supply every procedural detail or result. Without examining the full original papers, it would be careless to supply invented participant counts, quotations, or precise percentages. [S1][S2]

That historical restraint is important. Contemporary summaries sometimes make a research tradition sound like a single conclusive demonstration. The term covers a range of tasks, response measures, stimuli, and theoretical accounts. A subjective report of a word "losing meaning" is not equivalent to a measured reduction in semantic priming, nor are either of those equivalent to proof that the underlying concept is erased.

Consider the difference between three possible observations: a reader says "this word looks weird"; a participant is slower at a category decision after many presentations; an EEG signal differs between a short- and long-repetition condition. All three are interesting. They do not mean the same thing. To understand the phenomenon, an investigator must specify which observation was recorded and what comparison made it interpretable.

## Thirty repetitions and an electrical trace

A particularly useful modern study arrived in 2017. Kim Ströberg, Lau M. Andersen, and Stefan Wiens measured event-related electrical responses while participants performed a semantic-priming task. A prime word appeared **three or thirty times**; the investigators changed case and format across presentations to reduce a simple visual-habituation explanation, separated conditions carefully, and evaluated an EEG component called the **N400**. [S3]

The N400 is an event-related response often sensitive to how a word fits its semantic context. It is not a detector for a specific thought, not a clinical diagnosis, and not a readout of a single brain state. The investigators found a reduced N400 difference at **centrofrontal electrodes** following thirty rather than three presentations. Their exploratory source reconstruction suggested involvement of several regions; such reconstructions depend on modeling assumptions and should not be narrated as a photograph of meaning leaving the brain.

The authors explicitly positioned the experiment against earlier inconsistent results and designs vulnerable to nonsemantic confounds. That is a better account of scientific progress than the slogan "science proved words stop meaning something." The measured effect supported the researchers' semantic-satiation interpretation **under the conditions they tested**. It does not show that listening repeatedly to a particular commercial recording produces concentration, emotional healing, or any promised brainwave transformation.

That is where an attractive story can go wrong. Imagine a seller placing "EEG study" beside a listening product. A reader may reasonably assume that the scientists tested the product's sound files and measured the benefit the seller advertises. Here, they did not. A difference in a semantic-priming experiment is not evidence for the efficacy of a meditation program.

## Repetition is not one process

Other research has broadened the problem beyond a single word repeated in isolation. A 2018 article by Nathaniel and colleagues examined deterioration in semantic categorization even **without repeating the same stimulus**, as people repeatedly retrieved related conceptual information in a paced task. The paper discusses possibilities including competition between representations and changes arising from previous retrieval. The results complicate any one-size-fits-all claim that the phenomenon is simply fatigue from looking at the same letters. [S4]

A 2022 paper on adult-age differences varied the number of prime presentations—on average about 2.5, 12.5, or 22.5—and assessed a subsequent word-triad task. It addressed age-related sensitivity rather than trying to diagnose an individual's memory or cognitive fitness. [S6] Work on emotional valence also suggests that a word's emotional properties and the task's timing can change whether and when a repetition effect emerges. That is another reason to avoid declaring that thirty repetitions must make every person feel a particular thing. [S7]

Several hypotheses remain worth distinguishing. Repeating the same sound might alter auditory or visual habituation. Repeatedly accessing a concept might alter the efficiency of semantic access. Expectation, demand characteristics, and attention might affect what a participant reports. A thoughtful experiment tries to separate these contributors rather than choosing whichever description sounds the most dramatic.

Science does not become weaker when investigators admit that a vivid phenomenon has multiple possible mechanisms. It becomes more informative. The difficult question is not only *did something happen?* It is *which variable made the difference, which measurement captured it, and how far may we generalize?*

## Two experiments that are easy to confuse

There is another famous effect of repetition that should not be smuggled into this story. In the **illusory-truth effect**, repeated statements may be judged more believable than new statements. In semantic satiation, repeated words may momentarily feel stripped of meaning or alter subsequent semantic processing. These findings are not opposites, because the dependent variables are different: perceived statement truth on one hand, word-meaning experience or processing on the other.

One cannot reason from "repetition makes a word feel strange" to "affirmations can never influence thought." Nor can one reason from "repeated statements can feel more credible" to "affirmations install a new personality." Neither inference follows from the experiments. The most useful question is whether someone is measuring subjective familiarity, belief, mood, performance, clinical symptoms, or something else.

A third confusion concerns **N400 attenuation after repetition**, which can occur through several task-dependent pathways. Evidence of an EEG response changing is not by itself a map of someone's personal intention or voluntary control. The 1998 work by Okita and Jibu on attention and spoken-word repetition, for example, found an N400 repetition effect in attended-attended trials but not in some ignored presentations. Attention can matter to the measured response. [S8]

## A gentler demonstration, with no flashing screen

You can investigate the *experience* without claiming to recreate the laboratory. Choose an emotionally neutral word—**window**, **planet**, or **spoon**—and write down, before the exercise, what it means in a sentence. Look away for a moment. Repeat the word at a comfortable pace, perhaps fifteen times; stop whenever you prefer. Note separately whether the spelling seems odd, whether the sound feels unusual, whether the meaning feels less immediate, and whether your ability to define it actually changed.

Then perform a control: choose a different neutral word, say it only once, and attempt the same observations. Try the conditions on another day and switch which word gets repeated. Keep your notes rather than forcing a dramatic outcome. If you dislike repetition, skip the exercise. There is no prize for making yourself uncomfortable, and no failure if nothing unusual happens.

This is **an informal demonstration, not a validated test**. Its most important weakness is that word differences, expectations, fatigue, and the order of conditions are not properly controlled. The observation does not establish a mechanism or diagnose a mental condition. If you had an unusual sensation, record it as an experience. If you did not, that is a legitimate observation too.

For readers who prefer not to speak or repeat text, a safer, entirely non-provocative alternative is a paper comparison. Write three columns labeled **reported experience**, **observed task performance**, and **explanation**. Put "the word felt unfamiliar" in the first, "participant took longer on a category choice" in the second, and "repetition altered semantic access" in the third. Ask yourself what evidence would be necessary to travel from one column to the next.

## The historical bridge: a witness, an operator, and a cause

In the 1780s, Armand-Marie-Jacques de Chastenet, marquis de Puységur, an artillery officer associated with animal magnetism, described phenomena later called magnetic somnambulism. Final Vestige's published *Puységur's Animal Magnetism Files* catalogs source rows and case dossiers on witness testimony, suggestion, consent, obedience, and competing explanations. The National Library of Medicine catalog dates a relevant *Mémoires* volume to 1784 [i.e., 1785], with a note that its closing signature is dated February 4, 1785. [S9][S10]

The comparison is methodological, not evidentiary. Nothing about semantic satiation shows that Puységur's patients experienced the same phenomenon, nor does it refute or confirm every historical testimony. The point is subtler. A witness may faithfully report a sensation while an operator incorrectly identifies its cause. A later reader may faithfully quote the report yet exaggerate what it establishes. An independent archive needs room for those possibilities.

When a modern promoter promises that certain repeated sounds "reprogram" the brain, we face an analogous sequence: the promised feeling, the claimed mechanism, the actual outcome, and the evidence connecting them. Each demands its own documentation.

If a commercial audio product offers convenience, a structured sequence, or sounds that a buyer happens to enjoy, those are intelligible descriptions of a product's design or a person's preference. They are not proof of a neurophysiological benefit. The distinction is not hostile to meditation; it protects readers from mistaking a plausible story for an independent trial.

## The seller's leap from mechanism to outcome

Inspire3's official product catalog describes Zen12 as a twelve-minute meditation-audio offering, Hypnosis Live as a library of downloadable self-hypnosis MP3s, and Brainwave Shots as fifteen-minute audio products. These are **seller descriptions**. Zen12's official Premium sales page specifies twelve levels, four audio styles per level, and forty-eight MP3 files, while separately making claims about brainwave entrainment and improved states. [S11][S12]

An audio library may genuinely contain those advertised files without the advertised psychological outcomes being established. The EEG studies cited above did not evaluate Zen12, Hypnosis Live, Brainwave Shots, or any other specific Inspire3 product. They cannot substantiate claims that particular commercial recordings treat anxiety, enhance cognition, eliminate stress, guarantee creativity, or reproduce clinical hypnosis.

The FTC's guidance addresses not merely literal promises but claims a reasonable consumer may take from the overall presentation. Scientific images, clinical-sounding vocabulary, and technical references can imply proof even when a small disclaimer says otherwise. Health-related efficacy assertions require adequate, product-relevant substantiation, commonly competent and reliable scientific evidence. [S13] The safer editorial choice is to leave unsupported claims out of the recommendation itself.

There is a commercial decision a reader can still make. Does an organized collection of downloadable tracks offer sufficient practical convenience compared with a free timer, free recordings, or silence? What exactly does the checkout contain, what device can play it, how is support accessed, and how would a refund be requested? These are buyer questions. They are distinct from a promise that a brain changes in a clinically meaningful way.

## What this little word can teach us

The most interesting outcome of the word exercise may be neither a profound insight nor an eerie sensation. It may be the realization that confidence in a familiar experience is not an instrument for explaining it. When a word seems to lose its ordinary meaning, the observation is yours. The possible explanation belongs to careful research. And a seller's promise about a recording remains a separate claim requiring separate proof.

A historical witness, a laboratory participant, and a prospective buyer each deserve that distinction. It is the difference between taking experience seriously and handing its interpretation to the loudest voice in the room.
