# The White-Noise Control That Improved, Too
## What a 2025 self-hypnosis trial actually tells us about listening, suggestion and evidence

*Final Vestige Studios | Mind & Perception | Original research, unpublished draft | November 17, 2026*

**The question:** If a recorded voice can change what someone experiences, how much of the result belongs to the words, the act of listening, the expectation, or something that would have happened anyway? In a particularly revealing randomized clinical trial, researchers tested a clinical hypnosis recording against a comparison recording of white noise **labeled as hypnosis**. Both groups improved. One improved more. The distance between those statements is where an honest investigation begins.

### Two very different recordings with the same name

Imagine the comparison condition. There is no magician or swinging watch. Participants receive information about hypnosis and listen to an audio recording at home. Yet in the control group, the recording contains white noise. The researchers have deliberately preserved the label and the ritual while changing the content. This attempts to answer a much more demanding question than whether people feel differently after listening: does the specific hypnotic treatment outperform a credible substitute?

The trial by Elkins and colleagues, published on **November 11, 2025** in *JAMA Network Open*, recruited **250 postmenopausal women** who experienced frequent hot flashes. The randomized intervention lasted six weeks. One group used daily recordings involving hypnotic induction, relaxation and cooling imagery; the comparison group used time-matched white noise presented as hypnosis. Both groups received educational materials. Their primary outcome was the **hot-flash score**, calculated using daily symptom frequency and severity reports. It was not a test of meditation depth, exam performance, memory, or instant concentration. [1]

At six weeks, the active hypnosis group's hot-flash score had fallen **53.4%** from baseline; the white-noise group's score had fallen **40.9%**. The reported between-group test at that timepoint had **P = .04**. Daily-interference reports and perceived benefit also favored hypnosis. Those are meaningful results for the clinical question. They do **not** demonstrate that every consumer hypnosis recording works, that white noise produces brain entrainment, or that twelve minutes of audio substitutes for an hour of meditation. [1]

The detail many commercial summaries would hide is the substantial improvement in the comparison group. The trial had no usual-care-only arm that could tell us how much of the shared improvement reflected expectancy, symptom fluctuation, a quiet daily ritual, regression to the mean, or simply listening to a potentially relaxing sound. A genuine control should be interpreted, not wished away. The most useful estimate is the *additional* effect in comparison with the control, not the biggest before-and-after percentage on a marketing banner.

### Six guardrails before we draw a conclusion

**Population is not a technicality.** The participants were postmenopausal women meeting a defined hot-flash burden. Sixty-two of the 250 reported a history of breast cancer. Their mean age was roughly 56. This does not make the trial irrelevant to learning how evidence works; it makes its clinical conclusion **specific**. A student taking an exam or someone struggling to focus at work cannot inherit that population's results just because both experiences involve sound.

**Dose is part of the intervention.** The active recordings lasted about 20 minutes per day over six weeks. A retail 12-minute meditation program, a 15-minute brainwave product, and a single 33-minute focus hypnosis session differ not only in packaging but in time, content and intended result. The name of an intervention is not a scientific equivalence class.

**A sham is not always an inert object.** White noise accompanied by information and the label hypnosis is an ingenious comparison, but it does not remove attention, ritual, expectation or relaxation. The trial cannot tell us that the active script alone produced all improvements. The control's response is an observation requiring explanation, not an embarrassment.

**Read secondary and trajectory analyses too.** The authors reported a statistically significant primary six-week comparison, but a mixed-effects analysis of the wider response trajectories did **not** reach conventional significance (**P = .06**). Several symptom-severity category comparisons were also nonsignificant. Selectively repeating only the most flattering number would misrepresent the evidence. [1]

**The measurement matters.** Participants' diaries of hot-flash frequency and severity were real symptom-report outcomes, yet they do not constitute evidence that a particular audio track permanently changes neural circuitry. The researchers followed participants to twelve weeks; that is not a lifetime guarantee. The paper also noted limited participant demographic diversity and education differences that affect generalizability.

**A study can support a narrow finding while leaving competing explanations alive.** An accompanying methodological discussion warns that the white-noise comparison may itself encourage relaxation and that regression toward the mean may play a role in symptom improvement. This does not cancel the reported advantage of active hypnosis at six weeks. It reminds us that a valid randomized comparison is more informative than a testimonial, while still requiring precise interpretation. [2]

### One study, three very different retail offers

Consider the precise products to which a visitor might be directed.

The official Inspire3 catalogue identifies **Zen12**, **Hypnosis Live**, and **Brainwave Shots** as distinct offerings. Zen12 is described as a twelve-level program with four audio styles per level: guided meditation, relaxation music, white noise and sounds of nature. A full-course sales presentation advertises 48 MP3 files. The seller promises that a twelve-minute session provides the benefit of an hour's regular meditation, and makes broader relaxation and health assertions. The course format is an observable seller-described feature. The claimed equivalence and therapeutic benefits have not been independently substantiated here for this particular product. [3,4]

Hypnosis Live's **Total Focus** product page describes a **33-minute, 47 MB** hypnosis MP3 recorded by J. A. Amos. It presents promises of unusually strong concentration, sharper productivity and brain reprogramming, plus distinct single-track and bundled offers. These are merchant claims. The clinical trial did not test Total Focus, did not measure occupational productivity and cannot supply evidence of its specific promises. [5]

The Brainwave Shots **Focus Shot** page describes 15-minute and 30-minute tracks and brainwave-only versions, with claims of hours-long focused attention. The 2023 systematic review by Ingendoh and colleagues on binaural-beat brain entrainment reported contradictory findings and methodological heterogeneity across the literature. That review does not prove the product fails; it does undermine any leap from 'audio frequencies have been studied' to 'this branded track reliably creates hours of superior focus.' [6,7]

The products could nevertheless offer ordinary value: a convenient prompt to stop, a structured listening experience, a format a listener enjoys. The difference between **format satisfaction** and **efficacy demonstrated by a product-specific trial** is not semantic. One is a consumer choice; the other is a scientific claim that needs matching evidence.

### A paper trail does not prove the force behind it

In 1784, Armand-Marie-Jacques de Chastenet, Marquis de Puységur, began compiling reports of unusual responsive states connected to animal magnetism. The source-centered Final Vestige **Puységur Animal Magnetism Files** now preserve 153 source rows and 16 case dossiers, including predictions, failures, operator doubts, vulnerable patients and conflicts around consent. The archive never treats a witnessed, notarized or repeated claim as automatic proof of a magnetic physical force. [8]

That archival discipline resonates with modern blinded research. What did the subject report? What had the operator suggested? Who controlled the language of the experience? When a participant knows the procedure has been called hypnosis, expectation may play some role. That is a question to investigate, not a reason to dismiss every experience as invented.

Puységur's surviving notes also contain admissions that operator curiosity and authority could elicit compliant false answers. The relevant modern lesson is not to revive animal magnetism. It is to ask whether a narrative supplied by an investigator can become part of what a participant reports—and to design a study that might tell us the difference.

A trial's white-noise condition is one imperfect method of testing that boundary. Modern trials bring random allocation, predefined outcomes and transparent comparisons to questions that historical witness networks could not isolate. Yet they still cannot make expectation, context or human variability disappear.

### Twelve minutes of observation without buying anything

This **free exercise is a personal attention log, not a hypnosis induction, medical treatment, randomized test, or clinical recommendation**. Choose a safe, ordinary setting. Do not undertake it while driving or performing attention-critical work. Stop if sitting quietly or attending inward causes distress.

For the first minute, name one small practical action, such as opening a document or drafting a message. Write what successful *starting* would look like. Keep the observation behavioral rather than diagnosing your mental state.

For the next four minutes, sit with ordinary quiet or a familiar unobtrusive environmental sound. Notice distraction; no special frequency or thought suppression is required. Avoid comparing your experience with a commercial promise.

For the following four minutes, take the planned smallest step. Did you write the first sentence, make the list, or open the page? Observing behavior is more useful than awarding yourself a made-up 'focus score.'

In the last three minutes, note the conditions: time of day, distractions, fatigue, and whether the task felt clear. If you repeat the exercise, keep the circumstances visible. Improvement from one day to the next cannot be attributed to your background audio without a defensible comparison. A ritual may help you begin without explaining *why* it helped.

### Five questions for any paid audio

1. **What exactly is included?** A single MP3, the complete twelve-level library and a bundle are different purchases. Clarify format, runtime, access, support and choice of sound.
2. **Which benefit is being promised?** Find evidence for the same product and the same outcome. Hot flashes are not concentration. Meditation experience is not income. Listening satisfaction is not treatment.
3. **What was the comparison condition?** A randomized study with a credible control is stronger than testimonials, celebrity praise or a set of scientific citations about related subjects.
4. **What are the *actual* checkout and refund terms?** Public merchant pages show varying offers. An affiliate's referred checkout needs its own authorized, current price, policy and product identity check.
5. **Have you tried the free baseline?** Before paying for structure, determine whether a simple task plan, a quiet interval or a free Fieldbook helps you start. A free alternative is not an obstacle to an ethical recommendation.

### What we can conclude

The most interesting result is neither 'every audio works' nor 'every effect was imagined.' It is that a particular recorded clinical intervention and a credible white-noise comparison were both associated with symptom improvement, with a more modest additional advantage for the former on the designated six-week measures. The study deserves to be understood with its actual population, outcome, dose, comparisons and limitations intact.

At the end of the historical archive, one question remains: **What did the witness observe, and what did the investigator supply?** At the end of a modern audio trial, the question is almost the same. It is a far better starting point for a purchase decision than a promise that headphones can override the ordinary demands of science.
