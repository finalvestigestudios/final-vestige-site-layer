# The Glass That Wasn't There
## What a single word can do to a witness's memory

*Final Vestige Studios — Mind & Perception Archive | Research manuscript | Unpublished, 4 November 2026*

**Evidence status.** This investigation describes experiments on eyewitness recall and misleading information. It does not diagnose anyone, validate recovered-memory techniques, or show that every inaccurate recollection was implanted. No product recommendation appears here.

A person watches a collision. Later, someone asks how fast the vehicles were going when they hit each other. Change one word in that question, and the reported speed may change. Ask again days later, and a detail that never appeared in the film can become something a participant believes they saw.

The object in question was glass. In Elizabeth Loftus and John Palmer's 1974 experiments, there was none on the road in the film used for the delayed broken-glass test. The detail came later, in the phrasing of a question. That distinction makes the study unsettling: a witness need not be lying for the description of an event to become less faithful to the event itself. [1]

The story is often told as if an experiment proved memory is fiction. It did not. The more useful finding is narrower and more consequential: **after an event, the language used to discuss it can influence some subsequent memory reports.** If an eyewitness recalls correctly, that remains meaningful. If someone confidently supplies a detail, confidence alone does not reveal whether the detail came from the original experience or from subsequent suggestion.

## A question with five different verbs

Loftus and Palmer published *Reconstruction of Automobile Destruction: An Example of the Interaction Between Language and Memory* in 1974. In their first experiment, people watched films of vehicle accidents and estimated speed. The questions varied by verb: the cars had *smashed*, *collided*, *bumped*, *hit*, or *contacted* each other. Reported speed varied with the wording. [1]

There are at least two plausible reasons that might happen. A strong verb may change what a participant remembers about the collision, or it may simply change the numerical response that seems appropriate while the original visual memory remains much the same. The first experiment alone could not settle that distinction. The authors did not stop there.

Their second experiment included a delayed question about broken glass. Participants viewed a film of a collision, were questioned using either *smashed* or *hit*, or were not asked a speed question, and returned a week later. The people who had earlier heard *smashed* were more likely to report seeing broken glass, although the film did not contain any. [1]

The effect concerns responses within those particular experimental conditions. It cannot be converted into a universal percentage of inaccurate criminal testimony. Nor does it mean a single loaded question necessarily overwrites an intact memory trace. It shows that misleading post-event language can affect subsequent testimony — an important reason to separate an initial observation from the later conversation about it.

The word *smashed* is vivid; the study is memorable. Those two features have also made it vulnerable to oversimplification. An experiment can reveal a mechanism without supplying an unrestricted theory of all memories. We should resist turning a useful warning about suggestion into another dogma.

## The difference between a memory and its source

Most recollections do not arrive with a printed provenance label. You can remember a detail while remaining uncertain whether you saw it, heard it afterward, read it in a report, or imagined it while reconstructing a scene. This is the practical problem of **source monitoring**: recognizing where information came from, not merely whether it feels familiar. [2]

Consider a family photograph, a story retold at holidays, and an event that happened when you were young. Years later, they may be woven together into one seemingly effortless account. Some details may come from experience, others from the photograph or repeated narration. The memory may be sincere without every detail sharing the same origin. This is an illustration of the problem, not a claim about any particular reader's childhood.

The distinction matters for investigators. A leading question can import an assumption: *Which hand held the knife?* already implies that a knife was present. Even when a witness rejects the premise, hearing it can complicate later retrieval. A good inquiry asks first what was observed, records answers before offering possible explanations, and preserves the provenance of each new fact.

It matters for historians too. A source written years after an event may be rich in detail, but it has passed through conversation, later discoveries, motives and memory. The correct response is neither blind acceptance nor wholesale dismissal; it is to compare accounts with contemporary records, physical evidence, and each account's circumstances.

## A larger experiment, and a complication for familiar assumptions

Nearly half a century after the collision study, Ciara Greene, Richard Bradshaw, Charlotte Huston and Gillian Murphy compared **six ways of delivering misinformation** in a controlled eyewitness task. Their work, published in the *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied* in 2022, involved **1,182 participants** who viewed a simulated robbery. Researchers then presented misleading details through different materials — among them leading questions, narratives and altered photographs — before testing memory. [3]

The result was not that the fanciest visual always wins. The authors reported that conditions encouraging deeper processing of misleading information produced more distortions. In that particular design, doctored photographs were **not** an effective method of implanting the tested misinformation. Participants were also more likely to notice misinformation in the simple-narrative condition. [3]

This is a valuable corrective. The folklore around memory distortion sometimes treats a forged image as an irresistible weapon. The experiment suggests a more complicated relationship among attention, meaning, and the way misleading information is integrated. The result is bounded by the stimuli, task and outcomes measured; photographs can mislead in other settings, and this study does not declare them harmless.

The same care is necessary when discussing emotion. A 2022 systematic review of thirty-nine eligible studies examined whether emotional experiences make memory more or less vulnerable to misinformation. The literature did not yield a simple, universal protection rule. [4] A vivid event is not automatically remembered perfectly, and a mistake does not prove the original event lacked emotional force.

## What research can and cannot tell us about implanted episodes

The delayed glass question concerned a detail within an event that participants really watched. That is not the same as getting a person to believe that an entire invented autobiographical episode occurred. Researchers have also studied the latter question, but it is a distinct experimental and ethical undertaking.

In a 2005 review, Loftus traced three decades of misinformation research and discussed work on false autobiographical beliefs. The review distinguished the impairment or distortion of memory reports after misleading information from the more complex question of whether people come to believe they personally experienced events that never happened. [2] Treating both outcomes as interchangeable is an error.

Researchers and clinicians have debated the interpretation and generalizability of different false-memory paradigms. Some laboratory studies involve short delays, unusual tasks or suggestive interviews. Real historical allegations and clinical experiences may involve corroboration, repeated trauma, long intervals and competing sources. It would be irresponsible to use an introductory psychology experiment to pronounce on an individual's real allegation.

There is a similarly important distinction between a correction and a cure. Warnings can reduce certain misinformation effects under some conditions, as the 2005 review discusses, but there is no simple script that guarantees a person's recollections will become accurate. [2]

## From mesmerism to the modern authority of a recorded voice

Final Vestige's published investigation of **the 1784 inquiry into mesmerism** and the **Puységur Animal Magnetism Files** concerns a different era, with different claims and methods. Those histories are not evidence that animal magnetism worked as described, nor can the 1974 collision experiments explain every historical report of trance or suggestion. [5][6]

The common thread is more modest: the conditions under which people report experiences matter. Who asks a question? Does the question imply the answer? Is the observer aware of what is expected? Can a claim be checked independently of the person making it? These are questions that historians and psychological researchers can ask without assuming that eighteenth-century testimony and modern laboratory measures are equivalent.

This point also matters when commercial audio promises to change a listener's mental state or unlock hidden potential. A confident narrator, an attractive explanation, and a sophisticated-looking scientific graphic are **not** product-specific evidence. Suggestion and expectation may influence how someone interprets an experience. That possibility alone does not demonstrate that any particular meditation or hypnosis recording works, nor does it establish that it cannot be enjoyable.

A scientific claim is strongest when the actual intervention and outcome have been tested under conditions capable of detecting alternative explanations. The word *brainwave*, like the word *smashed*, can create an impression. An impression is not the same thing as a measurement.

## A source-checking exercise: three columns, no tricks

Try this exercise with an ordinary, low-stakes public event — an interview, a lecture, or a documentary clip that can be watched again. Avoid using a personal trauma or asking someone else about a disputed experience.

Before discussing the clip, write three columns on paper. In the first, record five concrete details you believe you **directly observed**. In the second, record interpretations or inferences, such as someone's likely intention. Leave the third for details learned **afterward**, from a commentary, caption or another person's recollection.

Now read a separate description of the clip. Does it include any details you did not initially record? Mark them in the third column instead of silently moving them into the first. Finally, replay the original and check each candidate fact. Keep errors and corrections visible rather than changing the old entries.

This is a **source-literacy exercise**, not a validated test of susceptibility to suggestion, a memory-improvement method, or a measurement of how many false memories you have. It does one useful thing: makes the difference between seeing, inferring and hearing afterward explicit.

For a historian, the columns might be **contemporary record**, **later testimony** and **modern interpretation**. The paper does not create certainty. It makes provenance easier to audit.

## A witness is a person, not a recording device

One bad question does not make all witnesses unreliable. One sincere recollection does not make corroboration unnecessary. The important lesson is that a recollection is an evidentiary claim with a history of its own.

Better questions preserve that history. They distinguish what a witness originally perceived from what the witness learned later. Better reporting distinguishes a study's actual method and finding from the claims people build around it. Better self-help journalism distinguishes a seller's promise from an independently measured result.

That discipline fits Final Vestige's broader mission: restore the chain between an extraordinary story and the evidence it rests on. Sometimes the most dramatic moment in the story is not the crash in the film. It is the question asked afterward.
