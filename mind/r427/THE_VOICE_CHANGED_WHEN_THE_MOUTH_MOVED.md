# The Voice Changed When the Mouth Moved
## The McGurk effect, the unreliable witness of hearing, and the gap between a convincing experience and its cause

**Final Vestige Studios | Mind & Perception | ORIGINAL SOURCE-ONLY DRAFT | 16 November 2026**

*An experiment about what people hear, and a larger question about what they believe their senses prove.*

Imagine a tiny experiment with one actor and two recordings. The film shows a mouth shaping one syllable. The audio track contains another. Neither is especially dramatic. Yet the listener can report hearing a third syllable that was never spoken on the soundtrack. The claim sounds supernatural only until the experimental conditions are revealed. The soundtrack has not secretly changed. The human auditory experience has.

In 1976, Harry McGurk and John MacDonald put precisely this observation into print. The letter was titled *Hearing lips and seeing voices*, and its deceptively simple finding reshaped discussions of how hearing works. On the soundtrack, a speaker repeatedly said something like **ba**; on the film, the visible articulatory movement was for **ga**. Adult observers reported **da**. In the reverse pairing, responses more often took other combination forms, including **bagba** and **gaba**. The asymmetry matters: a mismatched mouth does not always produce a tidy third syllable. The percept depends on the configuration. [1]

A magic trick would conceal its mechanism. The research exposed one.

### What the 1976 finding really means

We often talk as though sound enters our ears, is decoded, and becomes a word. Vision arrives through a separate doorway. The McGurk experiment challenges that division. Information about a talker's mouth can influence the experience of speech sounds themselves. The listener is not merely *guessing* which word was spoken based on lip-reading context. Under particular mismatched conditions, the reported syllable may differ from the original acoustic signal. [1]

That difference has a narrow but remarkable implication. The audible world, as experienced, is not a perfect transcription of the waveform. It is the nervous system's interpretation of available evidence. In ordinary conversation that integration can be useful: seeing a face may help when a voice is muffled. In a manipulated experiment it becomes visible as an error. The same broad capacity can support accurate communication in one context and misleading certainty in another.

The original report is frequently shortened to "everyone hears da." That is wrong. It observed a pattern in its participants and stimuli; it did not establish one response for every ear, language, face, recording, or hearing condition. Nor did the researchers establish that belief, hypnosis, meditation, or an external "frequency" can unlock the effect. They changed the correspondence between sound and visible articulation.

This is important for Final Vestige. A disciplined investigation asks what conditions were changed, what was held still, what was observed, and which explanations were ruled out. It does not convert an unusual experience into a preferred theory.

### A second experiment inside every demonstration

A careful demonstration has at least three conditions. First: listen to the actual audio with the speaker's mouth out of view. Second: see the video with its matching or mismatching audio. Third: return to audio-only. Record answers before revealing the expected response. Keep volume, playback speed, listening environment, and response choices constant where possible.

Why repeat the audio-only condition? Because it exposes an alternative explanation: perhaps the sound itself was merely unclear. If the same sound is reported differently when the visible articulation is changed, that comparison isolates the visual contribution more convincingly than showing only a surprising montage. If responses never change, the observer has not "failed a brain test." This effect varies. Audio compression, lip visibility, timing, hearing, language background, and response format can matter.

We are **not** embedding the original 1976 film or another creator's copyrighted demonstration. Nor are we depicting an invented demonstration as if viewers have experienced the illusion. The original Nature paper is linked in the source trail. Any later FV audiovisual experiment will need an original rights-cleared speaker recording, original sound capture, synchronized and intentionally incongruent variants, accessibility review, and a precise methods note. An SVG poster of a mouth is not a valid substitute for moving audiovisual speech. [1]

### When the group average hides the person

Decades after the celebrated discovery, researchers asked a different question: how consistent is the illusion *across people*? A study of 360 participants and 20 different McGurk stimuli found very large variation between listeners and between stimulus clips. Across repeated trials, some people reported no McGurk fusion responses and others reported them every time. Switching from open-ended answers to forced-choice answers increased the estimated frequency of a McGurk response by about 18 percentage points. [2]

Notice how much that changes the story. Suppose a social video offers only three labeled buttons, and one of those buttons reads "da." A viewer choosing that button has not necessarily had the same perception as a viewer who volunteered the word with no options. The answer format is part of the experiment. Neither is automatically fraudulent, but the second cannot be reconstructed from the first.

This is a particularly useful warning for an evidence-led publisher. When a creator says "most people hear this," the sample, stimuli, and form of the question matter. The famous result may survive scrutiny, yet an inflated retelling can still be misleading.

In 2018, Brown and colleagues tested candidate explanations for those individual differences. Their analysis associated susceptibility with a more specific ability to extract place-of-articulation information from visual speech. It did **not** find the expected association with the tested measures of attentional control, processing speed, working memory, or auditory perceptual gradiency. The authors described the explained share as small. That does not mean attention never contributes under any circumstances; it means broad claims that this particular illusion diagnoses weak concentration are not supported by those measures. [3]

This distinction is crucial to any proposed commercial bridge. A person who rarely reports an illusory "da" has not been shown to need a focus product. A person who reports it consistently has not demonstrated special suggestibility, poor listening, or a brain "in need of rewiring." The effect cannot be used as a screening tool for Zen12, hypnosis audio, or Brainwave Shots.

### The surprising problem with comparisons between groups

A further paper in 2018 used empirical results and simulations to examine published group differences in McGurk susceptibility. It warned that when only statistically significant group comparisons reach publication, the apparent size of differences can be exaggerated. [4] A 2025 study compared native Mandarin speakers in China with native English speakers in the United States across nine stimuli and 307 people. Frequencies were similar between groups, even as substantial person-to-person and clip-to-clip variation persisted. In that study the main cultural-group effect explained only a very small proportion of the variability. [5]

Neither paper proves cultural experience is irrelevant to every form of audiovisual speech processing. Each limits how confidently we can turn a vivid public demonstration into a sweeping statement about national populations or personal cognitive traits. A striking effect and an overbroad theory can coexist. That is not a defect in science; it is the reason science needs replications, careful sampling, and estimates of uncertainty.

### Does the effect reach the brain's processing of sound?

One possible explanation is merely that people change their answer when they see lips move. But EEG experiments have probed earlier auditory processing. Abbott and Shahin used consonant-vowel syllables and measured an N1 auditory-evoked component. Their results suggested that visual speech information could influence neural representations relevant to the heard consonant. [6] This is interesting evidence for audiovisual processing beyond a late, verbal guess.

Still, even a measured brain response is not proof of a commercial outcome. A neural pattern obtained with deliberately mismatched speech syllables does not establish that a twelve-minute background soundtrack boosts productivity, treats anxiety, improves memory, or permanently changes mental states. Those are different interventions and different endpoints. A common vocabulary about "the brain" does not make two experiments equivalent.

The temptation to make that leap is familiar. A scanner image appears; a marketer says "scientifically proven"; an ordinary buyer is invited to fill in the rest. Final Vestige's answer is to ask for the *exact bridge*: product, intervention, dose, population, credible comparison group, measured outcome, size of effect, and independent replication.

### The historical echo: when an experience becomes a theory

There is a reason this investigation belongs beside the Studio's Puységur files, even though an eighteenth-century magnetic séance and a modern speech laboratory have different subjects.

In 1784 Benjamin Franklin's commission tested Charles Deslon's animal magnetism claims using conditions that separated expectation from the allegedly active treatment. A subject might report sensations when she believed a magnetic action was occurring, even when the action was absent; an alleged magnetic influence could fail to have its predicted effects when the subject did not know it was directed toward her. The surviving commission report rejected the proposed magnetic fluid as an explanation, without denying that people had experiences. Final Vestige's Mesmerism investigation follows the experimental record and its limitations. [7]

Puységur subsequently built extensive testimony around what he called animal magnetism and artificial somnambulism. The Studio's source archive explicitly preserves failures, contradictions, uncertainties, and original terminology. It does not treat the words "cure," "vision," or "rapport" as scientific validation of modern therapies. [8]

The analogy is about **method**, not a claim that Puységur's subjects experienced the McGurk effect. A report of an experience is one kind of evidence; an experiment that identifies its cause is another. People can sincerely describe what they perceived while remaining mistaken about why they perceived it. Taking that distinction seriously is fairer to the witness as well as to the reader.

### A free twelve-minute field investigation: record the distinction

You do not need a subscription, special audio, headphones, a volunteer, or a new account to practice the underlying research habit. This is **not a McGurk experiment**, and it does not measure cognitive improvement. It is a notebook exercise in separating observation from inference.

**Minutes 0–2 — Make two columns.** Label a page "I observed" and "I inferred." Choose a short, ordinary conversation or a brief public news clip that you have legal permission to watch. Do not secretly record anyone. If using a clip, do not assume its subtitles are a ground-truth transcript.

**Minutes 2–5 — Observe without replay.** Write down three things you actually heard and two things you saw. Avoid assigning intention. "The speaker looked away" is an observation; "the speaker was dishonest" is an inference. This practice alone is more demanding than it sounds.

**Minutes 5–8 — Replay or verify.** If the source is replayable, listen once with the image concealed and compare your immediate wording with the clip's words. If there is no replay, simply mark the claims you cannot independently check. Do not invent remembered precision.

**Minutes 8–10 — Name an alternative.** For each inference, propose at least one other explanation consistent with the observation. Separate uncertainty about the event from uncertainty about your memory of it.

**Minutes 10–12 — Write the evidence limit.** Complete this sentence: "I directly observed ___; I cannot determine ___ without ___." A useful session can end with fewer confident claims than it began with.

### When a sound product appears next to a hearing illusion

Zen12's official site describes guided, twelve-minute listening sessions, a staged twelve-level course, and multiple MP3 packages. Hypnosis Live's *Total Focus* describes a 33-minute recording. Brainwave Shots' *Focus Shot* describes 15- and 30-minute audio versions. These descriptions can legitimately tell a buyer what form a product takes. They cannot prove that the product changes the reader's attention or mental health. [10–12]

The Zen12 sales page makes much stronger statements, including an asserted equivalence between twelve minutes of its audio and an hour of advanced meditation. That comparison is **seller advertising**, not a conclusion from the McGurk research. The same boundary applies to claims of "reprogramming" from hypnosis product descriptions or instant concentration gains from Brainwave Shots. The studies here were not trials of those branded products.

A person may value structured listening for its format, pacing, or convenience. Someone else may prefer a free self-directed practice. Both are reasonable preferences, but neither is a medical or cognitive-performance result. Before paying, verify the current package, price, refund terms and what is being promised; ask what independent trial actually studied the finished product. If the honest answer is "none available," that belongs in the buying decision.

### What this record can and cannot support

**Supported:** under specific conditions, sight of a speaker's articulation alters reported speech perception; responses differ substantially between stimuli and individuals; answer format matters; at least some studies find neural correlates of audiovisual interaction. [1–6]

**Not supported:** every viewer hears "da"; a missed fusion diagnoses poor focus; unusual perceptions demonstrate paranormal mechanisms; a branded meditation or hypnosis MP3 fixes the problem; the illusion proves an accelerated meditation session; anecdotal calm proves a medical outcome; any paid course produces guaranteed success.

That is the more interesting story. A person's experience can be vivid and authentic without being a faithful map of external events. The responsibility of an investigator is not to discredit the experience. It is to keep the evidence from being made to say more than it knows.
