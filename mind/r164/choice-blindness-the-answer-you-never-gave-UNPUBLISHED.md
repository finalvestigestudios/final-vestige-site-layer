# The Answer You Never Gave
## Choice blindness, hidden substitutions, and the stories we tell about decisions

Imagine being shown two photographs, choosing the face you find more appealing, and then being asked to explain your selection. You begin with confidence: the eyes seemed kinder, perhaps, or the expression felt familiar. But there is a problem. The photograph now in your hand is not the one you chose.

The replacement was deliberate. In an experiment, the researcher quietly exchanged the result without telling you. The remarkable part is not merely that you failed to notice. It is that you may be able to explain the *wrong* result with details that sound entirely sincere.

This is **choice blindness**: in certain controlled situations, people do not recognize a mismatch between a decision they made and the outcome they are subsequently shown. The result threatens a comfortable assumption about introspection. We know what we want because we can explain it. But what if some explanations are constructed *after* we see the result?

In 2005, Petter Johansson, Lars Hall, Sverker Sikström, and Andreas Olsson published a study in *Science* that helped name the phenomenon. Participants compared pairs of faces and selected the one they considered more attractive. On selected trials, a concealed manipulation meant participants were presented with the face they **had not chosen**. The investigators asked for reasons. People sometimes accepted the substituted photograph and gave reasons for the selection they believed they had made. [1]

The study did **not** show that all decisions are illusions, that people have no stable preferences, or that every decision can be changed by a clever trick. It demonstrated a specific mismatch-detection vulnerability under a particular procedure. The gap between those statements matters. It is where the experiment becomes an investigation rather than a slogan.

## A magician's move, a scientific question

The manipulation borrowed something from stage magic: timing. The participant had already completed a decision. A seemingly routine action then transformed its recorded outcome. Once the substitution was successful, an interview gathered explanations from someone who did not necessarily know the starting condition had changed.

The critical methodological point is the separation of three observations: **the recorded choice**, **the outcome presented**, and **the account of why the outcome seemed right**. Without that separation, a fluent verbal explanation might be mistaken for a transparent window into the process that produced the original decision.

An explanation is evidence that a participant can produce an explanation. It is not, by itself, proof that the explanation caused the initial choice. That does not make the participant dishonest. A person can try to be truthful while lacking access to the information required to recognize that a substitution has occurred.

We usually encounter this distinction in small ways. After buying something, we may emphasize its virtues because we own it. After choosing an option, we may remember its advantages more clearly than the reasons we doubted it. Those everyday experiences are **illustrations, not replications of the experiment**. Other psychological processes could explain them.

The laboratory finding is narrower and more powerful: if investigators keep a record of what was chosen, they can compare it with what someone later reports and see that the story does not always track the original act.

## The jam that changed after the tasting

The next test removed photographs from the picture entirely. In a 2010 paper in *Cognition*, Hall and colleagues described a supermarket preference experiment: people **tasted two jams or smelled two teas** and chose one. The researchers used paired, double-ended containers with different samples inside. An experimenter covertly **flipped each container after its initial sampling, before the participant's final selection**. When the participant sampled the supposedly chosen jam again—or smelled the supposedly chosen tea—the container instead presented the unchosen alternative, and the participant was asked to explain the choice. This design was a taste-and-smell mismatch, not two jam-and-tea tasting tasks and not a new swap carried out after the final selection. [2]

Across manipulated trials, no more than a third of the substitutions were detected, according to the paper's abstract. Even relatively distinct pairs did not always produce detection. That result extends the choice-blindness question into sensory judgments with ordinary consumer goods.

But this was not a retail-conversion experiment. It did not measure advertising performance, checkout behavior, post-purchase satisfaction, or willingness to pay for a meditation product. It tested whether people detected substitutions in a taste-and-smell procedure.

The lesson for readers is not 'your senses are useless.' It is to keep the **original record** separate from the **subsequent account**. If a claim about a product can be backed only by a persuasive description written after the fact, what independent evidence shows that the promised effect occurred?

## The harder questions: moral opinions and political responses

Researchers later adapted the paradigm to stated attitudes. In a 2012 *PLOS ONE* paper, a self-transforming paper questionnaire exposed respondents to reversals of some moral-opinion answers. The researchers found that 69 percent of participants failed to detect at least one of two reversals, and that participants sometimes argued for the reversed position. [3]

A 2013 follow-up paper examined the method in a Swedish-election political survey. It reported striking rates of undetected manipulated answers and shifts in expressed willingness to consider a different coalition preference. [4]

Both studies belong in a careful account of what people can fail to notice. Neither justifies saying that people's political identities are fake, that a survey can permanently change a vote, or that manipulating someone is ethically acceptable. The interventions involved deliberately engineered discrepancies in research settings. How durable the reported attitudes were outside the study is a separate question; expressing a revised response is not the same as casting a ballot.

This distinction is particularly important online. A dramatic video might splice the before-and-after answers and declare that minds are infinitely programmable. That would turn interesting empirical work into a claim the work cannot bear. An edited clip can demonstrate the setup; it cannot show all the controls, exclusions, and limitations unless those are explained.

## What an explanation is—and is not

For decades, researchers have debated how much access people have to the causes of their choices. A 2006 conceptual and empirical article by Johansson and colleagues placed choice blindness in dialogue with Nisbett and Wilson's influential discussion of verbal reports on mental processes. [5] That is a research debate about inference and introspection, not a claim that introspection has no value.

There are several competing possibilities whenever someone gives a polished explanation of a substituted choice. The participant may not notice the substitution; may notice some discrepancy but interpret it as unimportant; may accept the displayed outcome as a cue; or may be doing their best to make sense of an unfamiliar situation. A single interview statement cannot cleanly distinguish all those accounts.

Experiments are constructed to investigate that ambiguity. For an ordinary reader, the practical consequence is humility about explanations. Your memory of an intention, a screenshot of a choice, and a later explanation are three different kinds of evidence.

The methodological counterpart is a decision log: write down *what you chose and why* before learning how it turned out. Later you can compare the record with your recollection without relying solely on how coherent the later story sounds. This method cannot eliminate bias, but it keeps the starting condition visible.

## A free, consent-based demonstration

Here is an original reflection exercise that makes no attempt to deceive you or claim to reproduce the published manipulations.

1. Pick an ordinary, low-stakes decision: a book to read, a route for a walk, or a background sound for a work session. Choose among two acceptable options.
2. **Before** using the option, write three reasons for your selection and one reason you hesitated. Put the date beside the entry.
3. Return after a day or two. Without opening the entry, write what you now remember as your main reason.
4. Read the original note. Which reasons survived? Did the later account introduce a consideration you never recorded?
5. Mark the outcome: *consistent*, *partly changed*, or *not enough information*. Do not score yourself as 'choice blind'; the concealed-substitution protocol was absent.

The exercise is useful as a paper trail, not as a diagnostic test or a promise of better memory. If your answers differ, the difference might arise from ordinary forgetting, new information, changed priorities, or retrospective interpretation. Your notes alone cannot tell which.

It has a second use for buyers: when evaluating a premium meditation course, a self-hypnosis recording, or another advertised mental-performance tool, write down the **actual merchant offer**, what benefit you hope to obtain, the cost and refund conditions, and which claims are independently supported. Then make the purchase decision from the record, not from the excitement of a repeated sales slogan.

A free five-minute breathing or observation session can be tried without buying anything. Whether it helps you personally is an observation, not a clinical or commercial efficacy claim.

## From the stage of animal magnetism to the laboratory

Final Vestige's [Puységur Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) investigate a different historical world: the reports, performances, interpretive authority, and evolving language of eighteenth-century magnetic sleep.

Choice-blindness experiments do not validate animal magnetism. The historical cases do not establish the mechanism of modern perception experiments. Their point of contact is the evidentiary problem: the person who explains an extraordinary event may not have access to all the conditions that produced it.

In historical manuscripts, we ask who observed an event, who recorded it, how much time passed, and what the observer already believed. In the laboratory, a record of the selected photograph or questionnaire answer establishes the critical discrepancy. The same habit of thought—keep the raw observation apart from the confident explanation—can serve both inquiries.

There is a related ethical boundary. It is fair to show an audience a scientific demonstration with appropriate context. It is not fair to use concealed manipulation or ambiguity to make someone buy a product. A person should be able to inspect an offer, seek primary sources, choose a free alternative, and walk away without being told that hesitation proves a psychological deficiency.

## Three things the research does not prove

**It does not prove that every decision is unstable.** Some substitutions are detected. Task design, plausibility, expectations, timing and the type of response matter. One paradigm should not be universalized into a theory of human choice.

**It does not prove that someone can 'reprogram' a preference through audio.** The studies above concern covert substitutions in experimental choice tasks, not the effects of prerecorded self-hypnosis, brainwave audio, or Zen12 Premium. A study can be genuinely interesting and completely irrelevant to a particular advertised benefit.

**It does not prove that manipulating consumer choices causes purchase.** Detecting fewer substitutions is not the same outcome as independently confirmed paid orders. Any claims about a product's effectiveness or a campaign's revenue need their own appropriate evidence.

These are not technical footnotes. They are the difference between a reliable Mind & Perception Archive and a storefront that borrows the authority of science.

## The record before the story

Choice blindness raises an uncomfortable but productive question: how much of the reason we give is a report of what happened, and how much is our mind making sense of what seems to have happened?

The researchers did not resolve the entire philosophy of free will. They built a way to catch a mismatch that introspection could sometimes miss. That is the valuable result. A record of the earlier choice survived when a later explanation did not.

We need not abandon trust in our own judgments to learn from it. We can keep records, seek independent confirmation, and allow ourselves to revise a conclusion when better evidence appears. The most useful response to an unreliable witness—especially when that witness is oneself—is not cynicism. It is a better archive.
