# The Broken Glass Nobody Saw
## How a question can enter the record as a memory

**Final Vestige Studios · Mind & Perception / Evidence & Witnesses · UNPUBLISHED research draft · November 9, 2026**

*Editorial status: source review required before any public release. This is an investigation, not a diagnosis, legal opinion, treatment recommendation, or affiliate endorsement. No original participant testimony has been fabricated.*

The glass was not in the film.

That is the important detail. Before a question was asked about a collision, before a witness tried to recall the scene a week later, before the experiment became one of psychology's most memorable demonstrations, the relevant footage had already established what viewers could have seen. A window or windshield had not supplied the broken glass some participants would later report. The added material came from language, not the original image.

In 1974, psychologists Elizabeth Loftus and John Palmer published a deceptively simple investigation of automobile-accident memory. People watched films and answered questions about the events. The researchers changed a word in a question about speed. In one form, the question suggested that cars had *smashed* into one another; other forms used less forceful verbs. The wording influenced the speed estimates. In a subsequent experiment, the group exposed to the more forceful verb was more likely to report broken glass a week later, although the relevant film contained none. This is a bounded, experimental finding, not proof that any particular witness is always unreliable. [1]

For an archive that investigates accidents, claims, conflicting documents and witnesses, the lesson is unsettling but practical: a record can be accurate about what someone *now* recalls while mistaken about what the original event contained. And the process that makes a detail seem familiar may be completely ordinary.

### The two records

Imagine setting two folders on a desk. The first contains what existed at the time of an incident: images, contemporaneous notes, physical evidence and independently collected measurements. The second contains later accounts: descriptions prompted by interviewers, retellings, news reports, public comments, confident reconstructions. Both deserve preservation. They are not interchangeable.

The distinction is central to the memory experiments, and it is also central to good historical method. A later statement may be sincere without being a direct recording of the earlier event. A person may change an estimate because a question suggests a different level of force, or report a feature that seems consistent with the reconstructed episode.

This does not mean language mechanically inserts every suggested detail into a person's memory. The 1974 report established group-level shifts under controlled conditions. Its questions, stimuli, delays, participant sampling, and outcomes matter. It would be wrong to jump from a laboratory effect to a verdict on a specific courtroom witness or historian. That would replace careful inference with an equally confident overclaim.

### How the 1974 experiment worked

The researchers showed participants films of automobile accidents, then questioned them. Different groups were given different verbs in questions about the vehicles' speeds. The films themselves did not change from one wording condition to another. That separation let the researchers examine the effect of *postevent language*.

In the original paper's first experiment, the critical measure was a speed estimate rather than a sensor reading. People were not sitting in the vehicles with calibrated instruments. A reported speed reflects reconstruction and judgment. The more forceful wording produced higher average estimates than gentler wording in the reported comparisons. The finding is about the wording used to solicit an estimate, not a demonstration that participants visually perceived a different measured speed.

The second experiment asked a delayed memory question involving broken glass. One week after the film, the groups differed in their reports of a glass detail that had not appeared in the footage. Even this striking result leaves more than one plausible cognitive explanation: the postevent wording could reshape a remembered representation, affect the standard for saying “yes,” change what seems likely, or interact with later reconstruction. The study documents the outcome under its procedure; the exact underlying mechanism cannot be settled by repeating its memorable headline.

That last caveat is easy to omit when popular accounts translate a complicated paper into a one-sentence claim that memory is fake. But the very reason to study an error experimentally is that memory often works well enough to be worth studying. A useful finding points to the conditions under which confidence, perception and accurate source identification diverge. [1]

### A larger test, with a different manipulation

Four years later, Loftus, David Miller and Helen Burns published another experimental series, **Semantic integration of verbal information into a visual memory**. Across five experiments and a pilot, the researchers studied a total of **1,242 participants**, who saw slides depicting an automobile–pedestrian accident.

After the slides, participants received information that could be consistent with what they had seen, irrelevant to it, or misleading. Later recognition measures included yes–no choices and forced choices between two alternatives. The abstract reports lower accuracy after misleading postevent information on both types of test. [2]

This matters because a later question is not the only possible contaminating influence. An interview, another witness, a caption, a photograph's description, an internal summary or an apparently authoritative statement can arrive *after* the event and alter how evidence is later retrieved. The study's overall sample count also should not be mistaken for the size of every experimental condition or used to invent precision for a specific comparison.

The authors titled the paper around “semantic integration,” but it does not license the claim that every reported error was a permanent rewriting of a stored picture. In memory research, performance on a recognition task is an observation; the cognitive mechanism proposed to explain it is an inference. Alternative accounts can involve source monitoring, retrieval competition, familiarity, inference or decision criteria. The experiment shows an effect requiring explanation, not a photograph of memory being physically overwritten.

### The role of repetition—and what did not add much

A more recent question is whether false information becomes more potent simply because different people appear to supply it. If three distinct sources repeat a claim, does that make it stronger than the same claim repeated three times by one source?

Rachel O'Donnell and colleagues addressed this distinction in work published in 2023. Across two experiments they varied repetition and source variability. One experiment conveyed postevent information through written interview transcripts; another used video sources so individual witnesses were more salient. The authors reported that repetition increased suggestibility in their experiments, while increasing the number of apparent sources did not produce an additional effect under their tested conditions. The accompanying meta-analyses considered **19 effect sets on repetition** and **eight on source variability**. [3]

This is not a universal conclusion that source diversity never matters. It is evidence that the intuitive explanation “more separate people made it convincing” did not account for the particular effects these researchers tested after repetition was taken into account. It is also a useful warning for today's online archive: repeated wording can create a feeling of familiarity even if multiple posts ultimately trace to the same unverified origin.

Nor should this be conflated with a coordinated disinformation campaign, an eyewitness's motives, or a clinical memory disorder. Those are different questions with different measurements.

### Correction is a separate experiment

Correcting an error is not automatically the mirror image of introducing one. Nathan Walter and Riva Tukachinsky's 2019 meta-analysis examined the **continued influence of misinformation following correction**, aggregating **32 studies involving 6,527 participants**. Its abstract reports that, on average, correction did not entirely erase misinformation's influence, although it identified conditions associated with more successful correction. [4]

Crucially, many continued-influence studies concern reasoning with false explanatory information, not necessarily the same episodic memory paradigm used in the 1974 crash films. A reader should not combine these bodies of research into a single numerical claim that an individual eyewitness remains wrong after correction by some predictable amount. That transfer would require a justified comparison of outcomes and protocols.

What can reasonably be taken from the combined evidence is a set of questions: when did the witness encounter the later claim? How often was it repeated? Who supplied it? What was the timing of the first uncontaminated account? Was the correction made by the source of the original error? And was the later response about a sensory memory, a factual judgment or a narrative explanation?

Those questions are useful to both a scientist constructing a study and an editor reconstructing a record.

### A historical mirror: the investigator as a source of suggestion

The eighteenth-century history of animal magnetism presents a different, much earlier encounter with the problem of suggestion. Final Vestige's **Puységur's Animal Magnetism Files** preserve source trails, case dossiers and historical material describing magnetic somnambulism, expectations, witness observations and extraordinary cure claims. The archive is evidence of what participants and authors *reported*, not an independent clinical validation of the extraordinary effects. [5]

It would be anachronistic to say the 1974 misinformation experiments explain every event in a late-eighteenth-century magnetic salon. No comparable random assignment, measurement system, or well-controlled reconstruction is available for most historical cases. But the historian can ask a related methodological question: was an observation recorded before the expected outcome was named? Who introduced the wording? Did witnesses speak independently? Was a dramatic effect described only in a later printed retelling?

In the same period, French royal commissioners examining Deslon-associated animal magnetism attempted to separate the influence of imagination and expectation from claims about an invisible physical fluid. Those commission tests were historically separate from Puységur's own records. The distinction is important: collapsing the documents into one grand experiment invents continuity not established by the surviving chronology. [5, 6]

What links these historical and modern materials is not a proof of hypnosis, nor an argument that suggestion can achieve any advertised result. It is a discipline of separating what was observed, what was prompted, and what the surviving record permits us to conclude.

### When an editor should worry about the question

Consider a hypothetical archival interview about a factory incident. The interviewer asks, “When did you notice the **violent** shaking?” Perhaps shaking occurred; perhaps no observer used that adjective until the interviewer supplied it. If the interviewee later repeats the phrase, a subsequent reader might think two independent sources described violent movement. In fact, the phrase may have traveled from question to answer.

A safer interview would initially ask what the person remembers, with minimal prompts, then record follow-up questions exactly. Specific claims can be tested against instrumentation, contemporary notes and other independent sources. Neutral questioning does not make a person's account infallible, and a leading question does not automatically invalidate an entire interview. The point is to retain the chain of custody for language itself.

An editor should especially watch for the same evocative adjective repeating across documents that cite each other. The agreement may represent genuine corroboration, or it may reveal one unexamined sentence being copied through several layers. The investigation must trace the earliest independent appearance instead of counting citations as independent witnesses.

Likewise, a research brief should distinguish original stimuli and the conditions of an experiment from a later popular illustration. Presenting a staged image as though it were a historical photograph, or a newly invented eyewitness quote as archival testimony, defeats the very purpose of this archive.

### A four-column experiment any reader can do

You can explore the difference between observation and later interpretation without involving strangers, manipulating another person's memory or claiming to reproduce a laboratory result.

Select a public-domain photograph or an everyday still image that you are allowed to use. Look for ten seconds. Then cover it. Write four headings on paper: **Seen directly**, **Inferred**, **Learned afterward**, and **Still uncertain**.

Under the first heading, record concrete features you believe you saw: a striped awning, a clock face, a single open door. Under the second, write interpretations you made: perhaps the street was crowded, perhaps the building was a station. Then uncover the image and compare your original notes to what is actually visible. If you read a caption or historical description afterward, put anything newly learned in its own column instead of editing your original observation.

Try the exercise a second time with a different image and an intentionally neutral caption. The purpose is not to measure a false-memory rate. There is no randomization, blind interviewer, preregistered outcome, independent scoring or sufficient sample for a psychological conclusion. It is a recordkeeping drill that makes the origin of each statement visible.

If the exercise feels uncomfortable, simply stop. It is not therapy, a diagnostic tool or a recommended method of questioning children or vulnerable witnesses.

### What this does not mean about hypnosis or commercial audio

The misinformation effect is not the same as hypnotic responding, and neither is evidence that a meditation recording improves concentration. The 1974 and 1978 researchers manipulated postevent language and measured memory judgments. They did not test Zen12, Hypnosis Live, Brainwave Shots, binaural entrainment or a self-hypnosis intervention.

An audio seller may plausibly offer a structured file, a guided session, a predictable routine or a choice of background sounds. Those are product-format claims to check against the exact merchant offer. Claims that a recording **reprograms** memory, changes brainwaves in a clinically meaningful way, guarantees focus or matches an hour of meditation require a different, product-specific evidence trail. General research on memory, attention or suggestion cannot fill that gap.

### The practical case for a clean first account

The hardest version of the lesson is also the simplest: preserve the first account before it is polished. Record the exact question when a statement is prompted. Separate a contemporaneous note from a later reconstruction. Keep disconfirming evidence. When a claim moves through articles and social feeds, trace it backward to the original source instead of assuming repeated appearances are independent confirmations.

The automobile studies do not tell us that witnesses are liars. They show why sincere testimony and documented occurrence are different evidentiary categories. The distinction protects people as much as it protects historical accuracy.

The glass was absent from the film. The later report belonged to the record of what people said after a question. A careful archive preserves both facts without silently turning one into the other.
