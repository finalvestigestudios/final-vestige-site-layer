# The Experiment That Separated the Trance From the Suggestion
**Final Vestige Studios | Mind & Perception Archive | ORIGINAL INVESTIGATION — UNPUBLISHED REVIEW, November 13, 2026**

*When attention shifts under hypnosis, what exactly caused it: the words, the induction, the circumstances—or the person hearing them?*

### The test inside the test
Picture two ordinary computer screens. Both display a distracting word. The participant is instructed to respond to one feature and ignore another. The task is designed so that information nobody has been asked to attend to still competes for attention. A psychologist can count mistakes and milliseconds, but cannot directly watch the act of paying attention.

Now introduce another voice. It might ask the participant to disregard the meaning of a word. It might first invite the participant through a relaxing hypnotic induction. Or it might do both. If response times change, what has been demonstrated? Not mind control. Not a cure. Not the efficacy of a paid sound file. Something more specific: under these conditions, in these people, a particular procedure altered performance on a particular task.

That distinction is the core of this story. It turns an apparently simple statement—“hypnosis changes attention”—into four different hypotheses. One concerns what an instruction communicates. One concerns what an induction changes. One concerns who is susceptible to an instruction. And one concerns what people expect an experimenter wants to happen.

Those hypotheses are not interchangeable. Treating them as interchangeable is how a controlled laboratory observation can become an unearned consumer promise.

### A word that refuses to stay a word
One of psychology's most durable attention tests is the Stroop task. Readers see a color word printed in colored ink and name the ink rather than the word. When the word and the ink disagree, skilled readers often take longer or make more errors. Reading intrudes even when it is not wanted.

In 2002, Amir Raz, Theodore Shapiro, Jin Fan, and Michael Posner compared sixteen highly suggestible and sixteen less suggestible participants. Under a posthypnotic suggestion intended to impede word reading, Stroop interference was eliminated in their highly suggestible group, but the less suggestible controls did not show a significant reduction. This is a particularly striking experimental result because the usual Stroop competition can be hard to resist. It is also an unusually constrained result: thirty-two people, differentiated by suggestibility, carrying out a laboratory word-color task. [Raz et al., 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12470132/).

If an advertisement retold only the striking half, it might imply that a hypnotic recording can overcome unwanted automatic habits in any buyer. The less suggestible group is the first reason to reject that inference. The task itself is the second. Responding faster to printed words is not proof that a program improves real-world attention, protects memory, relieves a mental-health condition, or creates lasting behavior change.

### What happens when the induction is removed?
A later study asks a sharper question. In 2006, Raz and colleagues tested highly suggestible participants with a suggestion to see Stroop words as meaningless symbols. Half received it with a hypnotic induction; half received the suggestion without induction. The reported reduction in Stroop interference occurred in both conditions. The abstract notes that suggestion accounted for about 45% of the variance in Stroop responding in this experiment, **not** that 45% of ordinary readers would experience a benefit or that an advertised recording would be 45% effective. [Raz et al., 2006](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/j.1467-9280.2006.01669.x/).

The outcome takes some of the magic out of the machinery in the best scientific sense: it helps separate the potential causal work of the instruction from that of a formal induction. But a different protocol could yield different answers. “Induction is never necessary” does not follow from one experiment on highly suggestible people.

Think of a laboratory as a room full of deliberately controlled choices. A result tells us what happened when those choices were made. It does not automatically tell us what happens when a stranger buys an MP3, listens on a bus, uses earbuds, expects more concentration, and does not know their own level of suggestibility.

### A newer experiment crosses the two factors
In 2025, Jeremy Brunel, Stéphanie Mathey, Mathieu Landry and Sandrine Delord published an open-access study designed to pull induction and suggestion apart with a two-by-two comparison. Their first experiment involved 34 highly suggestible adults, predominantly young university students, completing an **emotional Stroop** task: an attention test using emotional information, not the same word-color test studied by Raz. [Brunel et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.concog.2025.103948).

Participants were tested across conditions that separated a relaxation-based hypnotic induction from an emotional-numbing suggestion. The authors found that induction alone, suggestion alone, and their combination each reduced measured attentional bias relative to control in the highly suggestible sample. Combining induction and suggestion did not produce a greater reduction than the relevant individual conditions under this protocol.

Their second experiment examined 38 low-suggestible people to probe whether repetition or demand-related features could explain the apparent effect. The researchers did not observe a significant corresponding modulation there. The two samples were not a population-representative consumer study; the design addressed specific hypotheses about emotional Stroop performance.

This result is valuable precisely because it is not a remake of the 2006 test. The experimental stimuli, the outcome, the manipulation, and the participant selection differ. Both studies provide evidence that suggestion can be experimentally consequential. The more recent study also supports a role for relaxation-based induction in its own tested conditions. Different mechanisms or routes may produce superficially similar behavioral endpoints.

The article's authors discuss the possibility that a relaxing induction operates partly as a suggestion in its own right. That is an interpretation supported by their experiment, not a settled description of every form of hypnosis.

### The question an EEG image cannot settle alone
The phrase “brain state” often appears as though it identifies one measurable, saleable thing. Yet hypnosis research distinguishes behavioral responsiveness, subjective experience, induction procedure, trait suggestibility and physiological correlates. An EEG trace is one measurement, not a receipt for a whole package of advertised benefits.

A 2024 systematic review by Anoushiravan Zahedi, Steven Jay Lynn and Werner Sommer compared prominent theories of hypnosis and found no consensus about how hypnotic suggestions produce their effects. Each explanatory framework carries strengths and limitations. The review does not make the laboratory findings meaningless; it warns against pretending that all the explanatory work has already been done. [Zahedi et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39032268/).

A 2024 review of brain functional correlates likewise considers EEG and brain-imaging findings in the context of variation in hypnotizability, rather than presenting a unique brainwave signature as a commercial performance guarantee. [De Pascalis, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38391691/).

A commercial argument can fail in more than one place. Research on suggestion is not identical to research on sound-induced entrainment. Research on listening to an audio track is not automatically research on a particular seller's recording. And changing a laboratory attentional bias is not equivalent to a reliably improved working day.

### The eighteenth-century archive of testimony
Final Vestige's [Puységur Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) offer a much older scene: late-eighteenth-century observers collecting speeches, witness statements, predictions, cures and repeated procedures as evidence for what they understood as animal magnetism.

This is not a straight historical line from Puységur to today's evidence-based hypnotic science. It is a contrast in ways of assigning cause. A witness could accurately report an experience without proving the magnetic mechanism proposed to explain it. An operator might record responses and genuine relief while still misunderstanding why they occurred. A document's existence supports the claim that someone recorded it—not automatically the underlying physical theory.

The 1784 royal commission examining animal magnetism, associated with Benjamin Franklin, also investigated the role of imagination and expectation. Final Vestige's existing [Mesmerism investigation](https://finalvestige.com/archive/) leads readers back to the public source trail. The archival point is methodological: separate **what people did and said** from **why they believed it happened**.

No claim in those eighteenth-century materials establishes the effectiveness of Zen12, Hypnosis Live or any modern recording.

### A demonstration you can run without buying anything
Before you visit a sales page, try a simple exercise. It will not measure your brainwaves or diagnose your attention, and it is not a substitute for a controlled Stroop experiment.

Choose a dull but useful task you can complete in twelve minutes: filing a small stack, outlining a paragraph, or sorting ten receipts. First write down a concrete endpoint, such as “six lines drafted” rather than “feel more focused.” Put your phone aside. Work for twelve minutes in a quiet, reasonably comfortable space. Count the completed units and one distraction that interrupted you. Do this on another day under similarly ordinary conditions; don't invent a missing baseline or score a subjective feeling as a clinical result.

What did the exercise actually test? Whether you could begin and observe a specific action. It did **not** establish that suggestion, induction, silence or audio caused a change. Changing your task, mood, expectations or environment can change the result.

### What the evidence proves, and what remains open
**Documented:** a 2002 Stroop study found an effect confined to its highly suggestible group; a 2006 experiment reported a suggestion effect with and without formal induction; a 2025 emotional-Stroop study separated induction and suggestion and found contributions in its high-suggestible sample.

**Interpretation:** under some protocols, spoken instructions, induction procedures and participant characteristics matter to measured attention. Researchers disagree about the best integrated explanatory theory.

**Not established:** a universal “hypnotic state,” the reliability of any specific commercial audio program for a general population, an hour-of-meditation equivalence, guaranteed focus, treatment of illness, or assured return on a purchase.

These distinctions do not make attention less interesting. They make it more interesting: a response can change even when the proposed explanation remains unsettled. That is the investigative question worth keeping, whether the evidence comes from a modern computer task or from a centuries-old archive.
