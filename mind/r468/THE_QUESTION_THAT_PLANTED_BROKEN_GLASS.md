## The glass that was never there

Imagine being shown a short film of a traffic accident. Then somebody asks how fast the cars were going when they *smashed* together. That word feels like part of the event. It isn't. It's part of the question.

A week later, you are asked whether you saw broken glass. There was none in the film. But the earlier wording changes who says yes.

This is not a metaphor for how gullible the public is. It was a controlled experiment, and the exact conditions matter. In 1974, Elizabeth Loftus and John Palmer published two studies of people viewing films of automobile accidents. Their first experiment enrolled 45 students and varied a verb in a speed-estimate question. The average estimate ranged from 31.8 miles per hour for *contacted* to 40.5 for *smashed*. Those figures are responses to different questions, not measurements of the actual film speeds. [1]

Their second experiment enrolled 150 students. Fifty were asked a question using *smashed*, fifty with *hit*, and fifty received no speed question. After a week they were asked about broken glass in a filmed collision that contained none. Sixteen of the *smashed* group said yes, compared with seven of the *hit* group and six of the control group. That is 32%, 14% and 12% of the corresponding 50-person groups. The difference is interesting precisely because the false detail was introduced by a word in an earlier question, not by broken glass on the screen. [1]

But a crucial question remains unanswered by those figures alone: did the person's memory representation change, or did the wording shift the criteria for giving an uncertain answer? Loftus and Palmer themselves discussed response bias as a competing interpretation of their first experiment. The second adds delayed reporting, but a later yes/no answer is not a recording of a hidden memory file. A careful account distinguishes *what people reported* from the unobservable experience that produced the report.

## The experimental chain: event, information, test

A useful way to read misinformation research is to draw three boxes.

**First:** the participant sees an event. **Second:** the participant receives material after the event, perhaps through a question, written account or another witness. **Third:** the researcher tests recall or recognition. If the post-event material is wrong, and later answers shift toward it, we have evidence about susceptibility to misinformation under those conditions.

In 1978, Loftus, David Miller and Helen Burns investigated that chain with a considerably larger series: 1,242 participants across five experiments and a pilot study. Participants saw slides depicting an auto-pedestrian accident, then received consistent, misleading or irrelevant information. On later yes/no and two-choice tests, misleading information reduced correct responding. The published abstract supports the broad result. It does not by itself specify every participant's subjective experience, demonstrate universal memory alteration or prove that every kind of post-event message works equally well. [2]

That distinction is especially valuable online. We often see a viral clip followed by a narrator's confident interpretation, followed by thousands of comments repeating the same presumed fact. An observer could later remember the narrator's description as though it were a visible feature of the clip. That is a *plausible risk illustrated by laboratory research*, not a demonstration that any particular social media viewer has formed a false memory. We should not pretend to have measured what we have not measured.

The safeguard is surprisingly physical: retain the original source separately from the commentary about it. Save a source location, date and exact wording before making a narrative claim. In a historical investigation, preserve the document image and the translated passage separately. In a clip, archive the original segment and list each later assertion as a claim to be checked.

## Repetition is not independent corroboration

A 2023 paper by Rachel O'Donnell, Jason Chan, Jeffrey Foster and Maryanne Garry tested another common assumption: does misinformation become more influential when delivered by *different people*, independently of how often a person hears it? The researchers separated repetition from the number of apparent sources in two experiments. Repetition increased susceptibility in their first experiment; changing source number alone did not show an additional effect there. A second experiment, using more salient video presenters rather than written interview transcripts, did not find the expected source-variability effect. The paper also presented meta-analyses involving 19 tests of repetition and eight of source variability. [3]

That result is a useful warning against an easy historical mistake. Five witnesses may be five independent observations. Or five witnesses may all be repeating a description that originated with one authoritative person. The *count of citations* is not the *count of independent sources*. The 2023 findings do not mean that source identity never matters. They show why one must actually separate frequency from origin before concluding that apparent corroboration is stronger evidence.

The same correction applies to modern commercial self-improvement pages. A manufacturer can quote a general study, a reviewer can repeat the manufacturer's interpretation, and several blogs can repeat the review. The four references are not necessarily four demonstrations that the particular product works. If one study tested a different population, different intervention or different outcome, repetition has not solved the gap.

## A witness can repeat something they did not see

Laboratory findings about discussion between witnesses add another layer. In a study by Helen Paterson, Richard Kemp and Sarah McIntyre, participants watched a crime video and then discussed it in groups. Some encountered false information from discussion partners. The researchers later tested independent accounts and investigated whether warnings or source monitoring could limit the incorporation of misinformation. Co-witness information did enter testimony; the reported warning and source-monitoring manipulations did not eliminate the phenomenon in those experiments. [4]

Here too, do not overgeneralize. It does not establish that warnings are always useless or that a witness has no agency. It does show that merely telling someone to remember accurately is not a substitute for preventing contamination of an original account. A good investigative practice is to capture what a witness says before other witnesses, narrators or investigators introduce competing details. The timing and wording of the first account are part of its provenance.

The experiment also helps explain why confident historical testimony deserves neither reflexive dismissal nor blind acceptance. People may sincerely recount a detail whose provenance they no longer distinguish. Sincerity, memory strength and independent external evidence answer different questions.

## The eighteenth-century archive that makes the problem tangible

Final Vestige's [Puységur Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) follow an eighteenth-century system of extraordinary claims into its own documentary apparatus. Puységur and his circle collected reported behavior, patient speech, predictions, witness attestations and written records to build a case for animal magnetism. The archive preserves failures and ethical limits alongside the claims: a failed cure, a failed prediction, doubts and warning notes do not disappear merely because they complicate the story. [5]

This archive is not a retrospective diagnosis of individual patients, and the modern Loftus experiments cannot tell us which eighteenth-century statement was mistaken. That would be a category error. Its methodological relevance is sharper: *who first supplied a description, who repeated it, who heard it before testifying, and what independent record survives?*

If a dramatic prediction is quoted in a later memoir, locate the earlier dated document. If an unusual action is described by several people, ask whether each person observed it separately. If a patient's words are given in translation, mark what is a faithful source quotation, what is an editor's paraphrase, and what is a later inference. A claim does not become evidence merely because it has a formal wrapper of ink, signatures or citations. Conversely, a historical record may establish that a person genuinely reported an experience without proving the proposed physical cause.

The best question is not, 'How could they have believed this?' It is, 'How was the claim recorded, transmitted and tested—and what survives of the original event?'

## What the laboratory cannot prove about hypnosis

It would be tempting to walk from 'questions influence memory' to 'hypnosis reshapes the mind' to 'a particular hypnosis download reliably produces a promised result'. None of those arrows is licensed by the traffic-accident experiment. The Loftus studies concern memory reports after leading questions, not a randomized evaluation of Zen12, Hypnosis Live or Brainwave Shots. Nor does a historical account of magnetic somnambulism establish contemporary product efficacy.

There *is* legitimate research on certain clinical hypnosis protocols. For example, a 2025 meta-analysis of 20 randomized trials involving 1,250 patients studied hypnosis around invasive medical procedures and found average reductions in anxiety and pain compared with standard care, alongside important differences in protocol and outcome measurement. Those procedures, populations and interventions are distinct from a consumer MP3 claimed to improve virtually any aspect of life. This is evidence for the studied circumstances, not transferable proof of a particular marketed product. [6]

For the brainwave-audio question, a 2023 systematic review of fourteen binaural-beat EEG studies reported five results consistent with entrainment, eight contradictory and one mixed; heterogeneity limited the strength of pooled interpretation. That review did not test Zen12 and cannot prove its proprietary recordings work. [7]

## A twelve-minute source audit anyone can perform

For the first two minutes, write a claim on a page without adding an explanation. Choose something testable: 'This historical witness reported an event before the relevant date' or 'This video clearly shows the object crossing the frame.' Avoid using a claim about your own trauma, health or another person's private experience.

For minutes three and four, find the earliest primary document or unedited media segment you can access. Record the creator, date, location and one exact neutral observation. If you cannot find it, put 'original unavailable'. Do not silently replace the original with a summary.

For minutes five and six, list the later descriptions you have encountered. Circle words that *evaluate* rather than report: 'shocking', 'clearly', 'impossible', 'proven'. Highlight any fact that appears first in the description instead of the source. This is the point at which a question, headline or voice-over might introduce information that was not available to the original viewer.

For minutes seven through nine, make a provenance tree. An independent observation earns a separate branch. Reposts, quotations and paraphrases of the same original retain a shared trunk. Five copies of one claim do not become five independent witnesses.

For minutes ten and eleven, attempt a disconfirming check. What would you expect to see in the primary record if the leading description were wrong? Can you identify a missing scene, timestamp, original language, conflicting date, failed prediction or unacknowledged alternative? It is permissible to finish with 'not determined'.

In the final minute, write two sentences: **What the surviving source directly establishes** and **What remains inference**. Keep them separate. You now have something more useful than a feeling of certainty: a small record that another reader could audit.

## The limits are the story

The broken glass was not in the film. The false memory claim was not written in the film either. What the study established is a measurable change in participants' answers after a question changed. Across later research, post-event information, repetition and discussion can influence reported recollection, with effects depending on design and context.

That makes a historical witness neither a fool nor an oracle. It makes the chain between an event and its retelling worth investigating. The moment a confident narrator tells you what the source 'must' mean is exactly the moment to ask whether you have seen the evidence yourself.
