# The Choice You Never Made: When the Evidence Changed After the Decision
**Final Vestige Studios · Mind & Perception · original investigation · unpublished source manuscript · 2026-11-18**

*Meta description (draft):* The 2005 choice-blindness experiment secretly switched the face participants chose. The unsettling result has lessons for eyewitnesses, archival testimony, and claims that audio can change the mind.

## The photograph in your hand

Picture two portraits on a table. They are not the same person. You point to one because, for reasons you could probably explain, you find that face more appealing. The experimenter takes the cards, moves a hand over the table, and returns your selection. Then comes the question: *Why did you choose this one?*

There is a possibility you were never asked to consider. The card in your hand may be the face you rejected.

The question is not whether the experimenter can fool someone with a card trick. It is whether the person who made the choice will notice that the result has changed, and what happens to the explanation when the evidence in front of them conflicts with a decision made seconds before. It is a smaller, more revealing question than whether people lack free will.

In 2005, Petter Johansson, Lars Hall, Sverker Sikström and Andreas Olsson published “Failure to Detect Mismatches Between Intention and Outcome in a Simple Decision Task” in *Science*. Their participants chose between photographs of faces. On selected trials the researchers used sleight of hand to substitute the unchosen portrait and asked the participant to explain the result. [Original study, DOI 10.1126/science.1111709](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1111709); [research summary from the Swedish Research Council, dated October 7, 2005](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2005/10/051007091302.htm).

A person who detected the trick might object immediately. But the startling cases were those in which a person looked at the wrong face, apparently accepted it as the one they had selected, and supplied an explanation. These explanations were not necessarily deliberate lies. The researchers called the phenomenon **choice blindness**.

The title requires care. It does not mean no participant noticed. It does not prove that every personal preference is imaginary. It names a measurable mismatch between reported choice and actual outcome under particular experimental conditions.

## The moment the record was switched

The original paper reported 354 manipulated trials after exclusions, with 46 switches—approximately 13 percent—noticed during the task itself. Detection measured later was higher, and the result depended on how detection was counted. The study manipulated some decisions, not every card, and assessed the participants' reports under experimental conditions. The number is a proportion of manipulated *trials*, not an estimate that 87 percent of people in ordinary life would accept any lie.

A stronger way to describe the result is: in this experiment, many mismatches went unnoticed even when the participants had just made the relevant decision. The investigators compared how subjects justified manipulated and unmanipulated choices; the observed accounts often failed to reveal that the outcomes differed. That is a problem for anyone treating confident first-person explanation as a mechanically perfect record of how a decision occurred.

There is a second caution. The setup made some information unavailable, and an expert created the swap. The finding need not imply that the mind routinely overwrites every memory. One explanation is that people represent the original choice less durably than they imagine. Another concerns their assumptions about the reliability of the experimenter and the card presented as the chosen result. The observation cannot, by itself, isolate an exhaustive mechanism of introspection.

Put another way: the evidence establishes a vulnerability in a task. It does not grant researchers a magic dial for controlling preferences.

## A political survey that returned different answers

Choice blindness did not remain confined to photographs. A later study by Lars Hall and colleagues, published April 10, 2013 in *PLOS ONE*, gave participants political attitude items during Sweden's 2010 election. The researchers covertly altered selected survey responses to appear as if the participant had answered on the opposite side of a political question. They then invited participants to explain the altered responses.

The abstract reports that no more than 22 percent of manipulated responses were detected, that 92 percent accepted and endorsed the altered survey score, and that 48 percent (with a reported uncertainty interval) indicated willingness to consider a left-right coalition change. [Hall et al., 2013, DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0060554](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0060554); [PubMed study record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23593244/).

That final distinction is indispensable. **Willingness to consider a political change is not evidence that 48 percent actually changed their vote.** Neither does the experiment prove that altering a survey automatically reprograms convictions. Its manipulated questionnaire and social setting helped expose a relationship between what people think they answered and what they will subsequently defend. The published results should not be turned into a claim about how an entire electorate behaves.

For Final Vestige, the deeper interest is epistemic: a statement of preference may tell us what someone currently endorses, while telling us less than we imagine about the sequence that produced that endorsement. A self-report is evidence. It is not infallible provenance.

## A more consequential setting: eyewitness decisions

A further set of experiments, published in *Applied Cognitive Psychology* in 2014, tested mismatches in facial recognition decisions after participants viewed mock-crime videos. Detection rates varied substantially with the decision type and timing; manipulation after a 48-hour delay produced more blindness than immediate presentation on some measures. [2014 article, DOI 10.1002/acp.3062](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.3062).

This is useful precisely because it is not identical to the portrait-attractiveness task. A witness choosing whom they recognize is making a different kind of judgment from a participant ranking appealing faces. The 2014 experiments explored those differences rather than treating one laboratory effect as universal.

The observation should sharpen, not sensationalize, questions about documentation: Was the exact original answer preserved? Did a witness see a stable, identifiable record of their earlier selection? Was a purported confirmation independent, or did it simply reproduce the choice that the witness was subsequently shown? These are legitimate reliability questions. They are not proof that an actual named witness or historical court record was manipulated.

## The historical archive as a stress test

Final Vestige's [Puységur Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) follows the eighteenth-century record of magnetic somnambulism through statements, predictions, failures, copied testimony, pressure, and operator claims. The archive distinguishes the 1785 institutional edition from an 1813 reproduction used for page-image checking. It retains the difficult cases—refusal, vulnerable subjects, operator interference, failed predictions—rather than editing them away.

It is tempting to collapse the distance between a modern experimental manipulation and a historical practice of suggestion. That would be an error. Neither the choice-blindness experiments nor the Puységur files establish that animal magnetism existed, that hypnotic influence operated in the same way, or that modern audio products produce the reported historical effects.

The connection is methodological. In both kinds of record, a powerful question is **what was written down before the purported outcome was known?** Did someone preserve the original choice or prediction, or only the later explanation? Who controlled the sequence in which evidence was presented? Could the observer object without social cost? Were failures kept with the successes?

A signed document can show what someone later said; it cannot automatically reconstruct an unrecorded step in how the statement emerged. A notarial document may strengthen the paper trail without confirming a proposed physical mechanism. The Puységur files make this distinction visible across named cases, and modern experimenters show a narrow but striking reason to care about it.

One of the archive's most uncomfortable episodes concerns an operator's capacity to steer compliant answers. In the historical text, the operator's late warnings about abusive pressure belong to the evidence, not merely to a dramatic retelling. That does not make Puységur a neuroscientist ahead of his era. It makes his archive an unusually rich place to ask whether authority changed what was said and saved.

## Why this matters when a product promises to change your mind

Some commercial meditation, self-hypnosis, and brainwave-audio pages promise dramatic changes in stress, cognition, habit, or even wealth. The fact that choice-blindness experiments reveal the malleability of a reported decision does **not** independently substantiate those products. The neuroscience of decision reports, clinical hypnosis trials for specific uses, and tests of a particular commercial recording are separate lines of evidence.

For example, the official [Zen12 product description](https://zen12.com/about) describes twelve-minute audio sessions, twelve levels and four versions of each level. It also makes sweeping benefit and time-equivalence claims. These are **seller statements**, not conclusions from the 2005 choice-blindness study. A 2023 systematic review of fourteen binaural-beat EEG studies reported five results in line with entrainment, eight contradictory results and one mixed result; heterogeneity limited direct comparison. The review did not test Zen12. [Ingendoh et al., *PLOS ONE*, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0286023); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37205669/).

A carefully designed guide can tell the reader what an audio package actually contains and what to verify before spending. It should not imply that choice blindness proves audio can alter a personality or that an engaging anecdote qualifies as a product-specific clinical trial.

## Try the documentary version—not a trick on a friend

Here is a twelve-minute, no-purchase exercise. It is an evidence audit, not a psychological test, diagnosis, or protocol for influencing another person.

**Minutes 0–3. Capture the original decision.** Pick two ordinary, low-stakes options—perhaps two short reading passages. Write which you prefer and why. Date the note; do not change it after the fact.

**Minutes 3–6. Separate outcome from explanation.** Write what evidence led to your choice and which elements are mere impressions. If you later change your mind, record the change rather than replacing the original statement. The point is to preserve provenance, not to catch yourself in a gotcha.

**Minutes 6–9. Trace one claim to its first document.** Visit a Final Vestige investigation or the [Puységur source trail](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/). Find a claim, its page or source locator, and one limitation. Can you distinguish an eyewitness's assertion from the editor's explanation? If not, annotate the missing step.

**Minutes 9–12. Write the missing question.** Draft one sentence: “To establish this conclusion, we would also need ____.” It might be the original questionnaire, a timestamped answer, a control condition, the first manuscript, or the record of failures. That one blank is often the difference between a persuasive story and an accountable investigation.

The exercise does not measure intelligence or immunity to influence. Even people familiar with an effect may be fallible in other settings. Its useful outcome is a repeatable habit: preserve the initial record, separate observation from interpretation, and make the missing evidence explicit.

## What survives the swap

Choice blindness is not a license to distrust every memory. It is a reason to take the sequence of evidence seriously. A convincing explanation may be sincere while failing to describe the original decision precisely. A confidence statement may coexist with an overlooked discrepancy. And a research result that sounds astonishing may be bounded by conditions the viral retelling leaves out.

The photograph in the participant's hand became a fact about what they were asked to explain. It was not necessarily the fact about what they had chosen.

That distinction is why Final Vestige studies both the mind and the paperwork.
