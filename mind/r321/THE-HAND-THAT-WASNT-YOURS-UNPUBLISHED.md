# The Hand That Wasn't Yours—and Felt Like It Was
*Final Vestige Studios | Mind & Perception Archive | Original investigation | UNPUBLISHED editorial review | 12 November 2026*

**The most disturbing part of the rubber-hand illusion is not that a fake hand can seem real. It is that two honest measurements of the same experiment can disagree about what happened.**

Place a life-size artificial hand on a table. Hide a volunteer's actual hand behind a screen. Stroke both hands with brushes, making the touch the volunteer feels coincide with the stroke the volunteer sees. In some experimental conditions, the subject begins to describe the artificial hand as if it belongs to them. It is a beautifully compact experiment: a partition, an object, two brushes, a willing participant, and one question about the apparent boundary of the self.

But there is a twist. Researchers can also ask volunteers to point to the location of their unseen hand. The estimated location sometimes shifts toward the rubber one. That shift is called *proprioceptive drift*. The tempting explanation is that if someone's pointing moved, their feeling of ownership must have moved too. A later experiment found a serious problem with that inference. Drift and ownership do not reliably travel together.

The distinction is more important than the illusion's theatrical appeal. It is the difference between a measurement and the story told about that measurement.

## A small room for an unsettled question

In February 1998, Matthew Botvinick and Jonathan Cohen published a short scientific correspondence in *Nature* titled "Rubber hands 'feel' touch that eyes see." Their claim was specific: under particular conditions, tactile sensations could be experienced as referred to an artificial limb, suggesting an interaction among vision, touch and proprioception. The original report did not demonstrate that an observer had permanently revised their body, unlocked some hidden faculty, or become susceptible to another person's commands. It offered an experimental opening into bodily self-identification.

Proprioception is information about where the body is positioned, including when you cannot see it. It ordinarily works alongside vision and touch without a great deal of conscious negotiation. Most people do not spend the day comparing what their eyes show with what their muscles and joints report. When evidence is consistent, the result feels simply like a body.

The rubber-hand procedure asks what happens when those signals are made slightly inconsistent. The real hand is hidden; an anatomically plausible replacement is visible. The participant knows the rubber hand is a prop. Yet its observed touches are synchronized with felt touches on the real hand. The sight of contact and the sensation of contact refer to different objects but share a rhythm.

This is an experiment about **apparent ownership**, not about a hand becoming physically different. The participant's real hand has not changed location. The visible rubber hand is still rubber. An experience can be genuine without the story that the experience seems to tell being literally true.

Primary source: Botvinick & Cohen, *Nature* 391, 756 (19 February 1998), https://doi.org/10.1038/35784. The publisher's public record provides the title, abstract and bibliographic context; its full text was not treated as freely readable in this review.

## The experiment the internet likes to show

The best-known version uses two brushes. An experimenter brushes the visible fake hand and the hidden real hand together. A control condition may use asynchronous strokes. This contrast is informative: timing matters to how touch and sight are combined.

What appears in a short social clip, though, is often a single participant startled by the procedure. The scene can become a demonstration of an allegedly universal effect, with the individual reaction substituted for an average. Published results are more complicated. The size and quality of the experience vary among people. Position, hand orientation, spatial separation, brushing pattern, prior knowledge, instructions and expectation can matter.

A vivid reaction is a useful invitation to ask a scientific question; it is not an estimate of how often the question's answer applies to the population. There is no defensible "everyone experiences this" statistic in the evidence reviewed here.

A second mistake comes from collapsing three observations into one: the person **said** the artificial hand felt like theirs; the person **estimated** the hidden hand's location nearer the fake one; and the person **behaved** differently when the fake hand was threatened. These measures need not support exactly the same interpretation. The fact that all seem relevant to embodiment does not make them interchangeable.

## The location moved. Did ownership?

Marieke Rohde, Massimiliano Di Luca and Marc Ernst addressed this problem in a 2011 paper with a blunt title: "The Rubber Hand Illusion: Feeling of Ownership and Proprioceptive Drift Do Not Go Hand in Hand." Their study varied how frequently perceived hand position was measured—every 10, 40 or 120 seconds—and compared synchronous stroking, asynchronous stroking and a vision-only condition.

With frequent position measurements, drift was observed not just during synchronous stimulation but also during control conditions. In their reported results, synchronous-condition drift was not greater than drift while looking at the rubber hand without brushing. Subjective ownership reports, by contrast, depended on synchronous stroking and were not present under the asynchronous condition. The authors concluded that location recalibration and subjective ownership should not be inferred from one another.

This does not "debunk" the sensory phenomenon. It changes what the experiment is allowed to establish. Watching a fake hand can influence where a person judges a hidden hand to be, even without inducing the same feeling of ownership. The more satisfying popular story—"they measured where you thought your hand was, so they proved the fake hand became yours"—is not the result the paper supports.

Reference: Rohde, Di Luca & Ernst, *PLoS ONE* 6(6), e21659 (2011), https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0021659; indexed abstract at https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21738756/. This is a specific dissociation under tested conditions, not proof that ownership reports never track spatial judgment.

## A questionnaire isn't a brain scan—and a brain scan isn't an experience

Researchers often ask volunteers to rate statements about the experiment. That sounds subjective because it is. The scientist wants information about a subjective phenomenon, and a volunteer is often the only direct reporter of it. The challenge is constructing questions that distinguish an authentic change of experience from expectation, interpretation or willingness to cooperate.

In 2021, Daniele Romano, Angelo Maravita and Marco Perugini reported a study of **298 participants** who experienced both synchronous and asynchronous visuotactile stimulation. The researchers examined a 27-item questionnaire and proposed a shorter, 18-item embodiment scale with three components: experiencing the rubber hand as embodied, feeling disembodied from one's biological hand, and physical sensations during the task. Their study helps make the problem more precise: even reports loosely called "the illusion" may describe different experiential facets.

The study also explored correlations with individual characteristics including empathy, self-esteem and mindfulness. Such associations do not demonstrate that meditation causes rubber-hand ownership, or that a particular purchasable audio recording changes bodily self-representation. An observed questionnaire relationship cannot carry that commercial leap.

Reference: Romano, Maravita & Perugini, *Scientific Reports* 11, 5029 (2021), https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-021-84595-x.

## The disagreement about suggestion

There is a deeper argument, and it leads directly into the history of hypnotism.

In 2020, Peter Lush published "Demand Characteristics Confound the Rubber Hand Illusion." Instead of applying touches in the conventional way, the study examined what observers expected volunteers to report after reading about or watching the procedure. Those expectations differed across the very statements and synchronous/asynchronous conditions researchers often use to distinguish illusion from control. Lush argued that established questionnaire controls could be confounded by demand characteristics—cues that tell a participant which experience is expected.

This challenges the confidence of certain mechanistic interpretations. It does not show that participants are faking reports, nor establish that all rubber-hand experiences are caused solely by suggestion. A person can genuinely experience something that has been influenced by expectations. Equally, a predictable expectation does not by itself demonstrate that sensory integration contributes nothing.

A 2024 replication and extension tested expectancy effects and order effects, illustrating that the dispute concerns the design of measurements, not a simple battle between "illusion is real" and "illusion is fake."

References: Lush, *Collabra: Psychology* 6(1), 22 (2020), https://doi.org/10.1525/collabra.325; "Order Effects on the Rubber Hand Illusion Expectancy: A Replication and Extension of Lush (2020)," *Collabra: Psychology* 10(1) (2024), https://online.ucpress.edu/collabra/article/10/1/116190/200587/Order-Effects-on-the-Rubber-Hand-Illusion.

## An eighteenth-century echo, not an eighteenth-century explanation

Final Vestige's [Puységur's Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) examine the historical claims, testimony and interpretive systems surrounding magnetic somnambulism. The archival question is not whether nineteenth- or eighteenth-century observers secretly possessed a modern theory of sensory integration. They did not.

The parallel is methodological. The observer sees a surprising response. The participant reports an unusual experience. An interpreter decides what mechanism produced the response. In the animal-magnetism record, claims about a special magnetic agency extended well beyond what the records could reliably establish. In rubber-hand work, researchers at least design controls, isolate sensory timing and invite competing explanations. The history can teach why the controls matter without flattening two very different investigations into one purported proof of "mind control."

One of the most useful things in that archive is its refusal to convert testimony into validated mechanism by rhetorical force. The same restraint belongs in present-day research. What the participant says is evidence. What the experimental condition does is evidence. The mechanism may be an inference, and the inference should remain open to challenge.

## A safe, low-tech observation with no theatrical promise

You do not need an artificial limb to see why sensory cues sometimes compete. Put one hand comfortably below the edge of a table, out of sight. Without looking, describe in a sentence where you believe your index finger rests in relation to the table edge. Now look at the hand and note how closely your description matched. Repeat once, with no coaching or score.

This is **not** a rubber-hand illusion protocol, diagnostic test or scientifically validated measure of embodiment. It is simply a demonstration of the difference between your internal estimate and what the eyes later verify. Some people may find little difference; that's an equally legitimate observation. If the task causes discomfort, skip it.

For an ethically designed classroom study of the actual illusion, researchers need consent, clear debriefing, appropriate control conditions and no alarming surprises or implied diagnosis. A dramatic threat to a fake hand is not necessary for a public educational exercise.

## The experiment has a better ending than the magic trick

The rubber-hand illusion begins by destabilizing a certainty: surely I know which hand is mine. But its lasting value is not a claim that the mind can be programmed at will. It is a set of carefully separable questions.

Can synchronized cues change a person's reported feeling? Under which controls? Can perceived position shift independently? What does a questionnaire actually capture? Could prior expectation change the report or the experience itself? Each question narrows an explanation instead of inflating it.

That is what serious perception research has in common with serious historical investigation. The surprising scene is the beginning. The evidence is what survives after the surprise.
