# The Gorilla in the Scan: Why Looking Is Not the Same as Seeing
*Final Vestige Studios · Mind & Perception · Original investigation · Unpublished review manuscript · November 11, 2026*

**The finding:** In a 2013 controlled laboratory experiment, 20 of 24 expert radiologists did not report an unexpected gorilla image embedded in CT scans they were reading for lung nodules. The result concerns a specific attention task, not the general competence of radiologists, and it does not validate any commercial focus recording. [1]

## A figure large enough to notice

Picture a darkened reading room: a series of gray cross-sections of a human chest, a cursor, and an assignment that demands experienced eyes. The specialist is not asked to read a mystery. The instruction is to find lung nodules, small features that matter precisely because they can be easy to overlook. The reader can scroll through slices of a CT study, moving forward and backward through the reconstructed body.

Then, on the final case, a silhouette enters the image. It is not something a radiologist would expect to find in a patient's lung. It is a gorilla, composited by the experimenters. Its rectangular bounding volume was more than 48 times the mean volume of the nodules in the selected images. It appeared gradually across five 2-millimeter slices rather than abruptly flashing on the screen. A white arrow was not pointing at it. Nobody was told a gorilla was possible. [1]

That design matters. A dramatic picture can be so conspicuous to someone who already knows what to look for that the central finding seems unbelievable. But these observers were concentrating on something else, and they had been instructed to detect it well. What the experiment asks is not simply whether the image landed on their retinas. It asks whether a particular unexpected shape became part of what they consciously noticed and later reported.

In the paper, *The Invisible Gorilla Strikes Again*, Trafton Drew, Melissa Võ, and Jeremy Wolfe described a counterintuitive result: of 24 radiologists performing the nodule search, **20 failed to report the unexpected figure**. That is 83 percent of this sample, in this task, not a universal risk percentage for actual diagnostic care. [1]

The seductive version of the story would end there, with a theatrical accusation that professionals cannot be trusted to see what is in front of them. The actual science is more interesting, and more restrained.

## What the researchers actually asked

The experiment was published online on July 17, 2013, in *Psychological Science*. The researchers recruited 24 radiologists, whose age range was 28–70, and presented five lung CT scans. Observers could spend up to three minutes on each, freely scrolling and clicking nodules. The gorilla appeared only in the final case. Each case contained roughly ten nodules on average. Eye movements were tracked. [1]

The nodules were the primary task. Finding an implausible animal was not. The researchers carefully distinguished reports of anomalies from seeing the gorilla: first they asked whether the last trial seemed different, then whether anything unusual was noticed, and finally asked directly about a gorilla. Most participants did not report one. Yet when they were later shown the image and directed toward the issue, all 24 recognized it. In other words, the shape was perceptually available when attention was guided to it. [1]

Eye tracking made the result stranger. The observers who missed the gorilla spent an average of 5.8 seconds viewing the five slices where it appeared. Of the 20 radiologists who did not report it, 12 looked directly at its location while it was visible, with a mean dwell time at the location of 547 milliseconds. Fixation of a region is not identical to consciously identifying every object in that region. The eyes and the report can tell different stories. [1]

The same paper did not equate missing the gorilla with failing at radiology. During the nodule task, radiologists detected 55 percent of the target nodules on average. In a comparison experiment, observers without medical training detected 12 percent after a brief orientation to identifying nodules. That difference should be mentioned whenever the much more shareable gorilla statistic is repeated. The specialists were markedly better at the task they had actually been assigned. [1]

A second experiment involved 25 naïve participants; the results section reports that 24 of these did not report the unexpected image. That nuance should be preserved instead of claiming a perfect miss rate. The task and expertise differed, so it would also be a mistake to treat the groups as interchangeable population estimates. [1]

## Why the gorilla was not simply invisible

A skeptical reader might propose an easier explanation: perhaps the image was hard to see, badly placed or too fleeting. The authors tested visibility in a third experiment. Twelve people were explicitly told to detect a possible gorilla in a CT sequence. Its approximate location was cued, and they made presence-or-absence judgments while the slices moved quickly. Accuracy was near ceiling—reported as 88 percent—even with displays shorter than the time the original observers had spent on gorilla-bearing slices. [1]

This was a different task. It did not prove that every original observer could or should have detected the shape. It did show that the unexpected figure was not inherently uninterpretable. Tell someone what to look for, and a previously missed stimulus can become easy to identify.

Now the crucial reversal: **the larger object was not necessarily a better match for what those experts were searching for**. The nodules were small and bright. The gorilla was large and dark. Its visual difference from the assigned targets may have helped it remain outside the search template. The authors even raised the possibility that a smaller or lighter-colored gorilla might have captured more attention, although that specific counterfactual was not established by their experiment. [1]

A reader's expectation of what counts as relevant can govern which features are elevated for inspection. That is not mystical brain blindness. It is a finding about the selective organization of attention.

## A gorilla had already crossed a basketball court

The 2013 study deliberately echoed a well-known 1999 experiment by Daniel Simons and Christopher Chabris. Observers watched dynamic scenes of people passing basketballs and carried out a counting task. An unexpected person in a gorilla suit crossed the action. Recognition of the unexpected event varied with the task and characteristics of the display. Their 1999 paper argued that noticing an event depends in part on similarity to attended events and on the demands of the primary monitoring task. [2]

The laboratory legacy reaches beyond one costume. In 2001 Steven Most and colleagues systematically studied similarity, selective ignoring and how an observer's attentional set shapes recognition of unexpected objects. In one condition, nearly 30 percent missed a conspicuous red cross moving across a display for five seconds. **Being distinctive is not an unconditional passport into awareness.** The visible evidence in the accessible 2001 journal abstract supports that limited conclusion; this review did not rely on inaccessible full-text methods. [3]

The radiologist study asked a sharper question: what happens when the observer is not an inexperienced student but someone expert at the assigned search? The answer was not that expertise is worthless. It was that expertise and attentional limits can coexist.

This connects to Final Vestige's [Change Blindness investigation](https://finalvestige.com/2026/11/02/change-blindness/), which examines noticing changes across interrupted views. Those are related but different problems. Change blindness concerns detecting a difference between states, often when the visual disruption masks the change. Inattentional blindness concerns failing to notice an otherwise visible, unexpected event while attention is occupied. We should not call them the same experiment merely because both embarrass our confidence. [2][4]

## The tempting medical headline is the wrong headline

One misleading headline would be: “Doctors miss obvious problems 83 percent of the time.” That is *not* what the experiment found. The gorilla was a digitally inserted, medically irrelevant object. The sample was small. The trial structure was artificial. Scans were selected for a research task rather than assessed as part of ordinary clinical responsibility, with clinical workflow, second opinions and reporting systems. There was no patient-outcome trial establishing how often radiologists overlook real lesions. [1]

Another misleading reading would blame the participants for failing a trick question nobody had told them would be on the test. The authors themselves explicitly cautioned against taking the result as an indictment of radiologists. They framed it as a reason to understand limitations of searching and to design more reliable systems. [1]

One hypothesis considered was “satisfaction of search”: after finding one target, an observer may stop searching carefully enough to find another. The gorilla was placed near a nodule. But the observed pattern did not provide simple confirmation of that mechanism: all seven radiologists who missed the nearby nodule also missed the gorilla; the four who noticed the gorilla also detected the nodule on the same slice. The authors said that a further experiment, including gorilla detection without that nodule, would be needed to isolate the role of this possibility. [1]

This is the sort of explanatory restraint an evidence-led investigation should preserve. The dramatic experiment does not entitle us to a dramatic causal story beyond the data.

## A quick exercise in choosing what counts

Try a quiet observational exercise without using any footage or photographs from the published studies.

Write down three objects you expect to see at the next ordinary place you visit: a desk, kitchen counter, bus stop or garden. When you arrive, identify those three objects. Then deliberately make a second pass for things you had not predicted: an unusual color, a label turned backward, an object outside its usual position.

Record two separate notes: what you noticed while pursuing the list, and what you noticed only after changing the instruction. If the second pass reveals something new, you have learned something about your search strategy. **You have not replicated the 1999 or 2013 experiments, diagnosed an attention problem or measured your cognitive abilities.** Nothing was hidden, the conditions were uncontrolled and the second look was not blinded.

The point is a modest and useful question: *What was I searching for, and what was outside that description?* A source trail should invite that question, whether the subject is a photographed artifact, an archived letter or a commercial claim.

There is also a contemporary temptation to leap from a finding about attentional limits to a shopping recommendation. A person may reasonably want to become less distractible. An audio manufacturer may promise concentration, relaxation or mental clarity. But the missing link is critical: the Drew experiment tested visual search under controlled conditions. It did **not** test whether guided meditation, hypnosis or isochronic tracks reverse inattentional blindness. [1]

## What remains after the gorilla exits

There are two facts worth holding together. Expert radiologists in this study were substantially better than novices at the task they practiced; most also missed an unrelated, unexpected figure in the same images. The surprising result does not erase the first finding. The first finding does not make the second one vanish.

The lesson is not that our eyes always lie. It is that **attention is a selection process, and evidence is often wider than the question we initially asked**. A good investigation changes the question when the surviving record demands it.

And sometimes the most useful part of an experiment is not a gorilla at all. It is the uncomfortable question left on the page: *What did the observer have reason to look for?*
