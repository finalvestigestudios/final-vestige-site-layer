# The Gorilla in the Scan: When Looking Directly Is Not the Same as Seeing

**Final Vestige Studios | Mind & Perception | Original investigation | DRAFT — not published | Research checked 18 November 2026**

**Standfirst:** A gorilla walks across a basketball game. Years later, a gorilla appears inside a medical scan. Both cases expose the same uncomfortable distinction: a person can direct their eyes toward a location without noticing the unexpected thing there. But a later experiment complicates the popular story. Attention is not simply an on–off switch; the speed of an intruder matters.

*Editorial boundary: This investigation is educational, not an attention test, a medical assessment, or evidence that a commercial meditation or hypnosis product works. No commercial recommendation appears in the research conclusions.*

## The interruption no one ordered

Imagine being asked to review a stack of images for a single feature. Your task is specific. There is a reason for the search, a target to recognize, and a moment when you will need to say whether you found it. You narrow your attention accordingly. Then someone reveals that an enormous object was inserted into one of the images. You did not report it.

At first that sounds like a joke with a cruel punchline. Perhaps you were careless. Perhaps the object was too faint. Perhaps an expert would surely have seen it.

That last assumption was exactly what researchers tested in an experiment published in 2013. Twenty-four radiologists inspected computed-tomography scans for lung nodules. A gorilla shape was inserted into the final case. The researchers reported that **83% did not report the gorilla**, despite its being far larger than the average nodule in the task. Eye tracking showed that many of the radiologists who missed it had looked at the relevant location. [Drew, Võ & Wolfe, 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797613479386).

Do not mistake the provocative statistic for a verdict on doctors, scan interpretation, or patients. The demonstration used a constructed experimental event in an image-review task. It was not an assessment of individual medical competence; no inference about real clinical error rates follows from it. The scientifically useful point is narrower: sophisticated visual expertise did not guarantee reporting an unexpected object outside the assigned search category.

That difference between **the target of a search** and **the rest of the scene** is the thread we will follow.

## The basketball game

In a 1999 paper titled *Gorillas in Our Midst*, Daniel Simons and Christopher Chabris studied sustained inattentional blindness using dynamic events. Participants monitored one kind of activity in a busy visual scene while unexpected objects and events occurred. Whether an unexpected item was reported depended in part on how similar it was to attended objects and how demanding the assigned monitoring task was. [Simons & Chabris, 1999](https://doi.org/10.1068/p281059).

The better-known classroom version asks viewers to count passes between players. A person in a gorilla suit enters the scene. Some people notice the intruder. Others finish the count without reporting it. The unsettling part is not that human vision has a narrow field. The object occurs within the broad scene they are watching. Their purpose, however, is to track a particular category of activity.

Popular accounts compress the result into a claim that people are blind to obvious things. That is too coarse. The original researchers manipulated the demands of attention and features of the unexpected object. Their findings concerned the *conditions under which an unexpected event was reported*, not a timeless, universal inability to detect gorillas.

This is the first source-reading lesson: preserve the task conditions. If an experiment changes what participants have been instructed to count, it changes the attentional question. A short social clip that leaves out the instructions has removed part of the experiment.

## Looked at, but not reported

The radiologist study adds another distinction. A record of eye position is not a window into conscious experience. Fixating on an area makes it possible for information there to enter visual processing; it does not establish what was understood, recognized, or later considered worth mentioning.

The 2013 paper reported that the majority of radiologists who missed the inserted gorilla looked directly at its location during the scan task. That is an important observation. But it is not proof that their brains secretly recognized a gorilla, nor proof that every observer had a complete visual representation and suppressed it. The defensible interpretation is that fixation and subsequent report diverged under those conditions. [Drew et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797613479386).

There are at least four different propositions often blurred into one:

1. Light from the object reached the retina.
2. The eyes were oriented toward its region.
3. The person consciously registered the object as an unusual feature.
4. The person subsequently reported the feature to the experimenter.

The paper gives evidence about the assigned task, eye positions, and reports. It cannot settle every step of perception for every person. The phrase 'they looked right at it' is memorable; the method behind that phrase is what makes it useful.

## Knowledge does not solve every surprise

By 2010 the original gorilla demonstration was sufficiently famous that it became possible to ask a revealing follow-up: if people know about the trick, are they better at spotting other unexpected events?

Simons designed a new video with several surprises. In addition to an expected gorilla, the curtain changed color and a person left the scene. Those familiar with this genre of demonstration regularly spotted the gorilla, but they were slightly *less* likely to report the other events. The conclusion was not that education damages attention. It was that anticipation of one familiar surprise did not automatically confer the ability to monitor every other surprise. [Simons, 2010](https://doi.org/10.1068/i0386).

The result has an editorial consequence. A caption that asks 'Can you see the gorilla?' tells viewers what to seek. It is useful as a challenge, but no longer recreates the original surprise. If we make a Final Vestige demonstration, we must say what it is: a newly designed observation exercise, not an authentic replication of the 1999 protocol.

It also has a historical consequence. Once an extraordinary phenomenon becomes famous, an audience may go into every demonstration expecting that very phenomenon. Expectation is then part of the observation, not an invisible neutral background.

## The counterevidence: a gorilla moving quickly

The familiar story becomes more interesting when a later result pushes against its most sweeping interpretation.

In 2023, Pascal Wallisch, Wayne Mackey, Michael Karlovich and David Heeger reported three experiments, with **4,493 participants in total**, designed to vary the speed of unexpected objects. Their central finding was that unexpectedly fast-moving objects were more readily noticed than slow-moving ones in their tasks, even without deliberate attention to those objects. Fast motion acted as a powerful cue. [Wallisch et al., *PNAS*, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2214930120).

This does not erase the 1999 basketball work or the 2013 radiologist result. It limits any easy slogan that *anything* unexpected can be overlooked no matter how visually striking it is. Inattentional blindness is conditional. Physical features, task demands, the timing of an event and its similarity to attended objects can all matter.

For a skeptical documentary, this is a better ending than 'your brain lies to you.' The evidence grows sharper when a later investigation identifies conditions under which detection changes.

It is also a reason to resist turning a single demonstration into a personality diagnosis. Missing one intruder does not certify that you have poor attention, low intelligence or a treatable disorder. Noticing one does not make you immune to future errors.

## When is an observation an explanation?

Final Vestige's historical [Puységur Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) examine a very different kind of claim. In the 1780s, Puységur assembled accounts of animal magnetism and magnetic somnambulism, including testimony, predictions, failures and the procedures used to produce extraordinary states. The archive carefully separates what historical observers recorded from what their proposed theory claimed those records meant.

We are not claiming that inattentional blindness explains Puységur's cases. Different centuries, procedures and claims make that shortcut indefensible. The connection is methodological: an observer can report a sincere experience without having established its cause.

That rule should apply symmetrically. If someone failed to report a gorilla, the task and measurement must be examined before telling a sweeping story about consciousness. If someone in an eighteenth-century record appeared to speak in an unusual state, the record does not by itself demonstrate a magnetic fluid or a cure. And if a seller says audio frequencies produce a particular psychological state, the existence of auditory perception research does not establish the seller's product-specific outcome.

A historical claim, a contemporary experiment and a commercial promise require different kinds of proof.

## A free twelve-minute observation audit

Here is a useful exercise that does **not** pretend to reproduce the original gorilla study.

**Minutes 0–2 — Write the assignment.** Choose a short, publicly accessible video you have permission to view. Before watching, write down one narrow feature to count—for example, the number of camera cuts. Do not record bystanders or use private content.

**Minutes 2–4 — Do the count.** Watch once, track your assigned feature, and record your answer. Do not pause or rewind during this first pass. There is no 'right' score for attention.

**Minutes 4–6 — Change the question.** Rewatch the exact same video, this time documenting what else occurred: movement in the background, changes of scene, sounds, written text or objects crossing the frame. Note what you truly noticed on the first pass rather than retroactively claiming you saw it.

**Minutes 6–8 — Mark uncertainty.** Divide your notes into 'reported on first viewing,' 'noticed only on second viewing,' and 'still uncertain.' Avoid assuming all newly reported details were genuinely absent from your first experience.

**Minutes 8–10 — Inspect a source.** Read the abstracts of [Simons & Chabris (1999)](https://doi.org/10.1068/p281059) and [Wallisch et al. (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2214930120). Write one feature of the laboratory experiments that your home exercise did not control.

**Minutes 10–12 — Explain without overclaiming.** Complete this sentence: 'The exercise showed me ______, but it did not establish ______.' That second clause is the discipline. Rewatching a natural video is not a validated measurement of inattentional blindness.

This method can be repeated with different kinds of media, but repeated viewing is not the same as an independent blinded experiment. The purpose is to improve the quality of your notes and questions, not to promise changes in concentration.

## Where the evidence ends

The experiments support a claim about visual attention under particular conditions. They do not show that commercial meditation audios, self-hypnosis MP3s, 'brainwave' programs or focus products prevent inattentional blindness. They do not establish a treatment for memory or clinical attentional difficulties. Research on selective attention can inform a useful reflection practice without validating a specific seller's promises.

Readers interested in a free, structured practice can use the existing [Seven Day Focus Fieldbook](https://finalvestige.com/focus/). It is a no-credit-card resource with an existing opt-in; this investigation does not add a new mailing list. Access to the source papers and the free exercise above does not require subscribing.

The larger question remains. How much of our confidence in what we saw is really confidence in what we were *looking for*? The answer is not that our senses are useless. It is that the source trail, the task and the counterexample all deserve a place beside the story.

- **Original dynamic experiment:** Simons, D. J. & Chabris, C. F. (1999). 'Gorillas in our midst: sustained inattentional blindness for dynamic events.' *Perception* 28(9), 1059–1074. [DOI 10.1068/p281059](https://doi.org/10.1068/p281059). Primary experimental paper; observations are conditional on stimuli and task. The original videos are rights-protected; no footage is reproduced here.
- **Familiarity follow-up:** Simons, D. J. (2010). 'Monkeying around with the gorillas in our midst.' *i-Perception* 1(1), 3–6. [DOI 10.1068/i0386](https://doi.org/10.1068/i0386). Studies the consequences of knowing this genre of demonstration.
- **Expert observers:** Drew, T., Võ, M. L.-H. & Wolfe, J. M. (2013). 'The invisible gorilla strikes again: sustained inattentional blindness in expert observers.' *Psychological Science* 24(9), 1848–1853. [DOI 10.1177/0956797613479386](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797613479386). Small sample of 24 radiologists; no clinical-performance inference.
- **A boundary condition:** Wallisch, P., Mackey, W. E., Karlovich, M. W. & Heeger, D. J. (2023). 'The visible gorilla: Unexpected fast—not physically salient—objects are noticeable.' *PNAS* 120(22), e2214930120. [DOI 10.1073/pnas.2214930120](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2214930120). Three studies, aggregate n=4,493, with motion-speed manipulations; not a general immunity to attentional blindness.
