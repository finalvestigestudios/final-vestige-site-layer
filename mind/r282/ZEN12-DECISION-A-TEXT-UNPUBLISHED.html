The second target nobody could report

In 1992, researchers presented letters rapidly and asked observers to identify one item, then detect another. When a second target appeared shortly after the first, reports dropped under particular conditions. The missing answer was a measurable feature of a controlled task—not proof of a person's general concentration or a cure for it. Read the 1992 study.

Fifteen years later, researchers reported that three months of intensive meditation training changed participants' performance on a related attention task. That is worth studying. It is not an experiment on the Zen12 Premium audio library and cannot establish that one paid twelve-minute recording reproduces months of training. Read the 2007 study.

Evidence boundary: Evidence about a laboratory attentional blink or months of training is not product-specific evidence that Zen12 delivers an hour of advanced meditation in twelve minutes.

A useful demonstration before you spend

Set a standard timer for twelve minutes. Choose an ordinary, safe seated pause, not a driving task. Notice how often attention wanders, without scoring yourself or trying to force a trance. When time ends, jot down three observations: what helped you begin, what distracted you, and whether you would prefer spoken guidance, natural sound, or silence next time.

Do not interpret one session as a clinical outcome, cognitive improvement, or a replication of an attentional-blink study. This is a preference check. If a free timer meets your immediate goal, there is no evidence-based reason to hurry into a purchase.
