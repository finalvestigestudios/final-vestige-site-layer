Tools under review · informed buying, not an endorsement

Will a 12-minute recording fit the way you actually practice?

Meditation advice can sound like a demand to become a different person. A structured audio routine promises a simpler starting point. Before you pay for that structure, know exactly what is being sold—and what science has not established.

The honest problem: sticking with a routine

It is easy to confuse a product's convenience with proof of its effectiveness. A dozen guided sessions might help you decide to sit down and pay attention more regularly. But buying a sequence of files does not demonstrate a measurable improvement in memory, concentration or health.

Consider the laboratory listening problem that helped establish modern attention research: in 1959, Neville Moray found that an ignored message was generally hard to report, yet a person's own name could sometimes draw attention. In a 1995 follow-up, 34.6% of a sample of 34 students recalled their names in the ignored channel; in a 2021 preregistered replication, 29% reported doing so. These were studies of competing speech, not tests of any meditation recording. See the 1995 and 2021 publisher-indexed abstracts.

The lesson is not that your attention can be magically upgraded. It is that attention is selective and can be interrupted, sometimes by something meaningful. A useful practice starts by observing the moments when your mind leaves the task.

Try twelve minutes without buying anything

Set a quiet, ordinary timer for twelve minutes. Sit comfortably, noticing the physical act of breathing, or listen to an everyday environmental sound. When thoughts intrude, name the kind of interruption in one or two words—planning, memory, worry, a notification—and return to your chosen anchor. At the end, write down what happened, without assigning yourself a score.
