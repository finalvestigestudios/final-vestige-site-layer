# The Photograph You Didn't Choose

*How a simple switch in a 2005 experiment exposed the frailty of our explanations—and why that matters when someone tries to sell us a changed mind.*

**Final Vestige Studios · Mind & Perception · Unpublished manuscript · Source review: November 13, 2026**

A person looks at two photographs and points to the more attractive face. The decision is ordinary and private: one face seems more appealing than the other. A few seconds later, the researcher hands over the photograph the person supposedly selected and asks why.

There is one small problem. It is not the chosen photograph.

In a 2005 experiment published in *Science*, Petter Johansson, Lars Hall, Sverker Sikström, and Andreas Olsson arranged for participants to receive, on some trials, the face they had not selected. The manipulation was performed during the handoff. Some participants noticed the switch; many did not. Among those who failed to detect it, people nevertheless gave reasons for the face presented to them. The finding was startling not because any participant had lost their ability to think, but because the apparent evidence of a choice and the remembered intention to make that choice could come apart. [1]

The researchers called the effect **choice blindness**.

The phrase is easy to sensationalize. The more interesting story begins when we resist doing that. It is about a measurable weakness in the link between intention, outcome and explanation—not proof that our decisions are fake, that people can be made to believe anything, or that a modern product can safely rewrite anyone's mind.

## The scene—and the missing step

In the original study, participants made judgments about photographs of faces. On manipulated trials, the experimenter covertly substituted the unchosen face for the one the participant had indicated, then invited the participant to explain the apparent selection. This was not a standard memory quiz in which someone had to recall a photograph from last month. The discrepancy was introduced within the same short encounter in which the choice occurred. [1, 2]

The important separation is between **what the person selected**, **what the person was shown afterward**, **whether the discrepancy was detected**, and **what they said when asked to explain**. Flatten all four into the headline “people don't know what they want,” and the experiment stops being a useful experiment. You have replaced a narrow observation with a general theory of human nature.

Why would someone explain the unchosen face? One possibility is that people construct explanations from the information currently available. Asked to give a reason for a visible outcome, we often find a plausible one. That does not prove those reasons are dishonest; it suggests the reason-giving process can be separated from an accurately remembered decision. The published result concerns a specific laboratory setup with particular stimuli and opportunities to detect the trick. [1]

The difference matters in everyday reading, too. Imagine encountering a claim that a twelve-minute recording provides the benefit of a far longer meditation session. The promise sounds neat. A source citation or scientific-sounding vocabulary makes it sound neater. If you later decide to buy, the story you tell yourself about your purchase might be perfectly sincere while leaving out the original advertising hook, the ease of the purchase, or a desire for relief.

That last paragraph is an **analogy**, not a result established by Johansson's experiment. No one in the 2005 face study was choosing Zen12 or evaluating meditation recordings. Choice blindness is a warning to document what we actually chose and why, not evidence about which recording works.

## A second experiment moved the switch from faces to moral judgments

Choice blindness was not left confined to pairs of photographs. In a 2012 *PLOS ONE* paper, Lars Hall, Petter Johansson and Thomas Strandberg used a self-transforming survey to explore what happened when expressed positions on moral questions were covertly altered. Under those experimental conditions, some people reasoned about changed statements as though the statements reflected their own original views. [3]

This is an especially delicate kind of result to report. Participants were not consenting in the ordinary way to have their attitudes manipulated in real life; they were enrolled in a controlled research context with its own procedures and ethics. It would be wrong to turn the experiment into instructions for deceiving a customer, a patient, or a friend. The study cannot establish that everyone will accept such changes, that altered explanations become permanent convictions, or that manipulation is harmless.

A 2013 study by Hall and colleagues pushed the method into the context of political attitudes. Researchers covertly altered responses to a survey in Sweden and examined whether participants detected the mismatches and how their subsequent answers changed. The study offers another demonstration that the wording of an apparently personal position can affect immediate reasoning. It does **not** show that all political views are arbitrary, or establish lasting shifts outside that research setting. [4]

The common mechanism across these studies is not a supernatural force. It is a method for exposing a mismatch between recorded intention and displayed outcome. An authentic interpretation keeps track of experimental context, the detection opportunities, sample, and what was actually measured.

## The record is stronger than the explanation

Final Vestige spends much of its time reconstructing events from surviving evidence: a dispatch sent before a ship disappeared; a maintenance log written before a failure; a note made before someone knew how a story would end. Choice blindness gives that discipline an unsettling psychological twin.

If the participant points to the face on the left but later explains the face on the right, a record of the original choice matters. Without it, the later explanation can be eloquent and still refer to the wrong decision. The lesson isn't that eyewitnesses are worthless. It's that testimony is most useful when we can compare it with independent evidence and with the circumstances in which it was collected.

You can find an older version of this problem in Final Vestige's [Puységur Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/). Puységur assembled patient statements, predictions, witnesses and accounts of altered states into an apparent system of evidence. Yet the surviving files also preserve refusal, pressure and contradiction. In one case, the person asks to be left alone while the operator continues; the archive treats that as a question of authority and consent rather than a proof of unusual magnetic power.

There is an important difference between the historical archive and the modern choice-blindness experiments. Eighteenth-century magnetizers had not discovered choice blindness. The physical claims of animal magnetism were not validated by controlled contemporary science. The legitimate connection is methodological: who controlled the situation, whose description survived, and what independent check was possible before an explanation hardened into a fact? [5]

The [Mesmerism investigation](https://finalvestige.com/2026/11/02/mesmerism/) supplies a related source trail for Benjamin Franklin's 1784 commission. Those experiments asked whether a claimed mechanism had measurable effects when subjects did not know where the purported magnetic influence was directed. That is a different experimental question, but the family resemblance is useful: separate an experience from the explanation offered for it.

## What this experiment does not give marketers permission to claim

The appeal of choice blindness is itself a trap. “Scientists made people explain the opposite choice” can be repackaged into a promise about rewiring a brain. But the study did not test a meditation product, music, hypnosis download, binaural beats, productivity routine or brainwave audio. It cannot establish a treatment for anxiety or inattentiveness. The original manipulation required a researcher changing what was displayed, not a frequency playing through headphones.

Independent product evaluation begins with a harder question than whether an effect sounds related to a brand's story: **was this product, used in a manner like the sales page recommends, actually tested against an appropriate comparator for the outcome being promised?**

The Federal Trade Commission's health-products guidance is relevant here. A general study of a concept does not by itself substantiate a specific product's health claim; marketers need appropriate evidence for the claims a reasonable reader takes from the advertising. Similar caution applies to objective performance claims and implied scientific proof. [6]

Zen12 is described by its publisher as a progression of short audio sessions; Hypnosis Live sells a *Total Focus* self-hypnosis recording; Brainwave Shots describes a *Focus Shot* audio package. These are three different formats and three different sets of seller promises. We can verify that they are described this way, but their public sales pages are not, by themselves, independent product-outcome trials. [7–9]

When an offer page implies that pressing play will make concentration effortless, the honest response is to ask for evidence connecting that precise claim to the precise recording. Not to insist it must be impossible, and not to import support from a separate attention experiment simply because both involve the brain.

## A no-deception demonstration you can do yourself

Choice blindness experiments involved covert substitution. Our demonstration does not, and it is intentionally **not** a replica or diagnostic test.

**Minute 0–2: choose.** Before reading another claim about attention or meditation, write down one outcome you actually want. “Start writing a report” is an outcome; “unlock my mind” is not specific enough to measure. Add one practical constraint, such as a twelve-minute time window.

**Minute 2–4: record reasons first.** Write three things that would make an audio worth paying for. Perhaps a format you enjoy, files available offline, or a clearly stated price and refund process. Then write two things that would make you walk away: an unsupported clinical promise or a checkout price you cannot verify. Date the note.

**Minute 4–7: inspect the pitch.** Read the product description and separate what is in the box from what you are told will happen after using it. Count the claims for which the page supplies a product-specific trial. The count may be zero; do not invent a supporting study.

**Minute 7–10: inspect the alternative.** Try twelve minutes without paying on another occasion. Sit quietly, take a short walk, or begin one concrete task for the same duration. Record what you did, rather than assigning it a brainwave interpretation.

**Minute 10–12: compare your reasons.** Return to the note you made before encountering the promotion. Which requirements did the product clearly meet? Which remain unknown? Would you still want it if the words “scientifically proven” disappeared from the page?

This is a decision notebook, not a blinded trial. Familiarity, mood, expectancy, order and circumstances can influence what you notice. A brief personal trial cannot establish clinical efficacy or causal benefits. Its practical value is that it prevents the advertising you read *afterward* from silently becoming the reason you claim you had *before*.

## The practical buyer question

Consider three people.

The first wants guided meditation with a gradual sequence and multiple sound environments. That person may reasonably care about Zen12's described twelve levels and four audio styles per level. The relevant questions are format, personal preference, the actual charged price, terms, accessibility and whether the buyer understands the limits of any claimed benefits.

The second wants a single spoken self-hypnosis session. The seller's *Total Focus* page says its recording is thirty-three minutes and approximately forty-seven megabytes. That is not the same format or the same purchase as a multi-level meditation library. Nothing in the choice-blindness literature decides which is better.

The third wants a fifteen-minute focus soundtrack and perhaps a longer audio version. The *Focus Shot* page describes a fifteen-minute and a thirty-minute recording, plus versions without the ambient background. Again: knowing the file contents is useful; proof of a lasting performance effect is a separate requirement.

For all three, a satisfactory purchase is not one made under pressure to “discover your true potential.” It is one where the buyer knows what they are getting, how much it will cost at checkout, what the merchant actually promises to refund, and what claims remain unproven. Nobody has to subscribe to a second newsletter to make this evaluation. Zen12's free-audio gift is explicitly paired with the seller's own newsletter, separate from Final Vestige's existing Dispatch. [10]

If we eventually earn a commission from one of those offerings, that relationship must be disclosed beside the recommendation—not buried on another page. The recommendation must remain independent of whether the provider pays. Until account-specific acceptance, channel permissions and the exact authorized earning URL are verified, this manuscript contains no activation of a product link. [11]

## The point the trick actually makes

In the 2005 study, the remarkable thing wasn't simply that a photograph changed. It was that an explanation could remain fluent after the object of the explanation had changed. Fluency, confidence and precision of language are not the same as an accurate record of what happened.

An extraordinary historical claim deserves its dated document. An extraordinary neuroscience claim deserves its experiment. A paid audio claim deserves evidence about the paid audio. And our own buying decisions deserve the small courtesy of remembering the reasons we had *before* somebody supplied better-sounding ones.

That is the point of the photograph you didn't choose.
