# The Question That Put Glass on the Road
**Final Vestige Studios · Mind & Perception Archive · Original investigation · UNPUBLISHED SOURCE DRAFT · 18 November 2026**

*How a single verb changed people's accounts of a crash—and what that means for witnesses, historical archives, suggestion, and the stories we tell ourselves.*

**Editorial boundary:** An observation about eyewitness memory is not evidence that hypnosis or commercial meditation audio works. This is an investigation, not health advice, therapy, a recall-recovery tool, or an endorsement.

## The glass was not there

Imagine watching a short film of a collision. You see two vehicles meet. The footage ends. Then someone asks how fast the cars were going when they **smashed** into each other.

Not *touched*. Not *contacted*. *Smashed*.

A week passes. You are asked a second question: did you see broken glass?

In Elizabeth Loftus and John Palmer's 1974 experiment, there was no broken glass in the film. Nevertheless, some people answered yes. The critical detail is not that memory sometimes fails; that is ordinary. It is that different people had been given different verbs in the question about the same filmed event. The word came **after** what the observers saw. A prompt about an event became another source of information about the event. [1]

This is not a demonstration that a single word can control anyone. It is a demonstration that the phrasing of a later question can influence how an earlier experience is reported. That distinction is the entire case.

## A narrow experiment with a remarkably wide implication

In the first 1974 experiment, student participants watched automobile-accident films and estimated speed. The researchers varied the collision verb used in the prompt: *smashed, collided, bumped, hit,* or *contacted*. Higher-impact wording tended to produce higher estimates. Those numbers are judgments given in response to a question, not instrument readings of actual vehicle speed. The movie was unchanged between conditions; the language was not. [1]

The researchers' second experiment is more revealing because its question arrived later. A separate group watched a crash film. Some were asked a speed question using *smashed*; others heard *hit*; a control group received no speed question. One week later everyone answered questions about the crash, including whether broken glass appeared. It did not. Of 50 people in each group, 16 in the *smashed* condition said yes, versus seven in the *hit* condition and six in the control condition: 32%, 14%, and 12%, respectively. [1]

Pause at the denominators. Most people in **all three** conditions did not report broken glass. One phrase did not erase everyone's memory or install a detailed fictional scene in every mind. Nor can an affirmative answer to one questionnaire establish precisely what a participant privately pictured, how confident they were, or whether they would maintain the account under a careful open-ended interview.

What the design does support is narrower and still striking: when the original event stays constant, later language can shift both an immediate estimate and the frequency of a later false affirmative report. The paper's interpretation was that memories may be reconstructed with inputs from the event and the subsequent question. We should keep the result and the mechanism distinct. An experiment can identify a response difference without showing every internal step that produced it.

## The new detail arrives wearing a witness badge

An important next chapter came in 1978. Loftus, David Miller and Helen Burns studied whether misleading information introduced after a photographed event affected later recognition. Across five experiments and a pilot involving 1,242 participants, subjects saw a slide series depicting an auto-pedestrian incident. Later information was consistent, misleading or irrelevant. The researchers found poorer accuracy after misleading material on recognition tasks, including forced-choice tests. The timing of the misinformation also mattered. [2]

A recognition test is not the same as an unprompted account. A film is not a traumatic real-world accident. Student participants are not equivalent to every witness in a courtroom. Those limitations matter because claims about memory often balloon from "a laboratory report changed" to "all memories are fabricated," which the experiments do not establish.

The information introduced after an event also does not have to be a malicious lie. It might be an interviewer proposing an explanation, a headline, a photograph caption, another witness's confident retelling or a documentary voice-over. Each may supply a possible detail before the original observer gives their own description. This is why separating what someone saw from what they subsequently heard is an evidence-management task, not merely a lesson in skepticism.

## Can't people just detect the contradiction?

One tempting cure for misinformation is to expose witnesses to contradictory stories and hope they realize something is wrong. That possibility is worth testing, but it cannot be assumed.

Ewa Smołka and Hartmut Blank published a preregistered experimental replication in *Applied Cognitive Psychology* on 6 May 2024. They manipulated the availability of misleading information and compared single versus double, contradictory misinformation. In that study, presenting double rather than single misinformation did **not** improve performance or interact as predicted with misinformation availability; participants sometimes reported misinformation despite detecting discrepancies. [3]

This is a useful corrective to two oversimplifications. First, susceptibility is not simply the absence of alertness: detecting a contradiction and using it correctly in an answer are separate steps. Second, the study does not prove that all warning or source-monitoring interventions fail. It tested a particular design and predicted interaction; readers should inspect that design rather than elevate an individual null result into a universal verdict.

The replication offers an especially relevant lesson for a documentary audience. More conflicting claims, shown without provenance, are not necessarily better evidence. A conscientious editor preserves dates, original wording and the order of exposure. The question is not just "What did this person say?" but "What material had this person encountered before saying it?"

## A pressure-test outside the laboratory

The implications reach legal settings, but the evidence becomes more complicated there. A 2024 Campbell systematic review of interview and interrogation methods found that accusatorial approaches were associated with more false confessions than information-gathering approaches in the studies it assessed. This research addresses different procedures and a different outcome from the 1974 broken-glass question. It should not be used to present a laboratory effect size as a courtroom risk estimate. [4]

The distinction matters. Suggestion is not a magic power, and a false confession is not simply a memory error about a slide. Power imbalance, questioning style, incentives, stress, fatigue, and what an interviewee believes the questioner already knows can shape answers. The 2024 review also discusses methodological constraints and the need to evaluate generalizability. The useful common ground is procedural: questioning can shape evidence, so the history of each question must be preserved.

The safest principle for a recorded interview is ordinary rather than theatrical. Start with open prompts. Record exact questions and answers. Keep the first account separate from later corrections. Avoid feeding a disputed detail into the earliest description and then citing its later repetition as independent confirmation.

## Puységur: the eighteenth-century archive meets the modern question

Final Vestige's live [Puységur Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) document an eighteenth-century case-making system built from witness testimony, patient speech, predictions, signed statements and repeated procedures. It also retains failed predictions, uncertain cures and the archive's ethical limitations. This is the right historical bridge because it is an archive about **how claims became evidence**, not a neat story about which extraordinary claims must have been correct. [5]

Picture the distance between an original event and a printed report. A subject speaks. An observer selects what to write. A later edition arranges the record. Readers supply modern categories—hypnosis, mind control, subconscious programming—that the old text did not establish in our present scientific sense. Every passage is mediated by people, prompts and decisions.

The appropriate question is not "Did a nineteenth-century precursor of hypnotherapy prove a modern product's effectiveness?" It did not. It is "What observations were recorded, what leading expectations surrounded them, what failed, and what survives independently?" The same standard governs both historical witnesses and twenty-first-century sales copy.

Puységur's texts belong to their own setting. Loftus and Palmer's data belong to a laboratory setting. Their relationship is methodological—how to inspect reports under influence—not a claim that either proves the other's mechanism.

## Why the hypnosis connection is often mishandled

Suggestion, attention, hypnotic procedure, meditation and misinformation are overlapping **topics**, not interchangeable interventions. Some hypnosis research explicitly investigates memory illusions. A 2002 study using an associative-word false-memory paradigm observed high false recognition in both hypnotized and non-hypnotized conditions; the authors did not find support for hypnosis as a reliable memory-enhancement procedure in that experiment. [6]

That result does **not** establish that hypnosis is universally harmful, that nobody experiences relaxation, or that every form of clinical hypnosis is ineffective for every purpose. It does show why a commercial promise to "recover perfect memories" or "unlock truth" would require carefully matched evidence rather than citations to entirely different effects.

Similarly, meditation studies, general binaural-beat studies and eyewitness-memory research cannot serve as product-specific clinical evidence for Zen12, Hypnosis Live or Brainwave Shots. The 2023 systematic review of fourteen binaural-beat EEG studies found five outcomes consistent with the entrainment hypothesis, eight contradictory and one mixed, with heterogeneity limiting comparison. It did not evaluate Zen12. [7]

Listening to an audio program may be an individual's preferred way to structure a quiet interval. That is a consumer-format choice, not a proven claim that twelve minutes equals an hour of meditation or that an MP3 automatically changes brain state.

## An original twelve-minute evidence exercise—not a suggestibility test

This exercise does not attempt to implant false memories or assess a person's psychology. Its purpose is to practice **source separation** using a harmless object rather than a person or a traumatic event.

**Minutes 0–2 — Observe.** Look at an ordinary object in your room: a mug, lamp or book cover. Write five concrete features without interpretation. If you prefer, use an archival photograph with a known credit.

**Minutes 2–4 — Preserve the first record.** Close the image or turn away. Write a brief first account from memory. Date it. Do not edit the original record later; make corrections as separate notes.

**Minutes 4–6 — Examine wording.** On a fresh sheet, write two possible descriptions: one neutral, one loaded with a judgment ("the damaged old book" rather than "the book"). Underline every adjective not established by the observation.

**Minutes 6–8 — Identify layers.** Use three columns: *observed*, *remembered*, and *suggested by wording*. Mark each phrase honestly. The goal is not to manufacture a difference.

**Minutes 8–10 — Check the source.** Revisit the original. Note what you omitted or inferred. Do not claim a controlled experiment or a predictable illusion if no discrepancy occurred.

**Minutes 10–12 — Change the process.** Rewrite the description in neutral terms and add a one-sentence editorial rule: keep the original record intact and append subsequent interpretations separately.

The payoff is not a promised cognitive improvement. It is a practical habit: preserve the trail from source to statement.

## The real danger isn't a single word

It would be comforting to end with "avoid the word smashed" and declare the problem solved. But people make sense of events through language. Every documentary chooses verbs; every archive has titles; every interview starts with a prompt.

The problem is a system that lets later interpretation masquerade as an earlier observation. The better system makes that passage visible. It records what was there, exactly what was asked, when additional information arrived, and how much the result can bear.

An eyewitness may be sincere and mistaken. A historical investigator may be careful and still miss a crucial negative case. A seller may accurately describe the length of an audio file while making extraordinary claims about what it does to the brain. These are different questions, but the same discipline helps us answer each: separate observation, wording, inference and proof.

**A missing piece of glass is not proof that nobody remembers anything. It is a warning that an answer can carry more history than the event itself.**
