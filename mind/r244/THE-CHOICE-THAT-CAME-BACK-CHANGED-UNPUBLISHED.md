# The Choice That Came Back Changed
**An investigation into choice blindness, the explanations we invent, and the price of a convincing story**  
*Final Vestige Studios · Mind & Perception · Unpublished editorial manuscript · 9 November 2026*

A customer tastes two jams in a supermarket and picks the one she prefers. A moment later she is handed a sample of the choice she made, and asked to explain it. Only she is tasting the other jam. The researchers have exchanged them.

For a striking number of people in the experiment, the switch went unreported. Some supplied reasons for preferring a flavor they had just rejected. The surprise is not that people can be tricked by a concealed swap. It is the moment the mind begins explaining a decision whose result is no longer the decision it made.

This is **choice blindness**: a failure to detect a discrepancy between an intended choice and the outcome presented as that choice. It differs from failing to notice a changed building in a photograph, or missing an unexpected visitor in a basketball video. It concerns the link between **what you chose**, **what you were given**, and **what you say afterward about why**.

That link is enormously important to an evidence-first publication. It is also important when a polished product page offers an elegantly simple answer to a difficult question: What will help me concentrate? What does meditation do? How could I know? To make a careful purchase, we need to preserve the difference between a description, a preference and a proven result.

## A choice returns in disguise

In 2005 Petter Johansson, Lars Hall, Sverker Sikström and Andreas Olsson published a short paper in *Science* titled *Failure to Detect Mismatches Between Intention and Outcome in a Simple Decision Task*. Volunteers considered pairs of female faces, choosing one on perceived attractiveness. In selected trials the experimenters used a covert technique to present the **unchosen** face as if it had been the participant's selected face, then invited an explanation.

The study's central observation was not that every person failed, or that researchers had rewritten people's deepest beliefs. It was that some conspicuous intention–outcome mismatches went unnoticed, and that participants nonetheless offered introspective accounts of why they had made what now appeared to be their choice. The paper's abstract identifies both the mismatch and those subsequent explanations; that is the result we can attribute directly to the published record without adding a universal detection rate to the abstract. [1]

Notice what follows from the design. A participant who gives a reason for the face now in front of them may be constructing a sincere explanation for an outcome mistakenly understood as their own. That does not prove the explanation is knowingly dishonest. It also does not prove that all preferences are invented, or that an external speaker could reliably prescribe the participant's next decision. The experiment shows a specific weakness in monitoring the continuity of one decision.

The researchers called the effect *choice blindness*. The label is arresting, but it should not become a shortcut for saying human decision-making is worthless. In the same way, the failure to notice a door-swap on a campus does not mean all eyewitness identification fails, or that every visual scene is an illusion.

## Jam and tea: the experiment leaves the laboratory

Five years later, Hall, Johansson, Betty Tärning, Sikström and Thérèse Deutgen brought the question to a supermarket. They offered passersby pairs of jams and teas. After a shopper selected a preferred sample, the researchers secretly exchanged what was offered again for tasting and asked for an explanation. A choice between tastes became a chance to discover whether the person noticed the result no longer matched the selection.

Their 2010 *Cognition* paper reports detection in **no more than one-third of manipulated trials overall**. Even for some markedly different flavors and aromas, detection did not exceed half of trials. Those are properties of these experimental manipulations, **not estimates of how many consumers in ordinary stores can be deceived**, and not the claim that two-thirds of all people will always miss a switch. [2]

An ordinary taste preference seems more immediate than a judgment about an unfamiliar photograph. If you have just tasted a jam, you might expect to recognize it. The experiment's useful discomfort is that the confidence of that expectation is not itself an observation. The person who explains the rejected flavor has received a manipulated prompt and is reasoning from a mistaken premise.

This matters when a product page gives you the answer before you've framed the question. The page may provide real facts: its recording length, number of files, and type of guidance. But a buyer can also begin rehearsing the seller's answer to *why this will work for me* before any product-specific outcome has been measured. That commercial analogy is an editorial caution, **not a finding of the choice-blindness experiments**. Neither the face task nor the jam study tested Zen12, meditation, audio entrainment, or affiliate presell pages.

## When the thing exchanged is an opinion

A 2012 experiment by Hall, Johansson and Thomas Strandberg used a self-transforming paper questionnaire about moral attitudes. The researchers covertly reversed some responses before asking participants to explain them. The *PLOS ONE* paper reports that **69% of participants failed to detect at least one of two reversals** and that some constructed reasons supporting the reversed view. [3]

This is a more consequential domain than flavor. Yet the proper interpretation remains bounded. The percentage describes failure to notice at least one manipulation in that particular study, not the proportion of people with no convictions. It does not show that hypnosis, background sound or subliminal messages can implant beliefs. The researchers manipulated **the presented record of an answer**; they did not demonstrate the efficacy of a commercial intervention. The method raises questions about the reliability of immediate self-explanation and the role of context, not a license to assume all stated commitments are false.

A reader's instinct may be to make the story about other people: careless shoppers, impressionable volunteers, strangers susceptible to suggestion. But that is a second trap. These experiments are useful precisely because self-confidence is not a substitute for checking the evidence. The safest question is not *Would I have caught the swap?* but *What would make a mismatch visible before I explain it away?*

## Not every blindness is the same blindness

Final Vestige's published investigation of change blindness examines a different failure. In the 1998 door experiment, a person asking for directions was switched with someone else while a door passed between them. Eight of fifteen original participants did not report the swap. The manipulation concerned **recognizing a change across views** of another person; choice blindness concerns **recognizing a substitution between your decision and the displayed outcome**. [4]

Inattentional blindness is different again: while monitoring a task, someone may fail to notice an unexpected object that is present. And attentional blink describes impaired identification of a second target when it appears shortly after a first in a rapid sequence. These mechanisms are not interchangeable, even when their demonstrations are equally memorable.

This distinction protects the science and the site. A dramatic TikTok demonstration can introduce a phenomenon, but it cannot certify a general theory of attention, much less guarantee a buyer's ability to change their mind through an audio file. An experiment's constraints are part of what makes it evidence.

## Where suggestion enters—and where it does not

The historical story of animal magnetism invites the same discipline. Eighteenth-century investigators tried to distinguish sensations associated with expectation from effects attributed to a supposed magnetic agent. Final Vestige's existing Puységur Animal Magnetism Files is an editorial bridge into that period; its underlying page could not be directly retrieved for this draft, so no specific passage from it is paraphrased here. [5]

History offers a question that is still useful: How can we tell whether an account of an experience confirms the proposed cause? The choice-blindness experiments add a related question: How well can people verify that the **object of their explanation** is the one they actually selected?

Neither question proves that a person's feelings are unreal. Neither makes suggestion an automatic cure, a route to mental control, or a substitute for treatment. Reporting an experience, reconstructing its cause, and demonstrating a reliable intervention are three separate tasks.

## A free exercise: the decision ledger

You do not need to swap somebody's answer or secretly manipulate a friend to practice better verification. Take five minutes before choosing any inexpensive digital learning or audio product, and write down four things **before opening the sales page**:

1. **My actual goal:** for example, “I want a repeatable short wind-down routine,” not “I need my brain rewired.”
2. **My minimum acceptable feature:** a certain session length, downloadable files, clear instructions or an accessible format.
3. **What would count as evidence:** a specification I can verify, independent trials of this exact intervention, or clear terms I can read before purchase.
4. **My free alternative:** a simple timer, a borrowed book, a quiet twelve-minute practice or the existing Seven Day Focus Fieldbook.

Then visit the product description, return to your original note, and mark any sentence that changed from a **need** into the seller's **promised outcome**. This is an original reflective checklist. It is **not** a scientifically validated test of choice blindness or a claim that it improves cognition. It simply keeps your starting criteria available when the presentation becomes persuasive.

A second step is even more useful: write the reasons you would **not** purchase. If there is no independent trial of the exact product, if the terms are unclear, or if the free alternative fits the goal, that can be a perfectly adequate decision.

## Three audio products, three different questions

Consider three offerings catalogued by Inspire3, each with its own official product page. [6–9]

**Zen12 Premium** is described by its seller as a progressive meditation-audio library with twelve levels, 48 MP3 files, four listening options and twelve-minute sessions. A reader who genuinely values a structured audio collection may find that format relevant. But its advertisement that twelve minutes provides the benefit of an hour of advanced meditation is a seller assertion; the exact-product evidence needed to adopt that efficacy claim is not established in this investigation. The seller also advertises a year-long refund commitment. We have not tested cart conditions or a refund. [7]

**Brainwave Shots Focus Shot** is described as a set of fifteen- and thirty-minute tracks plus versions without ambient background. The seller says headphones are not required and advertises focus lasting four hours or longer. That duration claim remains independently unsubstantiated for the specific product. Its FAQ qualifies the advertised one-year refund promise: the seller says it will provide no more than one refund during a six-month period and applies a download limit to large orders. The same FAQ includes listening precautions. [8]

**Hypnosis Live Total Focus** is described as a 33-minute narrated recording, a materially different format from a course library or sound-only tool. Its cognitive and productivity outcomes are seller promises, not independently established benefits for that recording. The seller's FAQ describes a fourteen-day refund period and caps the number of refunded sessions annually. [9]

Choosing a format because it suits your routine is different from purchasing a medical, psychological or productivity result. Where a seller makes an objective benefit or safety claim, independent product-appropriate substantiation matters. A general neuroscience paper cannot simply be pasted beneath three different recordings and made to validate all three.

## The explanation should follow the record

An investigator's rule is simple: preserve the original before interpreting the substituted version. A product buyer can do the same. Save the actual requirements, read the exact seller description, and distinguish a feature from a proposed effect. The lab studies leave one enduring warning: explanations can sound coherent even when the decision they are purportedly explaining has been altered.

The practical protection is not cynicism. It is record-keeping, clear uncertainty, and a willingness to conclude that the free option is enough.
