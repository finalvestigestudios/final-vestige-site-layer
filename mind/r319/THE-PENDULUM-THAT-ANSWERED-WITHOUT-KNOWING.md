# The Pendulum That Answered Without Knowing

*Final Vestige Studios · Mind & Perception Archive · UNPUBLISHED editorial draft · November 12, 2026*

## A movement without a decision

Hold a short thread between your fingers. A small weight hangs at its end. Imagine that it will begin to swing left and right, and try not to move your hand deliberately. Sometimes, after a little while, the weight begins to travel in the direction you pictured. It looks as though it has received instructions that never passed through your muscles.

It is a genuinely interesting experience. But the first observation is that the weight moved, not that it received instructions from somewhere else. There is a gap between noticing movement and knowing its cause, and another gap between producing an action and *feeling* yourself choose it. The history of the hand-held pendulum sits inside those gaps.

A pendulum was once asked to find water, detect invisible forces, give answers and expose knowledge unavailable to speech. What can it actually do? More important, how would we tell?

## The nineteenth-century document trail

In 1854 French chemist Michel-Eugène Chevreul published a book whose title linked divining rods, the so-called exploratory pendulum, turning tables, criticism and the experimental method. A rare-book catalog at the Huntington identifies the work and its publication date. A catalog does not tell us every detail of the original experiments, however, so this history does not reconstruct conversations or individual tests that the catalog alone cannot verify. [Chevreul, 1854, Huntington catalog](https://www.huntington.org/collections/lib-705184).

In 1852 William Benjamin Carpenter published a paper on the influence of suggestion on muscular movement without a conscious intention to move. The Wellcome Collection retains digitized images of this historic pamphlet. The title neatly identifies the problem: the fact that a person did not choose an action in the ordinary, conscious sense does not establish that the movement happened without the body. [Carpenter, 1852, Wellcome](https://wellcomecollection.org/works/k532hmn2).

A contemporary argument erupted around another moving object. In August 1853 Scientific American summarized Michael Faraday's table-turning experiments and printed a correspondent's objections. The experimental account attributed motion of the table to pressure exerted by the sitters' hands, rather than to an electric force emerging from the gathering. The critic offered accounts of other, startling incidents. Those claims might have seemed connected socially, but they were not the same proposition experimentally. Measuring hand pressure in one arrangement is not a test of every extraordinary story told about a table in the dark. [Scientific American, 1853](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/table-moving-spirit-rappings-and-sc/).

It was a dispute about the apparatus. If the participant touches the instrument, even gently, the participant belongs in the explanation. Measuring or varying that contact is the beginning of an investigation, not a personal judgment about the observer's honesty.

## What the ideomotor effect does—and does not—explain

The term *ideomotor* describes a connection between thinking about movement and producing motor activity. Small contractions in fingers, wrists or arms can be difficult to notice. A suspended weight converts those tiny inputs into an obvious swing. The resulting movement may surprise the very person whose movements set it going.

Consider the distinct claims. One: the weight moves. Two: movements in the holder's body may explain it. Three: the holder feels that they did not consciously initiate it. Four: the weight supplies meaningful, accurate knowledge about the world. The first three can coexist. They do not logically entail the fourth.

An honest report of an unexpected movement remains an honest report. The error may enter when a witness shifts from describing the experience to explaining its origin. The scientific account does not require ridicule or an accusation of deliberate deceit.

## A camera instead of a witness impression

In 1976 Randolph Easton and Ronald Shor tested the Chevreul pendulum illusion among **60 college students**, using time-exposure photographic methods to quantify the oscillation. The publisher's abstract reports that the effect varied with undivided attention, the degree of muscular involvement in holding the pendulum, and the presence of external oscillating cues. In the tested conditions, visual stimuli were more influential than auditory ones.

The authors also reported a sex-related difference in their sample. That finding is not a warrant for claims about any person's susceptibility or for universal sex differences. Their abstract does not make the full underlying measurement table available, and we do not invent specific effect sizes from it. [Easton and Shor, 1976](https://doi.org/10.1080/00221309.1976.9710871).

Why was this useful? An instrumented photograph makes movement inspectable independently of what someone remembers seeing. If altering sensory feedback changes the result, that gives the researcher a testable relation. It does not require the pendulum to have any special access to hidden information.

## Eighty participants and the problem of accuracy

The apparatus became an answer device in a 2017 experiment by Jay Olson, Ewalina Jeyanesan and Amir Raz. **Eighty participants** searched for target letters among rapidly presented characters. They supplied responses in ordinary speech or by holding a pendulum and assigning movement patterns to yes and no.

The publicly available institutional abstract reports **57% accuracy for verbal responses and 53% for pendulum responses**. The authors also examined response bias: the tendency to answer a certain way even when accuracy is unchanged. In a follow-up involving **40 participants**, they again found a difference in bias between conditions.

Those findings do not show that the pendulum detects hidden targets more accurately than an ordinary response. Nor do exploratory personality correlations become a practical test of whether someone's answers should be trusted. They show that response mode may change how a decision is expressed. [Olson, Jeyanesan and Raz, 2017](https://digitalcommons.chapman.edu/psychology_articles/82/).

A pendulum can give a confident-looking yes. Whether yes is right is a different question. A demonstration without a known answer cannot answer that question at all.

## The replication that failed to reveal secret knowledge

In 2022 Gyorgy Moga and Zoltán Dienes took up a stronger claim: could an ideomotor device uncover factual knowledge when participants believed their verbal answers were guesses? They adapted an earlier Ouija-board design but used Chevreul's pendulum and changed several procedural details.

On questions previously rated as guesses, pendulum and verbal responses performed comparably, at around chance accuracy. The authors did not find evidence that pendulum movement reliably expressed knowledge inaccessible to verbal report in this protocol. They called for further research. [Moga and Dienes, 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35184700/).

A failed replication with a modified procedure does not demonstrate that every earlier result is impossible. But it does directly challenge a sweeping marketing claim: that a pendulum, finger signal or altered state naturally unlocks a private store of reliable answers. The stronger the claim, the more important it is to retain the studies that did not deliver the promised effect.

## What changes when two people share the pointer?

A 2018 open-access field study, appearing in a 2019 journal volume, examined a related problem with **40 volunteers** recruited at a Ouija-board conference. Marc Andersen and colleagues fitted participants with mobile eye trackers and compared ordinary Ouija use with a condition in which participants deliberately selected known letters and words.

In spontaneous sessions individuals were less likely to look in advance toward the letters the planchette would reach than when spelling deliberately. Believers also described a diminished sense of agency. Yet a pair-level analysis suggested that the interaction of the participants could generate increasingly meaningful and predictable sequences.

That does not show that a single person silently planned every letter. It suggests that a system involving two moving hands and mutual visual feedback can generate structured output while neither participant experiences ordinary authorship. These were self-selected enthusiasts, so the study is not a universal survey of how all people use Ouija boards. [Andersen and colleagues, 2018/2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11097-018-9585-8).

The feeling of not controlling an action is itself worthy of study. It is not proof that no human controlled the apparatus.

## The historical bridge: Puységur

Final Vestige's [Puységur Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) follows eighteenth-century claims of magnetic somnambulism, witness testimony, predictions, obedience and failures. Those documents predate the experimental pendulum literature. Modern pendulum research did not retest every historical case, and it would be careless to claim that the nineteenth-century explanation simply resolves the whole archive.

There is a shared evidentiary problem, however. A physician may have accurately recorded an unusual answer without establishing the proposed magnetic mechanism. A witness may describe a surprisingly confident statement without proving that it came from an extraordinary source.

The archive keeps failed cures and failed predictions visible. A responsible account of the pendulum keeps negative replications visible for the same reason. What an investigator hoped would happen must never quietly replace what was observed.

## Try observation, not divination

For a harmless exercise, suspend a small smooth washer from a short piece of thread over an empty tabletop, with an elbow comfortably supported. Avoid sharp or heavy objects and keep the thread away from children and animals. Watch the weight while imagining one motion, then another. Write down what you notice. Try again while looking away from the weight.

This is personal observation, not a controlled experiment. Air currents, posture, fatigue and the way you interpret motion remain uncontrolled. If someone prepares randomized cards showing expected movements, you can record your guess before revealing the cards, but even that does not eliminate all confounds.

Do not treat any motion as evidence about a diagnosis, another person's feelings, investment choices or future events. Do not use it as a measure of your intelligence, mental health or suggestibility. The useful result is noticing how expectation can enter a measurement while remaining largely invisible to the person making it.

## What an audio product cannot inherit from this research

A historical review from 2011 documents the use of ideomotor signals in certain hypnotic practices. That is a history of methods, not independent confirmation that a pendulum extracts reliable hidden facts. [Shenefelt, 2011](https://doi.org/10.1080/00029157.2011.10401754).

Similarly, research on ideomotor action does not prove that a meditation recording improves cognition or treats a medical condition. A seller can accurately describe an audio format, a recording length and the styles included. Turning those descriptions into assured psychological or physiological effects requires independent evidence specific to the product and outcome.

## What survives the investigation

The pendulum's movement is real. Tiny, unplanned human movements can produce its swing. Controlled research demonstrates relations between attention, sensory information and movement, and more recent studies distinguish the subjective feeling of agency from objective accuracy.

The evidence does not establish a mind-reading pendulum, reliable access to concealed knowledge or the effectiveness of commercial hypnosis and meditation audio. It also does not justify accusing every surprised operator of fraud.

What started as a weight on a string becomes a demanding question about evidence: when an action feels as if it came from somewhere else, how do we trace its actual source?
