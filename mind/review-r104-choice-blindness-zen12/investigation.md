# The Choice You Didn't Make

**Final Vestige Studios · Mind & Perception · Investigation draft · 3 November 2026**  
**Working slug:** `/mind/choice-blindness/` · **Status:** unpublished, independent research, no commerce  
**Standfirst:** A photograph changed hands. A preference became its opposite. And a person supplied a perfectly plausible reason for a decision they had never made. What the choice-blindness experiments really establish—and what they don't.

There is a particular confidence in the sentence *I know why I chose it*. It feels unlike a guess. The reasons arrive with the decision, so naturally that we seldom ask whether they arrived **after** the decision—or after somebody quietly changed its outcome.

In 2005, four researchers at Lund University published a short report in *Science*. They presented participants with pairs of photographs of women's faces, asked which face they found more attractive, and invited them to explain the choice. On some trials, a magician-like manipulation substituted the unchosen face for the chosen one. The person looking at the photograph was now being asked why they preferred a face they had not selected. Many did not flag the switch. They supplied reasons anyway. Petter Johansson, Lars Hall, Sverker Sikström, and Andreas Olsson called the phenomenon **choice blindness** [1].

That is an arresting result. It is also smaller, stranger, and more useful than the slogan *we never know why we do anything*. Choice blindness is not a verdict that all preferences are fabricated. It is evidence that, in particular circumstances, someone can fail to notice a mismatch between an intended choice and its presented outcome—and can construct an explanation for that outcome.

## A sleight of hand that revealed a scientific question

The researchers did not simply interview people about their motivations. They created a discrepancy that could be observed. In a typical choice, the photographed face shown back to the participant should match the selected face. On manipulated trials, the experimenters engineered a mismatch. **Choice** and **outcome** became separate things.

Why is that separation important? Because ordinary interviews cannot easily distinguish a faithful report of reasons from an explanation assembled after the fact. A participant who says a face was appealing for its expression might be reporting a genuine prior preference. Or, when the face was not the one selected, the same explanation could be a plausible reconstruction prompted by what now appears to be the result. The switch gives researchers a way to test the gap.

The original paper reported substantial failures to detect these mismatches [1]. It did not find that nobody ever noticed. Detection depends on the experimental circumstances, the person, the pair of stimuli, and exactly how a detection is counted. It is misleading to turn any one study's rate into a universal percentage of human self-deception.

There is a second restraint worth keeping: the participants were asked to select an *attractive face*. This is a subjective, low-stakes preference under experimental control. It is not equivalent to choosing a medical treatment, recalling a crime, evaluating a mortgage, or making a deeply held moral commitment. Whether similar effects arise in those settings requires fresh evidence, not a catchy analogy.

## It wasn't only the photographs

In 2010, Hall, Johansson, Betty Tärning, Sikström, and Thérèse Deutgen took the method into a supermarket [2]. Visitors tasted two jams or smelled two teas, picked a favorite, then were offered what was described as their choice again. On manipulated trials, the samples had been exchanged.

The researchers reported that no more than about one-third of manipulated trials were detected overall. Even when the alternatives differed considerably, detection did not become universal [2]. This was **a specific study of tasting and smelling**, not a population-wide estimate of shopper vulnerability. A different sampling procedure, stronger warning, a wider gap between options, or different instructions could change the results.

The supermarket design matters for a reason beyond spectacle. The researchers had moved from images to tastes and smells. We often experience flavor preferences as bodily certainties: *I know what I tasted*. Yet a verbally coherent explanation can emerge while a mismatch escapes attention. The experiment does not imply that taste is unreal. It means the connection between an earlier choice and a later explanation can be less secure than it feels.

## How far can an altered answer travel?

A 2013 *PLOS ONE* study used a more consequential context: political attitudes during a Swedish election [3]. Researchers obtained answers to political questions and then surreptitiously altered some of the answers before inviting participants to explain their positions. The reported detection and endorsement findings showed that some participants accepted—and reasoned about—positions inconsistent with the responses they had originally given.

The authors also observed movement in expressed willingness to consider political alternatives. But the crucial distinction is between **an answer given under an experimental manipulation** and **a durable change in political identity or actual voting behavior**. The study does not prove that opinions can be reliably rewritten in ordinary life, much less that an audio product, advert, or social-media technique can command another person's decisions.

This is precisely why the experiment is interesting for a public record. It asks not whether one political position is superior to another, but whether an apparently confident explanation can be influenced by the document, survey, or result a person is handed. The manipulation's existence, and the method that revealed it, matter more than any partisan interpretation.

## Three things that are often confused

**Not noticing a substitution** is a failure of mismatch detection. A participant may concentrate on evaluating the face or taste currently in front of them, rather than comparing it with a prior selection.

**Explaining the substituted outcome** is a second behavior. The explanation can be fluent, internally consistent, and nonetheless directed at an outcome the person did not choose. The studies show this can occur; they do not furnish a mind-reading instrument that labels every explanation false.

**Actually changing a preference** is a third claim. A person might genuinely revise a preference after considering an option, or they might merely accept a mistaken premise in an interview. These are not interchangeable. The 2005 photographs, 2010 supermarket and 2013 political-survey investigations used different tasks and cannot be pooled into a single number that describes everyone's preferences [1–3].

The phrase *confabulation* is sometimes applied to persuasive explanations generated without access to the true causal history. It should not be confused with a clinical diagnosis or used as shorthand for lying. A participant who supplies an explanation for a switched option need not be intentionally deceiving anyone. The experimenter created the deceptive presentation.

## The uncomfortable lesson for anyone who sells certainty

Inspire3 and other companies sell methods for meditation, hypnosis, attention and personal development. Their products belong in an **independent buyer's guide**, not in the conclusions of an investigation about choice blindness. What the experiment should change is our editorial posture—not the facts of a seller's product.

A persuasive explanation is not evidence of a causal effect. A testimonial may genuinely report a person's experience while failing to isolate what caused that experience. A stated preference may be sincere while still being influenced by the description of alternatives. And an elegant story about what a practice does to a brain does not replace a product-specific controlled trial.

These are general rules of inquiry. They follow from the logic of testing alternatives; the choice-blindness studies do **not** test meditation recordings, hypnosis MP3s, or Zen12. They cannot be enlisted to show that those products work, fail, or manipulate customers.

Our own house has the same obligations. A Final Vestige call to action should never use a manufactured countdown, disguised endorsement, or invented testimonial. Readers should be able to inspect a source, leave the page, and return without the experience being engineered to coerce a purchase.

## An echo from the eighteenth century—not an equivalence

The published **Puységur's Animal Magnetism Files** follows the evidence trail surrounding magnetic somnambulism, purported cures, obedience, observation, and testimony in the eighteenth century [4]. The historical actors argued over remarkable experiences and the forces believed to explain them.

There is a methodological bridge to the modern studies: *what did an observer actually see, what were they led to expect, and how was the event described afterward?* In both histories, the difference between an experience and an explanation is critical.

There is also a strict boundary. The 2005 choice-blindness experiment does not validate animal magnetism, establish that Puységur's subjects were manipulated in any specific way, or allow us to diagnose eighteenth-century witnesses. Historical archival documents and modern controlled experiments answer different kinds of questions. Their connection is a habit of scrutiny, not a claim of identical mechanisms.

## A small, honest exercise you can do without being tricked

This is a **reflection exercise, not a replication of the published studies**, and it cannot measure choice blindness. It deliberately contains **no substitution**, so there is no experimental claim hiding inside it.

1. Before reading further, write down two ordinary options for a low-stakes personal decision—for example, whether you would rather spend a quiet fifteen minutes reading or walking.
2. Choose one. Write a one-sentence reason **before** any discussion of the alternatives.
3. Later, ask yourself what new information would make you change your choice. Do not silently edit the original reason; compare your new answer with the original note.
4. Record whether the reason stayed stable, changed for an identifiable cause, or became uncertain. All three results are acceptable.

The exercise encourages a simple practice: keep the record of a decision separate from the later story about it. It is not proof that either story is false, and it is not a clinical or psychological test.

If you wish to experience genuine choice blindness, read the original research methods rather than asking a friend to tamper with your choices without consent. Experiments involving deception require appropriate ethical controls, debriefing, and respect for participants.

## What the evidence allows us to say

**Established within the cited experimental tasks:** researchers covertly changed the reported outcome of a selection, and some participants failed to detect the discrepancy while supplying explanations [1–3].

**Supported with limits:** similar effects have been reported for visually presented faces, tastes/smells, and altered political survey answers. Detection and interpretation are task-sensitive; a lab result should not be generalized to every person or every decision.

**Not established:** that all decisions are irrational, that first-person explanations never contain truth, that choices can be permanently implanted at will, or that any specific commercial audio intervention causes or prevents choice blindness.

When people ask why someone could explain a decision they never made, the first answer is not that human beings have no agency. It is that **our access to the path between preference, action and report can be imperfect**. The recorded switch—not the confidence of the explanation—is what made that finding testable.

The photograph on the table looked like an answer. Sometimes it was a question the experimenter had quietly changed.
