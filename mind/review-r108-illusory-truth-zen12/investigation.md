# The Sentence That Grew Truer Every Time It Was Heard

*The mind can recognize a sentence without recognizing whether it is right.*

Imagine walking past a noticeboard. One card reads: **The library closes at six.** You do not stop to check it. A day later you hear somebody repeat the same sentence. By the third encounter, you find yourself thinking of the closing time as something you know. This is a constructed scene, not a laboratory observation. It captures a genuine problem: a statement's familiarity can begin to stand in for its evidence.

The useful question is not whether repetition turns everybody into a believer. It does not. The useful question is narrower and more unsettling. When asked to judge a claim whose truth is uncertain, can previous exposure shift our judgment even if nothing new has been learned about the world?

Research over nearly five decades says that, under identifiable conditions, it can.

## An experiment with no new evidence

In 1977, Lynn Hasher, David Goldstein and Thomas Toppino asked participants to evaluate sixty general-knowledge statements drawn from such domains as sport, politics and the arts. The statements were plausible but not necessarily familiar. Participants evaluated statements on three occasions separated by two weeks. Some statements recurred; others were new.

The striking difference was not that everybody eventually accepted every repeated assertion. It was that judgments of validity increased for the repeated statements, whether those statements were true or false, while the comparison statements did not show the same increase. Repetition had been supplied; corroboration had not.

This early paper, *Frequency and the Conference of Referential Validity*, is an important starting point. It described a relationship between the frequency with which a claim appears and its apparent validity, not a magic formula by which any sentence can override reality. Its experimental materials and student sample matter. We should not treat its finding as a measured probability that any particular visitor will believe a TikTok headline.

[Primary study: Hasher, Goldstein & Toppino (1977)](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-5371(77)80012-1)

The difference between evidence and repetition is easy to see on paper. In ordinary life, they arrive together. A reliable fact is often repeated because it is correct. An unverified rumor may be repeated because it is captivating, profitable or simply convenient. The feeling that accompanies hearing the sentence again does not tell us which process produced it.

## When you already know the answer

A reassuring escape route would be to assume that existing knowledge defeats the effect. A 2015 paper challenged that assumption directly.

Lisa Fazio, Nadia Brashier, B. Keith Payne and Elizabeth Marsh studied what happens when statements are repeated even though participants can retrieve the information needed to recognize a false assertion. They found an illusory-truth effect even where participants demonstrated relevant knowledge. Their analysis suggested that people sometimes relied on the ease of processing a statement rather than drawing on what they knew.

This is a subtle finding. It does not mean participants permanently forgot correct facts. Nor does it prove that the informed and uninformed are equally vulnerable in every setting. It means stored knowledge and the act of using stored knowledge are different things.

You might know that a familiar sentence is wrong, yet fail to consult that knowledge at the moment a quick judgment is requested. That is an important distinction for a media environment optimized for rapid impressions.

[Primary study: Fazio et al. (2015), *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General*](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000098)

A later study by Fazio, published in 2020, also found increased perceived truth for repeated statements that contradicted known facts. That strengthens the observation, but the results are still experimental averages over particular tasks and items. It is not a promise that repetition will always overpower knowledge, nor that misinformation cannot be resisted.

[Primary study: Fazio (2020), *Collabra: Psychology*](https://doi.org/10.1525/collabra.347)

## Why familiarity can impersonate verification

Researchers often discuss *processing fluency*: a repeated phrase is easier to recognize and process. That ease may be misread as a cue that the statement is reliable. If a claim seems mentally effortless, the person hearing it may give the claim more credit without identifying a supporting source.

This account is plausible and supported by several experiments, but it should not be presented as a settled single mechanism that explains every form of misinformation. Familiarity can interact with memory for the source, plausibility, motivation, existing beliefs and what task the reader thinks they are doing.

Notice what the 1977 experiment did **not** add when it repeated a statement. There was no independent document, new witness, observation, or test. A repeated assertion and a replicated finding are not the same thing.

Replication is a second attempt to investigate a claim under specified methods. Repetition is another encounter with the same claim. The first can provide new evidence; the second may merely provide a smoother sentence.

This matters for a site built around historical documents. A newspaper's reprinting of another paper's account may create two clippings but only one originating witness. A series of websites citing one viral video may look like consensus while remaining a single chain of transmission. Source counting without source tracing is a trap.

## A practical boundary: accuracy attention

The research also points to a more useful response than trying never to hear a false claim twice.

In four experiments reported by Brashier, Eliseev and Marsh, prompting participants to evaluate accuracy at their initial exposure could eliminate the later illusory-truth effect **when participants possessed relevant knowledge**. The benefit was selective: accuracy attention cannot conjure facts that the evaluator does not know.

The result is encouraging without amounting to a guaranteed defense. Pausing to ask whether a claim is accurate creates an opportunity to use knowledge instead of fluency, but some claims remain genuinely difficult to verify and a short pause does not substitute for source work.

[Primary study: Brashier, Eliseev & Marsh (2020), *Cognition*](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2019.104054)

Here is the research-informed habit worth taking away: before repeating a striking claim, ask for its *earliest identifiable source*. Then ask whether each later account has independent access to the evidence. A source trail is not merely a set of hyperlinks; it is the history of where a claim obtained its apparent authority.

The particular checklist below is an original editorial exercise, not a validated therapeutic or educational intervention.

## The three-pass source exercise

Choose an ordinary, non-sensitive factual assertion you have seen more than once, such as a claim about a historical object's date. Do not choose a frightening medical claim or repeat a rumor about a private person.

**First pass — familiarity:** Write down the claim and how often you think you have encountered it. Keep that separate from your confidence in it. Do not assume recall is an accurate exposure count.

**Second pass — origin:** Find the earliest dated primary record you reasonably can. Record whether a later source actually inspected that record or simply echoed another writer. Separate original witnesses from copies.

**Third pass — contradictory evidence:** Search for a source that could disconfirm the assertion: an original archive description, dated catalog record, technical paper, court document or corrected edition. If you cannot verify it, label it unresolved instead of promoting it to fact.

For a close comparison, give the same three questions to a claim you remember seeing only once. The point is not to run an experiment in your living room. Without random assignment, controlled exposure, outcome measurement and analysis, you cannot infer an illusory-truth effect from the exercise. The point is to make familiarity and substantiation appear in different columns on the page.

## An eighteenth-century caution, not a retroactive explanation

Final Vestige's [Puységur's Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) documents the eighteenth-century problem of interpreting remarkable reports about suggestion and the altered states people appeared to enter. Reports, expectations, observers and institutions all shaped the historical record.

It would be unsound to say that Hasher's 1977 experiment *explains* the participants in Puységur's 1780s accounts. No such cross-century test was performed. The connection is methodological. In both settings, a responsible investigator must ask how a conclusion became familiar, who first recorded it, who repeated it, what independent checks existed and what remained unobserved.

The [Mesmerism investigation](https://finalvestige.com/2026/11/02/mesmerism/) makes an equally useful distinction: a person's reported experience is not automatically evidence for the mechanism offered to explain it. The Franklin commission's specific historical findings are distinct from a twenty-first-century laboratory study of plausibility judgments.

This is where history and cognitive science genuinely meet—not in a claim that ancient healers secretly discovered modern neuroscience, but in the ongoing discipline of separating what was witnessed from what was inferred.

## Repetition is not mind control

It is tempting to dramatize the effect: repeat something enough times and it becomes true to everybody. That conclusion is not supported.

Laboratory effects concern distributions of judgment, particular materials and experimental conditions. Size and durability can depend on plausibility, timing, source memory, knowledge and attention. People can reject repeated misinformation. Multiple independent, high-quality sources may also repeat a truth precisely because it has survived verification.

The strongest claim this investigation can make is a modest but powerful one: **the familiarity of a sentence is not independent evidence for its truth**. An extra encounter is not an extra witness.

In the final analysis, the reader's defense is not mystical immunity from suggestion. It is a better question: *Where did this claim come from, and what would count as evidence against it?*
