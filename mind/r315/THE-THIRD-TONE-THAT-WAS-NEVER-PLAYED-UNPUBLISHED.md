# The Third Tone That Was Never Played
*An evidence-led investigation into binaural beats, brainwave entrainment, and the gap between hearing an effect and buying a promise*

**Editorial status:** Unpublished research draft · 12 November 2026 · Final Vestige Studios, Mind & Perception. Not medical advice; not a product review based on personal use.

Two headphones produce two notes. One ear receives a steady tone at 400 hertz. The other receives one at 420. Yet some listeners perceive a rhythmic change at roughly twenty cycles per second, even though no speaker is playing a third twenty-hertz tone. The discrepancy is part of the perceptual event: the auditory system combines information arriving separately at the ears.

That is a genuine scientific phenomenon. The much larger claim—play the right audio file and a person will predictably enter a specified mental state—is a different proposition. Confusing the two has become an unusually efficient form of advertising.

A careful buyer should keep three questions apart. **Can a person perceive an apparent beat?** Yes, under suitable acoustic conditions. **Will that beat reliably drive a measured EEG rhythm in the brain?** The published evidence is inconsistent. **Will listening to a specific commercial recording improve attention, productivity, anxiety or memory?** That requires product-specific outcome research, which the public seller descriptions examined here do not supply. [1–4]

## A sound between the ears

It helps to distinguish two arrangements. When two nearby frequencies are physically mixed and reach both ears, their waves can produce an ordinary amplitude fluctuation—an acoustic beat. In a binaural-beat experiment, different pure tones are instead sent separately to each ear. The perceived modulation comes from neural processing of the separated signals, not from an external loudspeaker producing the difference-frequency sound. [1, 2]

Gerald Oster's 1973 *Scientific American* article, **“Auditory Beats in the Brain,”** treated the phenomenon as a window into auditory processing. Its publisher's description is remarkably restrained: different tones presented independently to each ear generate a sensation that may show how sounds are processed. It did not test a modern MP3 subscription, guarantee relaxation, or certify a shortcut to disciplined attention. [2]

That historical distinction matters. A curious percept becomes a laboratory tool. The laboratory tool becomes a metaphor about the brain. Then the metaphor is promoted as a mechanism that can allegedly produce a desired mental outcome on demand. These steps are not interchangeable, regardless of how smoothly a commercial story connects them.

One surprising part of the research is how specifically the percept depends on the listening conditions. The 2023 review describes stimulus frequencies, carrier tones and interaural differences, and notes that perception varies among people. A stereo headphone experience may be essential to presenting distinct tones to the ears in a classic binaural arrangement. Yet the mere use of headphones establishes neither EEG entrainment nor practical benefit. [1]

## The 14-study test

The phrase *brainwave entrainment* is widely used to describe an idea: a rhythm outside the brain might induce neural activity that follows it. That is a testable neurophysiological hypothesis—not a synonym for hearing a beat, nor a guarantee of learning or calm.

In 2023, Ruth Maria Ingendoh, Ella S. Posny and Angela Heine published a systematic review in *PLOS ONE*, specifically examining whether binaural-beat stimulation influenced brain oscillatory activity measured with electroencephalography. Fourteen studies met their criteria. Five reported findings consistent with the entrainment hypothesis; eight reported contradictory results; one reported mixed results. The authors stressed that experiments differed substantially in their stimuli, procedures and EEG analysis. [1]

The numbers do not establish that binaural beats never influence brain activity. Nor do they allow a neat verdict that five positive studies cancel eight negative ones. Heterogeneity makes pooled interpretation difficult. But they do rule out the sweeping sentence that the EEG mechanism has been universally proven and will work identically for every listener.

A laboratory result may use one difference frequency, one exposure duration, particular headphones and a specific outcome measure. A seller's recording may use music, nature sounds, narration or other layers, different frequencies, and a completely different outcome promise. A mechanistic association cannot be copied wholesale from the first setting into the second.

## A result that appears more encouraging—and why it needs care

In a review and meta-analysis indexed in 2022 and published in *Psychological Research* in 2023, Sandhya Basu and Bidisha Banerjee examined binaural-beat interventions for memory and attention. They included fifteen studies, analyzed thirty-one effect sizes, and reported a medium overall effect size of **g = 0.40**. The same abstract also describes conflicting individual findings, including uncertainty around theta and beta frequencies. [3]

This evidence should neither be hidden nor turned into a sales claim. The studies pooled in that analysis were not a trial of Zen12, Focus Shot, or Total Focus. The mean across heterogeneous studies does not identify which exact audio product will work, on whom, with which tasks, and compared with what control. Nor is an average standardized effect a promise that any individual will become forty percent more focused.

Different review questions also produce different findings. The PLOS review asked about measured oscillatory entrainment; the Basu–Banerjee analysis examined attention and memory outcomes. A listener might perform differently on a task for reasons not uniquely attributable to EEG frequency locking. Treating these outcomes as one interchangeable “proven brainwave technology” collapses the distinction the science needs most.

A 2024 review by Chee and colleagues considered thirty-one studies of music, ambient noise, white noise and binaural beats, asking how physiological arousal measures related to cognitive performance. Its conclusion was cautious: results were mixed or insufficient, and research often assessed physiological arousal and performance separately. [4] A separate systematic review by Platt and Hammond, published online in July 2024 and later appearing in a 2025 journal issue, examined twelve randomized studies of non-clinical stress management; eight reported at least one favorable significant effect while four found no group effect. The authors nonetheless found methodological limitations and insufficient evidence for widespread recommendations. That was a stress review, not proof of commercial focus enhancement. [5]

There is no contradiction in saying that a percept is real, some interventions appear promising, some studies find effects, and a named brand's broad promises remain unsubstantiated.

## The passage from research to the sales page

The official Inspire3 catalog directs consumers to several products with overlapping psychological language, but their actual product formats differ. Zen12 describes a structured meditation-audio program: twelve levels, four styles per level, and a complete package advertised as forty-eight MP3 recordings. Its public pages claim that twelve minutes can produce the benefits of an hour of ordinary meditation. That equivalence is a **seller claim**, not a finding established by the independent research reviewed above. [6, 7]

Brainwave Shots' **Focus Shot** page describes fifteen- and thirty-minute versions plus supplementary audio variants. It also promises prolonged concentration and refers to brainwave entrainment. Those promised outcomes are not independently verified by the 2023 mechanism review, the 2023 memory-and-attention synthesis, or the 2024 arousal review. The page's money-back and safety language is merchant language, not an independent refund or safety audit. [8]

Hypnosis Live's **Total Focus** is a separate, seller-described thirty-three-minute, forty-seven-megabyte hypnosis recording, attributed on the product page to J. A. Amos. Its claims concern suggestion, NLP and concentration, not a documented replication of the binaural-beat trials. Its price display has varied across independently observed snapshots; a trustworthy buying guide should use a freshly verified checkout instead of hard-coding an attractive figure. [9]

These are three different purchasing decisions. **Meditation-program structure, brainwave-style audio, and a hypnosis script** are not equivalent interventions merely because all three trade on the audience's wish to focus.

The affiliate application is also not the authorization. Inspire3 advertises a public affiliate recruitment system and says it issues special links to accepted promoters. That generic page cannot establish that Final Vestige Studios has been admitted, which channels are allowed, or that any particular link tracks an actual cleared purchase. [10]

## An original observation, without a miracle promised

Try a low-stakes experiment before purchasing any attention audio. Choose a small task you can repeat in similar conditions, such as reading a short passage and answering three comprehension questions or sorting a pile of papers. Record the task, the time, whether you finished, and a short description of distractions. Repeat on another comparable day in quiet or with your ordinary background sound. Do not claim a health outcome from this diary; a tiny self-comparison is not blinded clinical evidence, and ordinary day-to-day variation can be large.

If you later choose a paid audio, treat the same record as a practical decision aid, not a neurological measurement. Use a comparable task and decide in advance what counts as useful. Note discomfort or distraction, keep volume comfortable, and stop listening if unpleasant. There is no reason to force an experience to fit a vendor's theory.

Its planning methods have separate research support but the Fieldbook itself has not been clinically tested. [11]

## Why this belongs in a historical archive

Readers of Final Vestige's [Puységur Animal Magnetism Files](https://finalvestige.com/free-resources/puysegurs-animal-magnetism-files/) will recognize the structure of the argument. Eighteenth-century observers assembled experiences, witnesses, repetitions, predictions and signed documents into a case for animal magnetism. Their unusually careful paperwork did not prove the proposed magnetic fluid. They also recorded refusals, failed predictions and warnings about operator pressure. [12]

The modern parallel is **methodological, not mechanistic**. We are not saying that brainwave audio is animal magnetism. We are saying that an observed effect, a persuasive theory of its cause, and a practical treatment claim need different evidence. A source can prove that somebody heard something without proving that an advertisement about the sound is true.

This also illuminates [Final Vestige's published Change Blindness investigation](https://finalvestige.com/2026/11/02/change-blindness/). That study describes when attention and memory fail in visual settings. It does not follow that increasing the subjective feeling of focus through audio necessarily fixes those failures. Perceptual science is most useful when its limitations survive the trip from an experiment to a reader.

## A fair buyer's checklist

Before paying for a recording, ask what audio files and support actually arrive; whether the format suits how you listen; whether the provider offers the sample or free trial described; which benefit claims are backed by research on that **particular product**; what current checkout and refund terms say; and whether the recommendation comes from someone who stands to earn a commission.

In the case of Final Vestige, a future earning link would require an immediate, plain-language notice: **“If you purchase through this link, Final Vestige may earn a commission at no additional cost to you.”** This unpublished manuscript contains no purchasing affiliate link and makes no claim that the author used any of the three products.

The most intriguing sound in this story remains the third tone that was never physically played. Its existence is a testament to how hearing works. It is not a purchase order.
