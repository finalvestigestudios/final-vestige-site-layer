# The Gorilla That Walked Through a Job
*Original Final Vestige Mind & Perception investigation — unpublished, 2 November 2026*

A figure crosses the scene. The viewer does not report seeing it. Nothing conceals it, and no trick editing removes it. What changes is the task: instead of watching for everything, the viewer has been asked to count something specific.

The celebrated gorilla experiment is commonly retold as a verdict on human eyesight. It is a much better story about what an observer is *doing*. If we erase the instructions and the experimental comparisons, we lose the very evidence that makes the finding meaningful.

## The assignment

In 1999 psychologists Daniel J. Simons and Christopher F. Chabris published *Gorillas in Our Midst: Sustained Inattentional Blindness for Dynamic Events* in the journal *Perception*. Their participants watched videos involving people passing basketballs and performed a selective monitoring task. An unexpected figure entered the scene. Across the conditions, that unexpected event could be a person with an umbrella or a person in a gorilla costume. [1]

The scene was not simply a test of whether a reasonably sighted person could recognize a gorilla. The observer was busy trying to keep track of the behavior of a designated group. Detecting the surprise was not part of the instructions. The researchers changed task difficulty, video presentation and the resemblance of the visitor to attended objects.

Their abstract reports that similarity to attended material and difficulty of the primary monitoring task mattered for detection. Proximity to a location the observer was attending did not straightforwardly guarantee noticing. That is the substance of the research. The costume is an instrument for revealing it.

In popular versions, the story sometimes hardens into “half of people cannot see a gorilla.” That number is not a stable trait of the public. Study-specific noticing rates differ by experimental condition. Someone warned to look for a gorilla is not a naive participant; someone seeing the film again is doing another task. Even a person who fails to report the visitor cannot be diagnosed as generally unobservant. [1]

## What was missed?

The technical term is *inattentional blindness*. An unexpected visible event fails to become part of what someone reports noticing while attention is directed toward a different task. It is not clinical blindness and does not mean an object never produced visual stimulation. The experiment observes a report under specified conditions. It does not read the private contents of every moment of consciousness.

This subtlety matters. Was some information about the unexpected figure processed and then lost before the participant answered the question? Did it never reach reportable awareness? Was a fragment noticed but not recognized as important? None of those detailed psychological histories can simply be read off the answer “no.” The experiment supplies evidence for an effect, not an exhaustive biography of a moment inside a mind.

There is also a difference between inattentional blindness and *change blindness*. In the latter, an observer may miss an alteration between successive views. Simons and Daniel Levin's 1998 work, in which a person could be substituted during a disrupted conversation, is one example. The gorilla experiment instead introduces an unexpected event during an ongoing demanding task. Related concerns about attention, different procedures, different inferences. [2]

## The work before the viral video

Selective looking had a research history before the gorilla entered the picture. Ulric Neisser and Robert Becklen's 1975 paper examined divided attention to visual events. Their work helped establish how attending to one stream could alter reports about another. [3]

The later gorilla case earned a place in public memory because the visitor appeared so conspicuous. It offended a common intuition: surely an absurd costume moving through a basketball scene would override anything as mundane as counting passes. Yet an unexpected event could be visible in the film and still go unreported.

This made a strong demonstration, but demonstrations can distract from experiments. Showing someone a surprising clip is not the same as presenting condition-by-condition comparisons, documenting exclusions or explaining the boundaries of the effect. The novelty is not evidence on its own; it is a reason to examine the design.

## When the observer is an expert

In 2013 Trafton Drew, Melissa Võ and Jeremy Wolfe took a related problem into an image-search task familiar to radiologists. Twenty-four expert readers examined lung CT images for nodules. The researchers placed an unusual gorilla-shaped image into the final case. Twenty of the twenty-four radiologists did not report seeing the inserted figure. The researchers expressed this as 83% of that particular group. [4]

That percentage should not become “83% of physicians miss serious diseases.” The inserted object was a contrived surprise, not an actual pathology, and the study was not a trial of patient outcomes. It probed whether professional experience with a particular search eliminated vulnerability to unexpected items. It did not show that expertise lacks value.

Eye tracking produced another uncomfortable detail: most of those who missed the figure had looked at the region containing it. Looking at a place and reporting everything contained in that place are not equivalent. Gaze direction is observable; full subjective awareness is not. After being alerted, the participants could recognize the image. [4]

That finding is unsettling precisely because it is narrower than a blanket claim that the eyes are useless. A person can look toward a region and still have attention organized around another target class. The professional task is meaningful. So is the cost of that selection.

## Fame changes the experiment

A visitor must be unexpected for the classic demonstration to measure what it was designed to measure. Once the world knows to look for the gorilla, the task has changed. A replay might now teach the history of attention research, but it cannot reproduce the initial uncertainty for a viewer already primed to search for a costume.

This is why the accompanying Final Vestige short does not pretend to recreate the original film. It uses original geometric counters and an unfamiliar outlined figure to explain what a selective task means. It does not administer a study, score perception, claim to diagnose attention difficulties or provide commercial training. It is a forty-second illustration of a research question.

The temptation, especially in short-form video, is to transform surprise into judgment: “If you missed it, your mind is broken,” or “If you saw it, you are unusually gifted.” The science supplies neither conclusion. It supplies evidence about how task demands and unexpected events interact.

## A source archive as a counterpoint

Final Vestige's published *Puységur's Animal Magnetism Files* examines an eighteenth-century system of reported magnetic somnambulism, testimony, witness statements, cures, predictions and the effort to preserve extraordinary experiences in written evidence. [5]

The relevant bridge is method, not proof. A person may sincerely report what they experienced while an investigator still must establish what caused it. Instructions can influence what observers attend to; expectations can influence which statements are preserved; a compelling report may lack a control that could distinguish competing explanations. These problems belong both to historical source criticism and to modern experimental design.

No finding about missed gorillas verifies animal magnetism. Nor does an attention experiment prove that every extraordinary historical report is false. Historical papers and laboratory protocols answer different questions. The honest connection is a discipline: identify the witness, record the conditions, separate the reported experience from its interpretation, and name the comparisons that could have changed the conclusion.

In the basketball study, ask who counted what, which video they saw, how the surprise entered and how detection was assessed. In Puységur's files, ask who spoke, who wrote the statement, who witnessed the event and which alternate causes were considered. The question is not whether all witnesses are unreliable. It is how a record earns the confidence its narrator asks us to grant.

## The free field test

Choose a photograph you are allowed to examine. Before looking, write one constrained goal—count every bicycle, for example. Study the scene for twenty seconds. Hide it. Write what you remember outside that assigned category. Then look again.

This is a small personal observation exercise, **not a reproduction of the scientific experiment**. There are no randomized conditions, no concealed surprise, no participant blinding, and no valid score for attention or neurological health. It cannot establish that you have inattentional blindness in a clinical sense or that one meditation method fixes it.

Its point is to make the assigned goal visible. What did you notice because you were asked to look for it? Which background elements did you fail to record? Were you sure you saw something that the photograph does not actually contain? Keep observations and interpretations on separate lines. That habit is more valuable than declaring yourself a good or bad observer.

The separate, published *Seven Day Focus Fieldbook* is available as an optional free reflection resource. It is not a clinical attention-training product and its existence should not be used to claim that a commercial recording improves the outcomes of gorilla studies. [6]

## What the gorilla can tell us

The effect does not show that seeing is always a lie. Attention is useful partly because it is selective. The same selection that allows a person to complete a complicated task can make an unexpected event less likely to enter their later report. Studies can discover the circumstances under which that happens without promising a universal rate, a diagnosis, or an instant cure.

The next time someone insists that something was too obvious to miss, a better first question is what the observers had been instructed to look for. Then ask what they actually reported, how soon they were questioned, and what independent record of the scene survives.

The figure did not become physically invisible. The job changed what was likely to be noticed.
