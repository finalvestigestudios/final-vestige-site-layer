The Mouth Said Ga. The Ear Heard Da.

The experiment that made vision an accomplice in hearing—and why its most famous demonstration requires a closer look.

A sound that no one had recorded

Imagine sitting in a small room with a television showing a face. The mouth moves; the soundtrack repeats a simple syllable. The speaker's lips appear to be forming ga, but the audio track contains ba. Asked what you heard, you might answer da.

There was no da on the recording. The answer was assembled by a listener whose eyes and ears had supplied conflicting information.

That is the unforgettable observation behind Harry McGurk and John MacDonald's short 1976 paper, Hearing Lips and Seeing Voices. Their experiment did not demonstrate telepathy, subliminal control or a hidden power of suggestion. It exposed something more ordinary and more unsettling: a sensory report can be altered by information that arrived through a different sense. The question, then, is not simply whether the observer is mistaken. It is what hearing means when the observer can see the speaker.

We tend to picture perception as a series of separate streams: sight records images, hearing records sounds, and the mind consults those records afterward. Speech does not obey that tidy arrangement. In conversation, we see faces and mouths before, during and after sounds. The brain must interpret a speaker in real time, often amid noise, uncertainty and incomplete syllables. Under some conditions, the visible movement of a mouth changes the very sound a listener reports.

To understand why the finding matters, it helps to reconstruct precisely what was—and was not—put on the screen.

The mismatched recording

McGurk and MacDonald described a film of a talking face in which the visible articulation of ga was combined with the soundtrack of ba. Some viewers reported da, an answer corresponding to neither isolated input. The reverse combination—the visible ba with auditory ga—was often reported as something more like bagba or gaba. That asymmetry matters. The original article was not a demonstration that any mismatched mouth and voice produces the same result.

The critical controls were equally important. When participants heard the soundtrack without the mismatched picture, or saw an untreated film, they could identify the expected syllables. Those comparisons helped isolate the influence of conflicting visual speech. If an audio-only track is already ambiguous, a report of da would tell us much less about the contribution of the face.

The paper appeared in Nature, volume 264, pages 746–748, on 23 December 1976. Its terse three pages hardly resemble the way the illusion later spread through classrooms and short-form videos. The experiment has been used to introduce multisensory perception, and sometimes to make sweeping claims that the brain 'creates reality.' The first conclusion is legitimate. The second is too broad for the evidence.

What the paper established was more specific: for certain carefully constructed speech stimuli, a discrepant visible articulation influenced listeners' reports of an audible syllable. That is remarkable enough without borrowing claims from hypnosis or metaphysics.

An accident worth following

The discovery also has a human history. In a later retrospective, MacDonald described the development of the research and the unexpected character of the finding. The original audiovisual combinations had emerged from work on how infants coordinate sight and sound. The mismatch became interesting because the audio appeared to be heard differently when accompanied by the incompatible lips.

That is an important distinction between discovery and proof. Something surprising was noticed, then it had to be tested against conditions designed to rule out simpler explanations. Curiosity supplied the opening; comparison supplied the evidence.

The method itself carried limitations. Dubbed films of isolated syllables are not natural conversation. Timing, the particular speaker, audio quality, the individual mouth movements and the selection of response alternatives can all influence what a participant says. The demonstration deliberately breaks the correspondence that real speakers usually maintain. This makes it a useful probe of perception, not necessarily a direct model of how every listener understands a normal sentence.

The face and the voice are not rivals

Why would the brain use a visual cue when we think we are simply listening? One plausible starting point is that the movement of lips, jaw and tongue conveys information about speech production. Some sounds are more visibly distinguishable than others; some are acoustically confusable, especially in noise.

Visual speech can therefore help listeners identify spoken material. But an experiment with conflicting cues poses a different question from a study of helpful cues. If a laboratory participant reports an unexpected syllable, that response need not tell us that ordinary visual speech is unreliable. In natural conversations, the face usually belongs to the voice.

This distinction is central to a 2022 analysis by Kristin Van Engen and colleagues, Audiovisual Speech Perception: Moving Beyond McGurk. The authors argue that the popularity of the classic illusion has sometimes encouraged researchers to treat it as a stand-in for everyday audiovisual comprehension. They point out that susceptibility to incongruent syllables and benefit from matching visual information are not identical capacities.

In other words, the most dramatic laboratory trick is not necessarily the best measurement of the practical skill we care about. A listener who rarely reports the famous fused syllable may still use facial movements productively when speech is noisy.

The replication question that makes the story stronger

It is tempting to write, 'Everyone hears da.' That would make a sharp headline—and it would be wrong.

A 2021 re-examination by independent researchers tested how different tasks, response options and environments affected reports from McGurk-type stimuli. Across experiments using the same audiovisual materials, the proportion of fusion responses ranged from 10% to 60%. The researchers argued that what looks like a robust illusion in one setup may weaken sharply in another.

That range is not a global prevalence estimate. It belongs to those experimental comparisons and must not be treated as the percentage of the population whose hearing is altered. The useful lesson is methodological: even with the same stimulus, the way investigators ask a question can influence the apparent strength of a perceptual effect.

A broader review also reports enormous variation between participants and stimuli. Some listeners show strong fusion responses; others do not. The degree of visual-speech skill, the specifics of the audiovisual pair and the experimental instructions may matter. It is possible to agree that visual information affects speech perception while disagreeing about whether this one striking mismatch should be the standard test of that phenomenon.

An honest investigation does not hide the qualification in a footnote. It makes the qualification the story. The effect survives as a subject of study because scientists can test, challenge and refine the account, not because every viewer hears the same magic syllable.

What an honest demonstration would require

For a short video, the familiar temptation is to display lips moving and promise that the viewer will hear a third syllable. We should not make that promise. A convincing demonstration would need an original synchronized audio-and-video master, an audio-only comparison, clear instructions, captions and an explicit notice that responses differ.

Captions deserve special care. If a caption says 'da' while the sound says 'ba', it could prime the answer or obscure whether the lips caused the difference. For an educational film, the spoken and visible tracks should be described after the viewer has had a chance to report what they actually perceived. The video must remain optional; a transcript should explain the experimental setup rather than claim that reading text reproduces the illusion.

A free observation exercise: audit your certainty

You do not need to recreate the original experiment to practice its central research habit. With a friend and their permission, watch a short original recording of a neutral sentence without sound and write what you think was said. Then listen to its soundtrack without watching and write down the words. Finally experience both together. Compare your three reports.

This is not a validated replication of the McGurk experiment: it uses normal speech rather than deliberately mismatched syllables, and the reports cannot measure your susceptibility to the illusion. It is a practical way to notice how incomplete evidence, expectation and confidence can be separated. Avoid recording anyone who has not agreed, and do not share private recordings.

Your most useful answer may be 'I couldn't tell.' That answer preserves a boundary the mind often wants to cross.

A cautionary bridge to hypnosis and suggestion

The history of animal magnetism is full of testimony about what people believed they experienced. Final Vestige's Puységur's Animal Magnetism Files investigates how eighteenth-century operators recorded speech, altered-state claims, apparent cures, disputed predictions, coercion and failures. That archive preserves the distinction between a documented historical report and independent proof of the theory that produced it.

The McGurk phenomenon belongs to a very different scientific tradition. Its researchers manipulated an audiovisual stimulus under comparison conditions; the historic magnetizers interpreted bodily and interpersonal events through a theoretical system that they also hoped to vindicate. Connecting the two should sharpen our standards of evidence, not erase the differences.

Neither the 1976 work nor later audiovisual-speech studies demonstrate that hypnosis can unlock hidden memories, that suggestion cures disease, or that commercial audio recordings control brainwaves. A change in one kind of perceptual report is not a general license to make claims about consciousness.

That is why Mind & Perception belongs inside a history-and-evidence publication. The subject is not the sale of a better mind. It is the difficult, fascinating work of learning what a witness's report can—and cannot—tell us.

The question to keep

On a muted screen, a mouth seems to say one thing. With the speakers turned on, the ears receive another. Some observers report a sound no one put on the soundtrack. Others do not. Each response is a piece of evidence, but it is not the whole experiment.

The extraordinary part is not that human perception is broken. It is that multiple sources of information are normally integrated so seamlessly that their cooperation goes unnoticed until a laboratory makes them disagree.

The next time a video declares that your eyes have fooled your ears, ask three questions: What was in the original audio? What was visible? What happened in the comparison condition? Those questions are a better souvenir than a promise that your brain has been hacked.
