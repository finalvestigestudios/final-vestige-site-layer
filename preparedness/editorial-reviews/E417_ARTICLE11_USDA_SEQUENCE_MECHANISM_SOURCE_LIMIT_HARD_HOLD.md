## New, meaningful primary-source distinction

USDA FSIS's official-domain indexed [Why Is Temperature Important When Making Jerky?](https://ask.fsis.usda.gov/article/Why-is-temperature-important-when-making-jerky) (shown last updated **2024-11-18**) explains a **mechanism**, not merely a procedural preference: evaporating water absorbs heat while the meat is drying, and surviving bacteria may grow more heat-resistant as drying proceeds. Its separate [How Can I Make Meat or Poultry Jerky at Home?](https://ask.fsis.usda.gov/article/How-do-I-dry-jerky) (shown last updated **2025-02-04**) emphasizes treatment **before** dehydration. Complete apparent text was returned from official-domain search indexing. **Direct retrieval of the original USDA article pages timed out again 2026-11-16**; these are **INDEXED CONTENT ONLY**, not authenticated full live agency pages. Neither numeric schedule nor recipe is reproduced here.

This scientific concern is relevant to the **directly opened** [University of Minnesota Extension drying guide](https://extension.umn.edu/food/preparing/cooking-at-home/food-preservation/drying) (reviewed 2026), which describes a treatment **after** drying, and [NCHFP's jerky chapter](https://nchfp.uga.edu/how/dry/recipes/jerky/) (reproduced from the **2014** UGA *So Easy to Preserve*), which describes differently conditioned methods before **or** after drying. The FSIS mechanism **does not prove** NCHFP's complete conditioned after-drying method is unsafe. Nor may an editor take one line from each source and invent a combined home procedure.

### Literal E417 reviewer-candidate replacement for the Article #11 heat-treatment subsection

> A dehydrator can take water out of meat. That does not tell you what happened to bacteria that may have been present before the meat dried. A brown surface, a leathery bend and hours on a machine's display are not measurements of pathogen control. An appealing finished product and a demonstrated safety process are different things.
>
> **Why the order matters:** The USDA Food Safety and Inspection Service explains that evaporation absorbs heat early in dehydration, while some surviving bacteria become more heat-resistant as the food dries. Its separate jerky Q&A consequently emphasizes treating meat *before* dehydration. These are official-domain search-index texts, last updated November 18, 2024 and February 4, 2025 respectively; attempts to open the original USDA pages timed out on November 16, 2026. We can verify what the indexed records say, not certify that we have examined every line of their live original pages.
>
> Other public university material must be read with the same discipline. The University of Minnesota Extension drying guide, reviewed in 2026, describes an additional oven stage *after* jerky has dried. The National Center for Home Food Preservation's jerky chapter, extracted from a 2014 University of Georgia publication, describes separately conditioned alternatives involving heat before or after drying. Both original pages were directly accessible on November 16, 2026. The USDA explanation gives a reason to question casual substitutions; it does *not*, by itself, establish that one fully specified NCHFP method fails.
>
> The unresolved editorial question is exact: **which complete, validated process applies to which meat, organism and household equipment?** A qualified food-safety reviewer must reconcile the current USDA interpretation with the narrower conditions and age of the NCHFP and Extension materials. No heating sequence, target temperature, duration or homemade-jerky storage permission is provided in this review draft. Drying, parasite control and room-temperature storage are separate determinations; none follows from a shortcut to another.
