## Exact proposed source-bounded article addition — qualified review only

**A notice can change after you first read it**

One public notice is a snapshot. FDA's [public announcements listing](https://www.fda.gov/safety/recalls-market-withdrawals-safety-alerts) contains selected notices, not every FDA-monitored recall; the items are archived from that page after three years. Its [Enforcement Report](https://www.fda.gov/safety/recalls-market-withdrawals-safety-alerts/enforcement-reports) has a different job: it records monitored recalls and may include entries labeled *not yet classified*. A firm can warn customers before an FDA classification and report entry appear. Neither a missing listing nor an unclassified entry is a finding that a product is harmless.

The [FDA explains](https://www.fda.gov/safety/recalls-market-withdrawals-safety-alerts/enforcement-reports) that its record history can show later changes to the classification, reason, product description and identifying codes. Its [definitions](https://www.fda.gov/safety/enforcement-reports/enforcement-report-information-and-definitions) say the distribution pattern can describe only initial distribution, not all later movements. A state absent from that initial list is therefore not independent proof that an item was never sold there. Even *terminated* describes an agency's recall-removal or correction determination; it is not an instruction to eat a package still in your possession.

If you are recording a real notice, preserve its exact URL, the date on the notice and the date you looked. Check for a later update before treating an old note as current. Compare the actual identifying codes and follow the issuing agency's directions, rather than translating database status, search silence or a distribution summary into a household safety verdict.
