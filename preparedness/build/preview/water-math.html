Water math: how much to store for one person, a family and the pets

The official baseline is one gallon per person per day. Here is what that number covers, what pushes it up, where it quietly runs out, and the one figure no official source gives you.

What to do now

  1. Count your people. Everyone who sleeps in your home most nights, including children and anyone who lives with you part of the week.
  2. Multiply by 1 gallon a day. The CDC's baseline is at least 1 gallon of water per person per day, for at least 3 days. It suggests aiming for 2 weeks if you can.[1] FEMA's Ready.gov uses the same figure.[2]
  3. Add on top, don't average down. Store more if anyone is pregnant, sick, nursing, a young child, or if you live somewhere hot.[1][2]
  4. Give pets their own line. Include a bowl and several days of water for each, and ask your veterinarian for the quantity. We could not find an official per-animal figure, so we will not invent one.[3]

Example: a household of four needs 12 gallons for 3 days and 56 gallons for 14 days, before any additions for heat, illness or animals. How to store it safely is covered in the companion guide on two-week water storage.

The number, and where it comes from

Two federal sources give the same starting point. The CDC says to store at least 1 gallon of water per person, per day, for 3 days, and to try for a 2-week supply if possible.[1] Ready.gov puts it as at least one gallon per person per day "for several days, for drinking and sanitation."[2]

Notice the difference in what the gallon is for. The CDC says the water can be used for drinking, cooking, brushing teeth and other uses.[1] Ready.gov says drinking and sanitation.[2] Neither says a gallon is generous. Ready.gov notes that a normally active person needs about three quarters of a gallon of fluid a day from water and other beverages, which leaves about a quarter of a gallon for everything else.[2]

Editor's note That quarter gallon does not stretch to a daily wash, a flush, or dishes. The baseline is a survival floor for drinking and the barest hygiene, and the practical consequence is that the first things to go are comfort and cleanliness, not thirst. A household that wants to keep washing hands properly and cooking from dry food should treat the baseline as a minimum, and extra water as the cheapest upgrade it can make.

Why three days, and why two weeks

The CDC states the three-day figure as the amount to create and store, and the two-week figure as what to try for.[1] It does not say why, and we will not guess at its reasoning. What the numbers allow you to do is plan for two different failures. Three days covers the typical short interruption while services are restored or you are directed elsewhere. Two weeks covers the longer outage where help is slower to arrive. If the plan has to choose, a full three-day supply is better than a half-built two-week one, because it is something a household can actually finish.

Editor's note A gallon of water weighs a little over 8 pounds, so a two-week supply for four people is roughly 450 pounds. That is a floor-loading and stair-climbing question as much as a purchasing one. Decide where it will live before you buy it, and see the storage guide for what makes a container safe.

What pushes the number up

Both sources say the baseline is a starting point. Ready.gov is explicit that individual needs vary with age, health, physical condition, activity, diet and climate.[2] It names several groups and conditions:

  • Children, nursing mothers and sick people may need more water.[2]
  • A medical emergency might require additional water.[2]
  • Warm climates. "In very hot temperatures," Ready.gov says, water needs can double.[2]
  • Pregnancy and illness. The CDC separately lists pregnant women and people who are sick among those who should store more.[1]

Neither source gives a multiplier other than the heat case, and we have no authority to supply one. If you live in a hot place, doubling the baseline is the only quantified adjustment in these two sources, and it is one the household can act on directly: four people in very hot weather for three days is 24 gallons, not 12. For pregnancy, illness, small children, or medical devices that use water, ask the clinician who looks after that person what they need. That answer belongs to a person, not a table.

Pets: the figure that isn't there

The CDC says to consider storing more water for pets.[1] Ready.gov's pet guidance says to keep a water bowl and several days' supply of water in each pet kit, and to keep a lighter evacuation kit separate from the larger shelter-in-place one.[3] Neither gives a quantity per animal.

That gap is real. Needs differ enormously between a cat, a large dog and a horse, and between a hot day and a cool one. The honest advice is to ask your veterinarian for a daily figure for each animal you own, write it down, and add it to the household total. We have deliberately not published a general pet number, and the planner tool on this site leaves that field for you to fill in.

Don't plan to ration

Ready.gov's advice is to never ration drinking water unless authorities tell you to. Drink the amount you need today and try to find more for tomorrow, reduce activity and stay cool to minimize how much water your body needs, and do not substitute caffeinated or carbonated drinks, because caffeine and alcohol dehydrate the body and raise the need for water.[2] Ready.gov also advises drinking water you know is not contaminated first, and treating suspicious water as a later step instead of waiting to become dehydrated.[2]

Editor's note This is the argument for storing a number you can actually drink. A supply sized on the assumption that you will cut back is a supply that fails the first time someone is unwell or the weather turns hot.

Water you may already have in the house

The CDC lists other sources of clean water inside a home if you have no bottled, boiled or treated water:[4]

  • Water from your home's tap water heater tank. The CDC is careful to say this is the tank connected to your faucets and showerheads, not the tank that supplies a home heating system in older houses.
  • Melted ice cubes, if the water they were made from was not contaminated.
  • Water from the toilet tank (never the bowl), if it is clear and has not been treated with toilet cleaners such as those that color the water.
  • Liquid from canned fruit and vegetables.

Pool and spa water can be used for hygiene and cleaning, but not for drinking.[4] The CDC also says that local officials may advise you to shut off the main water valve to your home so contaminants do not enter the pipes, so listen for their instructions before you use any of these.[4]

Editor's note Treat this list as a reserve, not a plan. A water heater tank is typically a few dozen gallons, it is shared among everyone in the home, and some of these sources depend on the nature of the emergency. If the water that filled them was unsafe, they may be unsafe too, which is why the next guide, making water safer in an emergency, matters.

Then: a frigate's allotment

Documented history U.S. Navy regulations of 1802 allowed at least half a gallon a day per man for drinking on foreign voyages. Testimony at the 1814 inquiry into USS Constitution's 1813 cruise records the crew's allotment as 250 gallons a day, 280 on pea and rice days, rising to 310 and 340 once grog water was allowed.[5]

Editor's note Spread over a crew of roughly 450, that is between about half a gallon and three-quarters of a gallon a head. The crew size is the museum's earlier figure and the division is ours, so read it as an order of magnitude.

Now: a household's baseline

Official guidance At least 1 gallon per person per day, for drinking, cooking and brushing teeth, for three days and ideally two weeks.[1]

Sailors were short of water by design, because every gallon took up hold space. A household is short of it by neglect. The modern baseline is above the old allotment, and Ready.gov's warning against rationing reflects the cost of living near the margin.[2]

A worked example

Take a household of four in a hot part of the country: two adults, two children, and a dog. Here is how the guidance above turns into a shopping number, step by step.

  1. Baseline. Four people at 1 gallon a day for 3 days is 12 gallons. For 14 days it is 56 gallons.[1]
  2. Heat. The household lives where summer temperatures are very high. Ready.gov says needs can double in very hot temperatures, so the summer figure is 24 gallons for 3 days and 112 gallons for 14 days.[2]
  3. The dog. The sources give no figure, so the household asks its veterinarian. Suppose the answer is a figure per day; it is multiplied by the days and added on top, and a bowl goes in the kit.[3]
  4. Anyone unwell? If one adult is pregnant or someone takes a medication that needs water, the household asks the clinician for the extra amount and adds it.[1]
  5. Reserve. The water heater, the toilet tank (clear and untreated) and the ice in the freezer count as a reserve, not part of the plan.[4]

The result is a number the household can defend, built from sourced steps, with the two unsourced inputs (the pet and any medical need) supplied by the people best placed to know them. For summer, this household would aim for at least 24 gallons of bottled water to cover the first three days, then add storage toward the larger figure as space and budget allow.

Mistakes that make the number wrong

  • Counting only drinking. The baseline already assumes drinking, cooking and brushing teeth share the gallon.[1] Planning only for thirst leaves nothing for the rest.
  • Forgetting who is in the house. Visitors, a relative who is staying, or a child home from college change the count. Plan for the most people you could have on a bad week.
  • Using the figure as a target to ration toward. Ready.gov's advice against rationing is the reason the baseline should be met in full.[2]
  • Storing it all in one place. If the water is in one location and that location is flooded, blocked or left behind, it does not help. Splitting a supply across two places is a sensible hedge.

What this guide has and has not done

Every figure and adjustment here comes from the CDC, Ready.gov (both its water and pets pages), and the museum study, each read in full on the date shown. The Ready.gov water page is older than the CDC's, last updated in 2021, and the two agree on the baseline. The arithmetic table is ours. We did not verify any official infant-formula, medication or dialysis water requirement, and we say nothing about them. For those, ask the clinician who manages the condition.

This guide gives amounts only. Which containers are safe, how to clean them, and how long stored water lasts are in the storage guide, and what to do when the stored water runs out is in the guide on making water safer.
