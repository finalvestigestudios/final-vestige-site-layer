Salt beef and salt pork at sea: what the records show, and what salt never promised

Contracts, ration tables and sailors' journals show how navies fed crews on barrelled meat for months. They also show what salt could not do, and why none of it is a recipe for your kitchen.

Read this first

This is a history, not a method. Nothing on this page is a recipe, a ratio, or a storage instruction, and it should not be used as one.

  • Home-cured, smoked, salted and fermented foods have caused botulism. You cannot see, smell or taste the toxin.[1]
  • If you want to preserve meat at home, use a procedure published and tested by the USDA or the National Center for Home Food Preservation, and follow it exactly.[2]
  • If you do not know whether a food was safely preserved, the CDC's advice is short: if in doubt, throw it out.[1]

For emergency food that is safe to store, see the Food section of this archive as it grows. Symptoms of botulism can include blurred vision, difficulty swallowing or speaking, and weakness. It is a medical emergency; call your emergency number.

A ship's hold, counted

When USS Constitution was provisioned for a projected six-month cruise in 1813, the numbers recorded in the court of inquiry that later examined her captain run like this: 84,456 pounds of bread, 57,700 pounds of beef, and 50,600 pounds of pork, with about 47,000 gallons of water, for a crew of roughly 450.Documented history[3]

The beef and pork were not fresh and could not be. They were cut, salted, and packed into barrels on shore, loaded, stowed, and left to last. That arrangement is what most people mean by "salt junk," one of the scornful nicknames sailors gave it, along with "salt horse."[3]

What follows is an attempt to say what the records show, without the sea-chantey version, and then to be exact about why that record cannot be turned into advice.

What a sailor was issued

The 1813 weekly ration for an American sailor, as tabulated in the Constitution Museum's study, was built around bread and barrelled meat. Per man, it came to roughly 3½ pounds of beef and 3 pounds of pork a week, plus 98 ounces of bread (14 ounces a day), a pound of flour with half a pound of suet, and smaller amounts of cheese, butter, peas, rice, molasses and vinegar, along with a daily spirit ration. Meat came on six days out of seven. Friday had none.[3]

Two cautions from the same study. First, that table represents the ideal diet, and the author notes it "did not always reflect actual practice": ships on long cruises resupplied from whatever they could buy or capture. Second, the same author estimates, drawing on earlier work, that the menu delivered something like 4,240 calories a day, nearly all of it burned by shipboard labor.[3] The figure is an estimate built on a historian's earlier estimate, and we treat it as the right order of magnitude rather than a measurement.

The contract: how barrelled meat was specified

The most useful single document in the story is not a sailor's complaint but a purchasing order. In December 1812 the Navy's Boston agent, Amos Binney, put out a circular seeking 700 barrels of mess beef and 500 of mess pork. The beef was to come from well-fatted oxen of at least 600 pounds, with the neck, shins, shoulder blades and leg rounds excluded, cut into ten-pound pieces, twenty to a white-oak barrel. The pork was to come from hogs of at least 180 pounds, heads and legs excluded, cut into eight-pound pieces, twenty-five to a barrel.[3]

The document goes on to demand "a sufficient quantity of good Salt and Salt Petre," and that the barrels be branded to the standard set by Massachusetts law.[3]

The piece sizes look arbitrary until you read the mess table. Crews ate in messes of eight to twelve men. A pound of pork a day per man meant an eight-pound piece fed an eight-man mess for a day with no cutting needed. That is the museum historian's reading of why the weights look the way they do; the contract itself does not explain them.Uncertain[3]

What the contract does not say

Editor's note "Sufficient" is the only quantity the circular gives for salt and saltpetre. There is no ratio, no curing time, no brine strength, no storage temperature. That is not an omission we are leaving out for caution; it is what the source says, as quoted in the museum study. Whatever the contractors knew about quantities, it lived in their workshops and not on the page. We do not try to reconstruct it.

Editor's note "Salt Petre" is potassium nitrate. Whether those contractors or the Navy understood why it was in the barrel is not something the sources we read address. The modern explanation, discussed below, came later.

The reputation problem

Salt meat has a terrible name, and much of that comes from sailors who wrote memoirs. Herman Melville, who served in a U.S. frigate in the 1840s, described junks of lean pork "boiled in their own bristles," and the men tearing the hide off as if it were something worse.[3] It is vivid prose, and it is a literary artefact as much as a record.

The Constitution Museum's historian takes a different view. He argues that the evidence suggests salted meat bought for the Navy was of high quality, pointing to the Navy's careful contract terms and to an 1799 letter in which the Secretary of the Navy warned an agent that sailors "used to the best Beef will not eat the common kind." He suggests memoirists may be remembering only the occasional bad or excessively old cask.Uncertain[3]

That is an interpretation, and a reasonable one, but it cannot be tested: no source we have read tallies how many barrels were condemned. The honest position is that the quality of naval salt meat varied, probably more than either the horror stories or the defenders allow, and that nobody has produced the numbers to say by how much.

Steeping, boiling, and the salt that stayed

Salt meat could not be eaten as it came out of the barrel. It was soaked in fresh water, in a steep tub, then boiled in the galley copper. One set of ship's regulations from 1799, quoted in the museum study, required the salt meats to be issued each evening to the cook and soaked in changing water every four hours.[3]

Did it work? The museum's historian doubts it: even soaked overnight, he thinks it unlikely that much of the salt was removed, and notes that some writers recommended towing the meat astern in a net to wash out the brine. Cooking would leach more, but the quantity of salt a sailor swallowed each day "must have been shockingly high."Uncertain[3]

A contemporary doctor, quoted in the same study, noted the side effect: sailors, "in consequence of their salt diet, drink a great quantity of water, unless on an allowance."[3] The meat and the water supply were one system. The salt that kept the food also raised demand for the one thing a ship at sea could least spare. The companion guide on making water safer sets out the modern side of that ledger.

The other half: ship's bread

Ship's bread was an unleavened biscuit of flour and water baked until thoroughly dry. Kept in a dry, ventilated bread room, it could last for years.[3] The word "hardtack," which most readers will reach for here, is not the period term. The museum's study dates it to the 1840s and says it spread during the American Civil War.[3]

A post-war contract for Navy bread, quoted by the study, required wheat flour only (no rye) and that the bread be thoroughly kiln dried before shipment. Surviving British biscuits are about half an inch thick, 4¾ to 5¾ inches wide and weigh about four ounces, which puts a 14-ounce daily ration at three to four pieces.[3] A surviving example is in the National Maritime Museum's collection: a biscuit made to an 1846 recipe, marked with a broad arrow and the letter C, measuring 15 by 135 millimetres.[4]

The infestation stories are partly true. The museum study identifies real pests that could ruin a bag of biscuit (the cadelle beetle, the bread beetle, and weevils), and rats are recorded getting into the bread room of USS Essex on her passage to the Pacific. It also argues that the lurid versions of biscuit "crawling with weevils" are mostly concocted by civilian writers.[3] The Royal Museums Greenwich overview lists storage problems, poor ventilation and vermin among the reasons ship's food deteriorated.[5] A sailor on a round-the-world expedition in 1838 recalled mouldy biscuit full of grubs, but the study cautions that his ship had been at sea for many months with probably infested stores.[3]

What salt did not do

Salt kept meat from rotting. It did not keep sailors from dying of what the diet lacked. The Royal Museums Greenwich is direct: poor diet caused malnutrition and specific diseases such as scurvy, which is caused by a lack of vitamin C.Established fact[5] A chaplain aboard USS Congress described the sailor's meals as bread the hammer could scarcely break and meat "as juiceless and dry as the bones" with all the garden's fresh produce left behind.[3]

No one at the time knew the cause. In 1747, aboard HMS Salisbury, the naval surgeon James Lind watched 80 of 350 men fall to scurvy during a ten-week cruise and ran what is now regarded as an early comparative trial: six treatments across twelve patients. His 1753 Treatise of the Scurvy argued the evidence at length; Smithsonian Libraries holds a digitized copy.[6][7] The scale of the problem is stark in the same scholarship: an account of Anson's circumnavigation, published in 1748, is what first drew Lind to the subject, and at least 380 of a crew of 510 on one of Anson's ships had died of the disease.[6]

The Navy's response was slow. The year after the Treatise appeared, the Sick and Hurt Board rejected a proposal to supply sailors with fruit juice. Lind himself recommended a concentrated "rob" made by heating juice for hours, and the scholarship notes that heat destroys much of the ascorbic acid, so it is unsurprising later observers saw no benefit. The Admiralty did not sanction lemon juice on a generous scale until 1795; the author counts a 42-year lag between Lind's clearly described result and its adoption.[6]

Even the wrong explanations are recorded. In 1813 Captain David Porter ordered his cook not to let the crew use "slush" skimmed from the cask for frying their bread, because he feared that habit caused scurvy.[3] He was acting on the best belief of his day. It was not the cause.

Editor's note This is the part of the story that matters most for a modern reader. A preservation method can succeed at the thing it is designed to do (keep protein edible for months) and fail the people eating it in a way nobody has yet identified. "It worked for sailors" never meant "it was adequate for sailors."

A contrast: the salt on a fishing schooner

The Smithsonian's National Museum of American History preserves the provisions list in the journal of George W. Scott, cook aboard the Gloucester schooner Ocean King on a four-month Grand Banks voyage in 1879. It includes 210 hogsheads of salt for salting the cod, five barrels of beef, one of pork, one of hams, ten of flour, 330 pounds of sugar and 200 pounds of butter.[8] By volume, the salt aboard was not for the crew; it was for the catch, and the crew's own barrelled meat is a footnote beside it. The Smithsonian adds that in the dory-fishing era the cook prepared four or five meals a day.[8] Salt was the voyage's business; the crew's food was ordinary provisions.

Why salt works, and why that is not enough

The National Center for Home Food Preservation describes curing as using salt, and sometimes sugar and seasonings, to draw moisture out of meat, which creates an environment that is hostile to harmful microorganisms.[2] That is the mechanism sailors depended on without naming it.

The USDA adds the other half. Nitrites in cured meat inhibit the growth of C. botulinum spores, and the USDA began authorizing nitrite in meat and poultry products in 1925. Concern was raised in the early 1970s about nitrite forming carcinogenic nitrosamines, and processors may use ascorbic acid or related compounds to counter it.[9] All of that explanation is modern. The sailors' saltpetre arrived long before the reason for it did.

Editor's note That history cuts both ways. It helps explain why a barrel of properly salted and nitrate-cured meat could last. It also shows why you cannot copy the method from a history: the protective part depends on quantities, times and temperatures that the contract never states, and that the sources we read do not supply.

Current safety verdict

Not validated for modern household use

Do not reproduce this method at home. Salt alone does not guarantee that meat is safe from botulism, and nothing on this page gives a cure formula, time, or temperature, by design.

  • The CDC lists improperly preserved foods among the most common sources of botulism, says you cannot see, smell or taste the toxin, and says to throw out food you cannot vouch for.[1]
  • USDA's food-safety service records many botulism cases following home-canned, preserved, cured, smoked or fermented foods contaminated with toxin, and points consumers to the National Center for Home Food Preservation.[10]
  • FSIS lists traditionally prepared salted or fermented fish among foods linked to recent botulism illness.[9]
  • For low-acid foods, including meat, the CDC says pressure canning is the only recommended home canning method.[1]

We found no account of botulism aboard ship in the sources we read. That is not evidence it didn't happen, or that the method was safe: these records were written to account for rations and morale, not to count illnesses.

Then: the barrel

Documented history Cut, salted with salt and saltpetre, packed in oak, branded, stored in the hold. Soaked, boiled, eaten with bread. Fresh meat came in port and from prizes. The diet lacked fresh produce and the crews paid for it.[3]

Now: the inspected supply

Official guidance Commercial cured meats use nitrite that USDA has authorized since 1925.[9] Households who want to preserve meat should use a method published and tested by the NCHFP or a Cooperative Extension service.[2] If you do not know that a food was safely made, discard it.[1]

What we have not done

This piece leans on one long secondary study, the USS Constitution Museum's, because it brings together contracts, regulations and journals that sit in archives we have not yet visited. Its footnotes point to the National Archives (RG 45 contracts, the Fourth Auditor's accounts), to naval regulations, and to published journals. We have not pulled those records individually, and until we do, every claim attributed to them is reported as the museum study reports it.

The study covers the U.S. Navy from 1794 to 1820. The Royal Navy's victualling system had its own scales and its own bakeries, and earlier centuries differ again. The Royal Museums Greenwich page we cite gives no quantities. The 1753 Treatise is cited as a primary source through its digitized record; we read the library's catalogue entry, not its 456 pages. We have no image of a barrel, a ration tub or a biscuit that we have the rights to publish, so this page has none.
