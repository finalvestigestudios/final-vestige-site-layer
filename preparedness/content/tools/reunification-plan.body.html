What this does

  1. Fill in what you know. Names, who to call, where to meet, who needs extra help. Skip anything that does not apply.
  2. Print your plan and a wallet card for each person, or download it as a text file you can keep on your own device.
  3. Put the practice dates on a calendar, and share the plan with the people named in it.

Private by design. Everything you type stays in this page, in memory. It is not sent anywhere, and it is not saved in cookies, browser storage or the address bar. Closing or reloading the page clears it. Printing or downloading is the only way to keep a copy. If you are using a shared device, clear the page before you leave.

  • Official guidance The prompts follow Ready.gov, FEMA and the CDC pages listed under "Where the prompts come from."
  • Editor's note Suggested practice dates, the printed layout and the wording of prompts are ours.

If anyone is in danger, call 911 or your local emergency number. This tool organizes decisions you make; it does not tell you what to do in a particular emergency, and a completed plan does not mean your household is ready for any event. Follow local officials.

Where the prompts come from

  • The five questions and the household factors (alerts, shelter, evacuation route, communication, kit; ages, languages, medical needs, disabilities, pets, school-aged children) are Ready.gov's.{{cite:start-ready-plan}}
  • An out-of-area contact reachable two ways, a wallet or digital copy for everyone, meeting places in different directions, asking schools about reunification, and practicing the plan like a fire drill are in FEMA's "Are You Ready?" guide (2020).{{cite:ready-are-you-ready}}
  • Two meeting places, several ways to communicate, and microchipping pets are in the CDC's Prepare Your Health guidance, which also notes that text messages often get through when calls do not.{{cite:start-cdc-pyh-connected}}
  • A contact list of the people who support your health, who check on you, and annual practice and review come from the CDC's planning page for people with disabilities.{{cite:start-cdc-disability-plan}}
  • Choosing a pet-friendly place ahead of time is Ready.gov's advice, because most public shelters allow only service animals.{{cite:start-ready-evacuation}}
  • The medicine and equipment questions follow the CDC and Ready.gov advice to talk to your doctor or pharmacist about an emergency supply and to your medical provider about a power-outage plan. The tool gives no instruction about any medicine.{{cite:start-cdc-pyh-prescriptions}}{{cite:start-ready-power}}

For the reasoning behind each step, read {{link:household-emergency-plan|the household emergency plan guide}}. What this tool has and has not done

It stores nothing and sends nothing, and it checks only that phone numbers and dates look like phone numbers and dates; it cannot tell whether a number works. It does not verify that a meeting place is safe or accessible, that a school's policy is what you wrote, or that a medicine can be kept as you plan. Printing from a browser includes whatever your browser adds, such as headers and footers. Wallet cards print at roughly credit-card size and may need trimming. This tool is a draft awaiting human safety review.
