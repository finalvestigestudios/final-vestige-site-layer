What to do now

  1. Fill in what you know. Blanks are fine. The point is to talk it through with everyone who lives in the home.
  2. Mark two ways out of every room, choose one meeting place outside in front of the home, and write down who helps whom, with a backup.{{cite:usfa-fire-escape}}{{cite:shelter-nfpa-escape}}
  3. Print it or save it as a PDF, draw your floor plan by hand on the grid, and put the page where everyone can see it.
  4. Practice with everyone at least twice a year, as a simulation only.{{cite:shelter-nfpa-escape}}

If there is a fire right now, get out, stay out and call 911 from outside. Private by design: what you type stays on this page. It is not sent anywhere and is not saved. How it works, and what it will not do

The page is a form with a few small helpers. It does not score your plan or judge whether it is safe. It points out blanks against what the agencies ask for: two ways out of every room, a meeting place in front of the home, a helper and a backup for anyone who needs help, practice at least twice a year, and a smoke alarm tested monthly and replaced 10 years after its manufacture date.{{cite:usfa-fire-escape}}{{cite:shelter-nfpa-escape}}{{cite:shelter-nfpa-smoke-alarms}} Its fields follow USFA's printable escape grid, which asks for the fire department's number, a map showing all doors and windows, two ways out of each room, a drawn meeting place, and practice twice a year.{{cite:shelter-usfa-escape-grid}}

The floor plan is a printed grid you draw on by hand, with the rooms table as the text alternative. We chose that over a drawing canvas because a hand drawing needs no software, works with a pencil, a screen reader user can complete the table, and nothing about your home layout has to be stored or sent anywhere.

Practice as a simulation only

The sources ask you to practice twice a year, to practice finding your way out in the dark, and to find out whether children and others wake to the alarm.{{cite:shelter-nfpa-escape}}{{cite:shelter-ready-home-fires}} None of them asks for real smoke, flame or heat, and this page does not either. Do not switch off power, gas or any medical or safety equipment as part of a drill, do not block or lock any exit to test it, and clear tripping hazards before a dark or eyes-closed run.

Editor's note If someone in your home uses stairs differently, or lives on an upper floor of a tall building, the plan above is only the start. The building's own plan and your fire department's advice come first. In a high-rise, USFA and NFPA make leaving by the stairs the default, with sealing yourself in reserved for people who cannot get out.{{cite:shelter-nfpa-high-rise}}{{cite:shelter-usfa-high-rise-handout}} For the full reasoning, read {{link:home-fire-escape-plan|Two ways out: home fire planning}}. Privacy and printing

Nothing you type is sent, stored in cookies or browser storage, or kept after you close the page. Printing is the only way to keep a copy. Choose "Save as PDF" in your browser's print dialog if you want a file. A worksheet that lists who needs help getting out is personal; keep the printed copy at home, and think before posting a photo of it.

A human safety review is required before this tool is public, and the box at the bottom of the page shows where that stands.
