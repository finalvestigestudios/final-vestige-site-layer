What to do now

  1. Wash your hands with soap and water for at least 20 seconds, especially after using the toilet, before preparing or eating food, and after caring for anyone who is sick. No running water? Make a handwashing station from a large water jug.{{cite:cdc-hygiene-emergency}}
  2. If water or sewer service is in doubt, stop flushing and ask. Local authorities can tell you whether toilets are safe to use. Meanwhile, flush as little as possible or switch to a temporary toilet.{{cite:health-cdc-septic}}{{cite:health-osu-sanitation}}
  3. Set up a bag-lined toilet before you need it. A bucket or your own toilet bowl, a sturdy bag, something absorbent, and a lid. The Washington State Department of Health and Oregon State University Extension both describe how, and the steps are below.{{cite:health-wadoh-toilet}}{{cite:health-osu-sanitation}}
  4. Keep cuts clean and covered, and stay out of floodwater. Open wounds exposed to contaminated water can become infected. Seek immediate medical care if a wound develops redness, swelling or oozing.{{cite:cdc-hygiene-emergency}}
  5. Use bottled, boiled or treated water for drinking, cooking and personal hygiene when your tap water is suspect, and follow your health department's advice over ours.{{cite:health-cdc-power-outage}}

If someone is seriously ill, severely dehydrated, or injured, call your emergency number or a clinician. This page is about prevention, not treatment.

Why sanitation is part of the plan, not an afterthought

Most emergency lists begin with water and food. Toilets and handwashing rarely make the list, and Oregon State University Extension has a name for that: "toilet blindness." Its guide observes that snowstorms, fires and epidemics usually leave sanitation systems intact, so households forget what an earthquake or flood can do to them.{{cite:health-osu-sanitation}}

The reason it matters is plain. Without a safe way to dispose of human waste, the guide warns, illnesses that spread by the fecal-oral route, such as cholera and salmonella, can follow, and flies, pests and rodents can carry germs from waste to food and open water.{{cite:health-osu-sanitation}} The CDC puts it in two sentences: good personal hygiene and handwashing are critical to preventing the spread of disease, and they matter more in an emergency, when finding clean, safe water can be difficult.{{cite:cdc-hygiene-emergency}}

Editor's note That is why this guide sits beside the water guides rather than apart from them. Washing, flushing and bathing all draw on the same water you are trying to ration. For how much to store, see {{link:water-math|how much water a household needs}} and {{link:two-week-water-storage|storing two weeks of water}}. For what an advisory means for washing and bathing, see {{link:water-advisories-explained|what boil-water and do-not-use advisories mean}}. FEMA's kit page counts the one gallon per person per day it recommends as water for drinking and sanitation both.{{cite:health-ready-kit}}

Handwashing, with and without a tap

The CDC's advice is to wash with soap and water for at least 20 seconds, and to pay particular attention at the key times when you are most likely to spread germs.{{cite:cdc-hygiene-emergency}} Oregon State's guide reproduces the CDC's list of key times: before, during and after preparing food; before and after eating; before and after caring for someone with vomiting or diarrhea; before and after treating a cut or wound; after using the toilet; after changing diapers or cleaning up a child or adult who has used the toilet; after blowing your nose, coughing or sneezing; after touching an animal, animal feed, animal waste, pet food or pet treats; and after touching garbage.{{cite:health-osu-sanitation}}

Two points about the water itself. The CDC says to wash with soap and water that has been boiled or disinfected if local authorities or your doctor recommend extra caution. And during a boil-water or do-not-drink advisory, it says, it may be safe to wash hands with tap water.{{cite:cdc-hygiene-emergency}} The details differ by advisory type, so check your own notice and our {{link:water-advisories-explained|advisories guide}}.

Building a handwashing station

OSU's design is simple enough to keep in a closet. Place a water jug with a spigot over a catch basin set in an existing sink, or over a bucket on a table outdoors. Tie a jug with a handle to a branch if you have to. Put soap and towels within reach (a trick from the guide: bar soap in the toe of a tube sock or nylon stocking, tied to the handle). If you can, set up two stations: one near where food is prepared and one next to the emergency toilet, so people wash after using it.{{cite:health-osu-sanitation}}

Keep it clean. The guide says to sanitize the spigot handle regularly to avoid cross-contamination, to use a disposable paper towel to turn the spigot off if possible, and to replace cloth towels daily. It also says not to treat the water in the catch basin for reuse; water that has washed hands can, however, be used to dilute urine before dumping it outside.{{cite:health-osu-sanitation}}

What sanitizer is and isn't

Official guidance The CDC says alcohol-based sanitizer can quickly reduce the number of germs on hands in some situations, but that it does not eliminate all types of germs, might not remove harmful chemicals, and works less well when hands are visibly dirty or greasy.{{cite:cdc-hygiene-emergency}} OSU adds that many people do not use enough, and that sanitizer does not remove chemicals such as heavy metals or pesticides that may be in soil, air and water after a disaster.{{cite:health-osu-sanitation}}

Editor's note After using a bucket toilet or handling waste, hands are the dirty case. Soap and water is the right tool. Keep sanitizer for the in-between moments.

Toilets when water or sewage fail

First decide whether you can flush at all

A working toilet is not proof that the sewer is working. OSU's guide says to avoid using toilets even if your home sewer lines are undamaged, because pipes in the street may be broken, and to check with local authorities, who may be able to tell you through emergency radio or community message boards whether using your toilet is safe. It also suggests turning off your home's water supply to avoid contamination from broken pipes and to prevent leaks, and turning it back on once the pipes are known to be intact.{{cite:health-osu-sanitation}} With a septic system, the guide says you may be able to keep using it if you see no leaking or backing up into the house.{{cite:health-osu-sanitation}}

The CDC's septic page, written for floods, says to reduce water use during an emergency by limiting toilet flushing, dishwashing, laundry and showers, to flush as little as possible or use a temporary toilet, and not to pump a flooded septic tank more than halfway, or it may float out of the ground. It lists the signs of a failing system (slow sinks and toilets, overflowing floor drains, visible sewage outside) and says to get a damaged system professionally inspected.{{cite:health-cdc-septic}} It also warns to avoid contact with any floodwater that may contain sewage.{{cite:health-cdc-septic}}

The bag-lined toilet

The Washington State Department of Health gives three options when a toilet will not flush. First, use someone else's working toilet (a neighbor, friend, relative or local business). Second, modify your own toilet. Third, use a five-gallon bucket, with a toilet seat if you have one, set up the same way.{{cite:health-wadoh-toilet}} The steps for the second option are:

  1. Turn off the water valve to the toilet and flush out any water still in the bowl.
  2. Line the bowl, under the seat, with a plastic kitchen garbage bag inside a second one.
  3. Put something absorbent that also reduces odor, such as kitty litter, peat moss or lime, into the inner bag.
  4. Use the inner bag. You may be able to use the same bag more than once; when it is full, or you feel it needs replacing, tie it up, insert a new inner bag and add more absorbent.
  5. Store used bags away from children, pets and wildlife.
  6. Wash your hands with soap and running water, or use wet wipes or sanitizer if there is none.

All six steps are from the department's page.{{cite:health-wadoh-toilet}} Uncertain The page does not name a bag thickness, an amount of absorbent or a limit on how long a bag can sit, and we will not supply one.

The twin-bucket toilet

Oregon State prefers a "no-mix" design, two five-gallon buckets, one for urine and one for feces, and says agencies like it because it is safe, easy to manage and inexpensive. Keeping urine apart reduces volume and odor, because urine has volume and smell but is generally sterile, while feces carries the pathogens and, covered with carbon material, dries and shrinks.{{cite:health-osu-sanitation}}

  • Set-up. Mark the buckets "pee" and "poo." Leave the pee bucket unlined. Line the poo bucket with a heavy-duty 13-gallon bag, the kind sold as contractor bags, 3 to 5 mil thick. Add a seat, or a pool noodle split down the middle for padding.
  • Use. Try not to urinate in the poo bucket; the guide says that matters because urine is what produces the strong smell. Put toilet paper in the poo bag or in a separate bag, not in the urine bucket. After using the poo bucket, sprinkle about a half cup of carbon material (wood shavings, sawdust, shredded paper, bark chips or peat moss) so it covers the surface. Keep the seat down but not airtight, so the waste can dry.
  • Emptying. When the urine bucket is about one-quarter full or before it gets too heavy to carry, add water if you can to dilute it and pour it on the ground, changing the spot each time. Fill the poo bucket no more than half full. Then put on disposable gloves, double-bag the waste, seal it tightly, and store it far from food and water, secured against pets, flies and rats, and reline the bucket.

Each of those points is OSU's.{{cite:health-osu-sanitation}} The guide's supply list adds closable bags for used toilet paper, a privacy screen if the system will not be in an existing bathroom, and latex or nitrile gloves for bagging waste.{{cite:health-osu-sanitation}}

Where do the bags go? Two state-level sources disagree

Uncertain The Washington State Department of Health suggests placing used bags in the garbage can, for example in the garage, for disposal.{{cite:health-wadoh-toilet}} Oregon State's guide says not to put human waste into the normal garbage, because it has to be specially treated and should not go in a landfill with other household waste, and that you will likely have to store it until the public sanitary system is repaired.{{cite:health-osu-sanitation}} It also says to check with local officials for recommendations on disposing of human waste.{{cite:health-osu-sanitation}}

Editor's note We cannot settle this and we are not the right authority to. Disposal rules depend on your local waste and sewer authority and on how long the emergency lasts. Treat the disagreement as a prompt to ask your health department or waste provider now, not in the middle of a storm. This conflict is flagged for the safety reviewer.

The hole in the ground

If none of the above is possible and you will be without a toilet for only a day or two, Washington allows a "cat hole": dig on dry land at least 100 feet from wells, homes and open water, avoiding slopes that could erode in rain; make it at least 6 inches deep and about 6 inches across; cover and pack it down after use; mark or remember where it is. Toilet paper may go in the hole; wet wipes, tampons and other less biodegradable items should be bagged and put in the garbage. Each hole is for one use.{{cite:health-wadoh-toilet}} For waste you bury more deliberately, OSU's rule is not to bury it within 100 feet of a water source or where contaminants can leach downhill into one.{{cite:health-osu-sanitation}}

Editor's note For apartment dwellers and anyone without ground to dig in, the hole is not an option, and the sources do not offer a different outdoor method. That is the strongest argument for owning the bucket and bags in advance.

Bathing, teeth, wounds, diapers

  • Bathing. Follow local guidance on whether the water is safe. Water that is not safe to drink can sometimes be used for bathing, if you are careful not to swallow it or get it in your eyes. Do not bathe in water that may contain sewage or toxic chemicals, or in rivers, streams or lakes contaminated by floodwater, human sewage or animal waste. If you have a private well and have flooding or suspect contamination, contact your local, state or tribal health department about testing and disinfection.{{cite:cdc-hygiene-emergency}}
  • Teeth. Use only clean water to brush your teeth after a water-related emergency, and listen to local authorities about whether tap water is safe.{{cite:cdc-hygiene-emergency}}
  • Wounds. Keep open wounds as clean as possible by washing well with soap and clean water and covering clean wounds with a waterproof bandage. Avoid flood, salt or brackish water if you have an open wound; the CDC notes that naturally occurring bacteria such as Vibrio in certain coastal waters can cause skin infection.{{cite:cdc-hygiene-emergency}}
  • Diapers. The CDC notes that even a microscopic amount of stool can contain millions of germs, and publishes separate fact sheets and checklists on diapering in emergencies, including for shelters.{{cite:cdc-hygiene-emergency}} We did not read those fact sheets and do not summarize them. OSU suggests keeping an extra supply of disposable diapers in the kit, rotating them as the child grows, and bagging soiled diapers in heavy-duty 3-to-5-mil bags with the bagged waste.{{cite:health-osu-sanitation}}
  • Menstrual products. OSU recommends an extra supply in the kit, and says to bag soiled products in the same heavy-duty bags and dispose of them with the bagged waste; it also suggests getting comfortable with reusable menstrual cups before an emergency.{{cite:health-osu-sanitation}}

Different households, different problems

  • Older adults and people with mobility or balance limits. OSU suggests placing buckets where sturdy chairs, railings or other supports help with getting on and off, and elevating the buckets with scrap wood or bricks. The CDC's archived comparison also warns that bucket latrines can be difficult for disabled, elderly and small children to use.{{cite:health-osu-sanitation}}{{cite:health-cdc-sanitation-archive}}
  • Toddlers. OSU says a potty-training child who prefers a small toilet may keep using it, and its contents can go into the household's buckets.{{cite:health-osu-sanitation}}
  • Renters and apartment dwellers. The twin-bucket and bag systems work in an existing bathroom, and OSU says doing so adds privacy and normalcy.{{cite:health-osu-sanitation}} Editor's note The sources assume somewhere to pour diluted urine and, for a rural household, somewhere to bury waste. If you live in a building, ask your landlord or local authority what to do with waste during a prolonged outage, and decide before you need to.
  • Well and septic households. The CDC's septic page covers sealing manholes before a flood, keeping the tank at least half full, and turning off electric pumps at the circuit box before flooding. It says not to drink well water until it is known to be safe.{{cite:health-cdc-septic}}
  • Anyone with a weak immune system or a chronic illness. Uncertain None of the pages we read gives additional sanitation steps for them. Ask your clinician. See also {{link:medication-and-devices|managing medication and medical devices}}.

What this guide has and hasn't done

Every instruction here comes from the CDC, Washington State Department of Health and Oregon State University Extension pages cited, each read in full on the date shown, with FEMA's kit page for the supply list. We have not tested any toilet or handwashing design. We do not give a chemical quantity: none of the pages we read provides a bleach or disinfectant ratio for sanitizing buckets or surfaces, so there is none here. For sanitizing water containers, the CDC's steps are in {{link:two-week-water-storage|our storage guide}}.

We did not reproduce the CDC's five-step handwashing instruction, its diapering fact sheets, the Regional Disaster Preparedness Organization's emergency toilet guidebook that OSU recommends, or anything about building a latrine. Where sources differ on waste disposal, we say so above. The archived CDC page is for emergency response, not for a household, and is cited only for general principles.
