What to do now

  1. Pick one contact who lives outside your area and make sure every family member knows who it is and how to reach them. The FCC's advice is to designate someone out of the area as a central contact and to make certain all family members know who to contact if they become separated.{{cite:shelter-fcc-comms}}
  2. Write the plan down. Ready.gov's family emergency communication plan collects names, phone numbers, schools, childcare, workplaces, emergency contacts, meeting places and medical information on one form.{{cite:shelter-ready-plan-form}}
  3. Agree on more than one meeting place, familiar and easy to find, and agree when you would use each.{{cite:shelter-ready-plan}}{{cite:shelter-cdc-reunification}}
  4. Put an emergency contact card in each child's school bag, and help older children memorize the important names and numbers.{{cite:shelter-cdc-reunification}}
  5. Ask your child's school or childcare how it would reunite children with families, and find out where and how it will notify you.{{cite:shelter-ready-plan-locations}}{{cite:shelter-co-oss-reunification}}
  6. Practice the plan with everyone in the household.{{cite:shelter-ready-plan}}

In a real emergency, call 911 for anything life-threatening and keep other phone calls brief so networks stay open for those who need them.{{cite:shelter-fcc-comms}} This page is about reconnecting, not about first aid or rescue.

The problem a communication plan solves

Ready.gov's planning page begins from a plain fact: your family may not be together if a disaster strikes, so it is important to know how you will contact one another and reconnect if separated.{{cite:shelter-ready-plan}} The CDC puts a number on how often that is true for children: an estimated 69 million children in the United States are in school or childcare on any given weekday, and emergencies increase the possibility of children being separated from their parents. During an evacuation or sheltering, parents may be at work while their children are in childcare, school or a recreational facility, and children can be separated from caregivers even when the whole family is together.{{cite:shelter-cdc-reunification}}

The CDC also notes that separation can have mental and physical effects on children, and that the faster children reunite with the people they know and love, the better their outcomes will be.{{cite:shelter-cdc-reunification}}

There is a second problem, which is the phone network itself. The FCC explains that during an emergency many more people than usual are trying to use their wireless and wireline telephones at the same time, which can create network congestion.{{cite:shelter-fcc-comms}} A family that plans to "just call each other" is relying on the thing most likely to jam.

Step 1: The out-of-area contact

The FCC's guide recommends that you designate someone out of the area as a central contact, and make sure all family members know who to contact if they become separated.{{cite:shelter-fcc-comms}} Ready.gov's evacuation page assumes the same arrangement: if time allows, call or email the out-of-state contact in your family communications plan and tell them where you are going.{{cite:shelter-ready-evacuation}} The CDC's chemical-emergency plan says it in plainer words: let a friend or relative outside your area know where you are, and expect to send messages by text or social media.{{cite:shelter-cdc-chemical-sip}}

Editor's note The sources recommend the practice but do not explain why it works. Here is our reasoning. A disaster usually affects one region at a time, so a person far away is less likely to be affected by it, and if everyone in the family reports to that person instead of trying to reach one another, each of you needs only one successful message, not a chain of them. If you live in a place where "the region" is large, as in a hurricane, "out of the area" should mean far enough away that the same event will not reach them. How far is a judgment call, and no source we read gives a distance.

Practical points that follow from the sources and from common sense:

  • Choose someone who is reachable and willing, and ask them first. Make sure they know the plan and what to do with messages. Editor's note That is our suggestion.
  • Have a backup contact, since one person may be unreachable. Editor's note Also ours.
  • Everyone, including children, should know how to reach the contact without the phone's address book. The CDC suggests helping older children memorize important names and numbers (parents and another trusted adult), and giving children contact cards to carry in the school bag.{{cite:shelter-cdc-reunification}}
  • Keep the FCC's practical tips in mind: keep a list of emergency numbers in your phone, have charged batteries and a car-charger adapter for backup power, and keep at least one corded telephone that does not depend on electricity, since cordless phones usually do not work in an outage.{{cite:shelter-fcc-comms}}

The page says the information entered is not saved, stored or shared by any government agency.{{cite:shelter-ready-plan-form}} The fields show what the agency thinks belongs in the plan:

Editor's note Keep it on paper, too. A plan that lives only on a phone is lost when the phone is dead, lost or locked. Print or write the plan, keep a copy in a go-bag and one at home, and give a copy to the out-of-area contact. Consider what you put on a card children carry: names and numbers, but not your home address if it would help a stranger more than it would help a responder. This is our caution, not an agency's.

Step 3: Meeting places

Ready.gov asks for a family meeting place that is "familiar and easy to find."{{cite:shelter-ready-plan}} The CDC's description of a family plan includes "multiple meeting places," and its family checklist begins with choosing a meeting spot.{{cite:shelter-cdc-reunification}} Ready.gov's evacuation page adds that the meeting place should be updated depending on the circumstance.{{cite:shelter-ready-evacuation}}

Editor's note A workable structure, which is our suggestion, is three levels, each used in a different situation:

  1. Right outside the home, for a fire. This is the same spot named in your {{link:home-fire-escape-plan|home fire escape plan}}.
  2. Somewhere in the neighborhood, for a problem that makes the home unsafe or unreachable but leaves the area passable: a neighbor's house, a library, a school.
  3. Outside the area, for a disaster that makes the whole area unreachable: the home of a relative or friend, ideally one that is the same place as your {{link:shelter-or-evacuate|evacuation destination}}.

Whatever you choose, write it down, put the address and a phone number for each, and tell everyone including children. If the plan is to move between places, say who decides and by what time.

Step 4: Using phones and networks when everything is busy

Ready.gov's Get Tech Ready page (updated March 2026) is the current statement: mobile networks can become overwhelmed in emergencies, making calls hard to place and receive, and text messages require less bandwidth, so they can be transmitted more reliably when many people are using their phones. It also suggests social media and email, a group chat or thread for family, keeping your contacts updated and synced across phone, email and social media, an emergency charging option at home and in the car, and low-power or airplane mode to save battery.{{cite:shelter-ready-tech}} The FCC's older guide adds more detail. Its recommended practices for all users are, in summary:{{cite:shelter-fcc-comms}}

  • Limit non-emergency calls to reduce congestion and save battery. Keep calls brief and use them only to convey vital information.
  • Try text messaging, also called SMS. "In many cases text messages will go through when your call may not," and texting frees up space on the voice network.
  • If one service fails, try another: text, email, or a landline if you have one. Spreading demand over several networks should reduce overall congestion.
  • Wait 10 seconds before redialing, since redialing too quickly can contribute to a clogged network.
  • If you are in a vehicle, place calls while it is stationary.
  • If you have call forwarding on your home number, forward it to your mobile before an evacuation so incoming calls reach you.

Ready.gov's tornado, hurricane and wildfire pages give the same advice for the aftermath: save phone calls for emergencies and use text messaging or social media to reach family and friends, since phone systems are often down or busy.{{cite:shelter-ready-tornadoes}}{{cite:shelter-ready-hurricanes}}{{cite:shelter-ready-wildfires}} The CDC adds that children who use social media can use features in the apps to let loved ones know they are safe.{{cite:shelter-cdc-reunification}}

Uncertain The FCC page is undated, and its section for people with disabilities mentions pagers and personal digital assistants, so parts of it are plainly old. We used it only for general network-congestion points. The texting advice and the out-of-area contact are now also supported by Ready.gov's March 2026 page, Ready.gov's evacuation page and the CDC's 2026 chemical-emergency page. The FCC-only details (waiting 10 seconds before redialing, forwarding a home number, keeping a corded phone) should be treated as older guidance that we have not seen repeated by a current source. We also have no source that says which channel (text, messaging app, social media, email) is most reliable in a given disaster; this varies with the event and the carrier. The plan should name two or three ways to reach one another and be tested.

Step 5: School, childcare and reunification

This is the part of family communication that causes the most anxiety, and also the part where the details are most local. Ready.gov says to plan for the places you go regularly, including school and daycare, and to find out what plans are available at each. It lists the information to ask for: the local alerts or warnings available, building alarm or alert systems, evacuation plans including alternate exits, shelter-in-place plans, and the supplies you would need for temporary sheltering. It adds that buildings like schools have different safety plans depending on how they are built and what kind of emergency is happening.{{cite:shelter-ready-plan-locations}} Ready.gov's form has a field for each school or childcare provider's emergency or hotline number, website and "Emergency Plan/Pick-Up."{{cite:shelter-ready-plan-form}}

The CDC defines reunification as the process of ensuring that children return to the care of their parents and family as quickly as possible after an emergency. School and childcare centers have their own plans to do this. The CDC suggests parents learn about school safety drills from the principal and safety officials, and notes that lockdown drills vary by state.{{cite:shelter-cdc-reunification}}

A state example shows what a school plan can look like. Colorado's Office of School Safety describes reunification as "not a normal pick-up" but a slow and careful process meant to make sure no student is lost or given to the wrong person. Its parent guidance is: stay home and do not rush to the school (you will not get your child sooner by arriving early); trust the school or district notification system for the time and location of reunification; when told to move, bring photo ID and a pen; and limit calls and texts to keep networks open for emergency use.{{cite:shelter-co-oss-reunification}}

The sources point slightly different ways

The CDC says the faster children are reunited with family, the better their outcomes.{{cite:shelter-cdc-reunification}} Colorado's guidance tells parents not to rush to the school, because arriving early will not get a child back sooner, and staying away keeps roads clear for fire and police vehicles.{{cite:shelter-co-oss-reunification}} The FCC says to try texting; Colorado says to limit calls and texts so that teachers and police can use the networks.{{cite:shelter-fcc-comms}}{{cite:shelter-co-oss-reunification}} Editor's note These are compatible once you see that they apply at different moments. The CDC states a goal for planners; the Colorado page, which is state-specific, describes one school-safety office's procedure for parents during an active school emergency. Your own school or district may handle this differently, and its plan, not ours, governs. Ask in advance, and keep what you learn in your family plan.

If a child is missing after a disaster, the CDC lists the National Center for Missing and Exploited Children's 24-hour hotline, 1-800-THE-LOST.{{cite:shelter-cdc-reunification}} NCMEC's own site lists its 24-hour hotline and call center as 1-800-843-5678, which is the same number written with digits, and says to contact NCMEC if you think you have seen a missing child.{{cite:shelter-ncmec-hotline}}

FEMA's reunification fact sheet explains the national picture. FEMA, the Red Cross and NCMEC co-lead a National Reunification Committee. After a Presidentially declared disaster, and at a state, tribal or territorial request, NCMEC can activate a National Emergency Child Locator Center call center to help identify and locate children separated from parents or guardians; NCMEC's Unaccompanied Minors Registry takes reports about a missing child 24 hours a day; and the Red Cross takes welfare-check requests at 1-800-RED CROSS, including for someone inside the affected area who has a serious health condition or a functional or access need. FEMA also says it works to address the communication access needs of people who are deaf or hard of hearing, people without speech, people with cognitive or intellectual disabilities, and people with limited English proficiency.{{cite:shelter-fema-reunification}} Editor's note The fact sheet is written for emergency planners, not households, and FEMA's site marks it "Archived Content" that may not reflect current policy or programs, so we do not describe these as services you can count on in a particular event. They are why a written plan with the out-of-area contact comes first.

Teaching children their part

Ready.gov's summer-break list for families is a useful short curriculum: make a family communication plan that includes the whole family; practice evacuation plans and other emergency procedures with children regularly; teach children when and how to call 911 and other important numbers; make sure they have an emergency contact person and know how to reach them; decide on a meeting place if you are separated; and create a family password or phrase so a child does not go with a stranger.{{cite:shelter-ready-heat}} The CDC asks you to include children in making the plan.{{cite:shelter-cdc-reunification}}

Editor's note Keep it undramatic. A child who is told "this is what we do, and here is the card" is calmer than a child who has been warned about everything that could happen. Practice by asking "what would we do if..." at dinner, and let the child answer. Children in their teens, who may have phones and independent routines, need their own part of the plan: who they report to, where they go if school is closed, and what they do if they cannot reach a parent. We have not read the CDC's separate teen-preparedness page and make no claims from it.

Adults and others with particular communication needs

  • People with communication disabilities. Ready.gov's disability page suggests carrying printed cards or storing information on a device to inform first responders and others how to communicate with you; people who use speech-generating or augmentative devices should plan how to communicate if the device fails, for instance with laminated cards of phrases or pictograms.{{cite:shelter-ready-disabilities}}
  • People who are deaf or hard of hearing. The same page lists a weather radio with a text display and flashing alert, extra hearing-aid batteries, pen and paper, and a battery-operated lantern to allow signing or lip reading in the dark.{{cite:shelter-ready-disabilities}}
  • People who rely on a caregiver or support network, including older adults. Ready.gov says to create a support network, keep its contact list in a watertight container in your kit or on devices, and tell them where your supplies are.{{cite:shelter-ready-disabilities}} Decide in advance who will check on an older or disabled family member or neighbor and how, since Ready.gov's building guidance asks neighbors to decide who will check on elderly or disabled neighbors.{{cite:shelter-ready-plan-locations}} The FCC adds that if you have a personal care attendant, you should decide with them how you will communicate if separated.{{cite:shelter-fcc-comms}}
  • Households whose members speak different languages. Ready.gov's checklist includes languages spoken; its fillable communication card and several other materials are offered in additional languages, though we did not open them.{{cite:shelter-ready-plan}}
  • Neighbors, condominiums and apartments. Ready.gov suggests talking to neighbors about working together in an emergency, finding out who has equipment like a generator or expertise such as medical knowledge, deciding who will check on elderly or disabled neighbors, and making backup plans for children in case you cannot get home.{{cite:shelter-ready-plan-locations}}
  • Pets and service animals. Ready.gov's planning list includes them. Many public shelters allow only service animals, so a family plan should say where pets would go.{{cite:shelter-ready-plan}}{{cite:shelter-ready-shelter}}

Editor's note Households with more than one home, shared custody, or informal caregivers are not addressed by the sources we read. The plan should list every adult who might legitimately collect a child and every adult the school has been told about, and your school's own authorization rules will control who may take a child. We make no legal claims.

Step 6: Practice, and keep it current

Ready.gov's final step is to practice the plan with your household.{{cite:shelter-ready-plan}} Its evacuation page suggests updating the meeting place depending on the circumstance.{{cite:shelter-ready-evacuation}} Here is a short, low-effort routine, offered as our suggestion rather than an agency standard:

  • Once or twice a year, review the form: numbers still right, contacts still willing, school and workplace details current. Ready.gov's fire-escape advice for drills is twice a year; the same rhythm can be borrowed.{{cite:shelter-ready-home-fires}}
  • Test the out-of-area contact: have each person send them a text and check that the message arrives.
  • Run a "what if we can't reach each other for an hour" talk-through, once, with the kids.

What this guide has and hasn't done

This guide reproduces the contents of Ready.gov's planning pages, the FCC's emergency-communication tips, a CDC page on reunification and one state school-safety page, each read in full on the date shown. The FCC page is undated and partly outdated, the Colorado page is specific to Colorado, and the CDC page's displayed review date (January 2021) is earlier than its publication date (November 2024); we recorded each as shown. We did not open the Ready.gov fillable PDF card, the Red Cross's Reconnecting Families program, NCMEC's disaster pages, or the Standard Reunification Method materials the Colorado page links. We read FEMA's reunification fact sheet and NCMEC's hotline page on 2026-11-01. We did not verify any other state's or district's reunification rules. Several practical suggestions here, including the three-level meeting place structure, the paper copy, the backup contact and the questions for schools, are ours and are marked as such. We have not tested any messaging service or app, and we do not recommend one.
