Read this first

This is a history of how people made and carried fire, ending in how to handle fire safely today. It is not a how-to. Nothing here teaches you to start a fire by any old method, and it should not be used that way.

  • Before any outdoor fire, find out whether it is allowed that day. Windy, dry weather can mean campfires are banned, and some places never allow outdoor fires at all.{{cite:hist-field-nps-campfires}}{{cite:hist-field-nfpa-outdoor-fire}}
  • Never burn charcoal or use a camp stove indoors, and never burn anything in a stove or fireplace that is not vented. Carbon monoxide has no smell and no colour.{{cite:hist-field-cdc-co-basics}}
  • Never use gasoline or other flammable liquids to start or feed a fire, and never leave a fire unattended.{{cite:hist-field-nfpa-outdoor-fire}}{{cite:hist-field-smokey-campfire}}{{cite:hist-field-nps-campfires}}

If a fire gets away from you, call your emergency number (911 in the United States). Headache, dizziness, nausea and confusion near anything that burns fuel can be carbon monoxide poisoning; get into fresh air and call for help.{{cite:hist-field-cdc-co-basics}}

The problem fire solved, and the one it created

For most of human history, having fire meant either keeping it or making it, and making it was work. The Science Museum Group, which holds one of the largest collections of fire-making tools in the world, puts the first evidence of controlled fire at least a million years back.Documented history{{cite:hist-field-smg-fire-methods}} The same museum offers a plain account of what any method has to do: raise the temperature of a fine, easily burned material (tinder) until it glows as an ember, then use the ember to heat larger material (kindling) until it burns as a flame.{{cite:hist-field-smg-fire-methods}}

That is the mechanism behind every tool in this story. The tools differ in how they get the first spark or the first heat. What changed over the centuries is mostly how long it took, how much skill it needed, how portable it was, and who got hurt along the way.

Editor's note We lead with that last point because the story is usually told as a triumph of convenience. The records we read tell a more mixed one. Each step toward easier fire also moved fire into more kitchens, pockets, workshops and camps, and each of those places has its own ways of going wrong.

Fire you carried: flint, steel and the tinder box

The Science Museum describes striking a sharp-edged flint, or another hard stone such as quartzite or chert, against a steel striker as one of the oldest and most widespread methods of making fire. Tiny hot particles of metal split off the steel and, if they land well, ignite the tinder.Documented history{{cite:hist-field-smg-fire-methods}} Fire steels were invented once iron could be forged, are found across the world in many shapes, and are almost always curved to fit the hand. The museum's examples include a hanging household tinder box from Norway that was a common fixture of homes until the mid-nineteenth century, a gourd tinder kit from the West Indies, a leather belt pouch from Tibet, and a flintlock "tinder pistol" owned mostly by wealthier European households.{{cite:hist-field-smg-fire-methods}}

In the United States, the National Park Service's Pipe Spring National Monument describes the same toolkit from the pioneer side. Flint and steel were used "long before matches," and both pioneers and American Indians used them. Most people, the park explains, carried charred cotton cloth, which the sparks light far more reliably than raw plant material, and the glowing cloth was then moved into a bundle of tinder and blown into flame. The park also notes that steel was a popular trade item for trappers and explorers dealing with tribes.Documented history{{cite:hist-field-nps-flint-steel}} The Smithsonian's National Museum of American History holds an iron tinder box classified under domestic life, though its online record gives no date or maker.{{cite:hist-field-nmah-tinder-box}}

Editor's note The word "carried" is doing a lot of work here. A tinder box was a household appliance, kept in a known place and refilled with dry material. It was not a pocket gadget you pulled out in a storm. The records we read describe the tools; they do not tell us how often the method failed, how long a household needed on a damp morning, or how many fires were lost to sparks in a thatched room. We found no source that measures any of that.

Fire you made: friction, pressure and chemistry

The Science Museum sorts historic fire-making into percussion (the flint and steel above), friction, compression of air, chemical, solar and electrical methods, and says that when each began is unclear: most have ancient roots and were used in different places at the same time.{{cite:hist-field-smg-fire-methods}}

Friction. Friction methods grind solid combustible materials together until an ember forms. The museum's examples carry the names of the people who used them: a twig twisted in a hearth by the Sambaa people of north-eastern Tanzania, a thong-driven fire-drill from the Inuit at Port Clarence, Alaska, and a photograph of two Maori people using a fire-plough in Aotearoa New Zealand. The museum's one general statement is this: "All techniques require great skill, fitness, and knowledge of environmental conditions."Documented history{{cite:hist-field-smg-fire-methods}}

Compressed air. A fire piston ignites tinder by rapidly compressing air in a sealed cylinder. The museum places its origin in Southeast Asia and the Pacific Islands and says fire pistons were patented in France and England in 1807, with scholars disagreeing about whether the European versions developed independently.Uncertain{{cite:hist-field-smg-fire-methods}}

Chemistry. Early chemical fire-makers did not trust hand skill at all. Johann Wolfgang Dobereiner's lamp of 1823 made fire from a reaction among zinc, acid, hydrogen and platinum. The museum says more than 20,000 were sold in Germany and Britain in the 1820s, and that their popularity was short because they were heavy and dangerous.{{cite:hist-field-smg-fire-methods}}

A note on the Science Museum's collection

The museum says most of its fire-making objects came from a private collector active in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, at the height of British colonial power, and that tracing the provenance of purchases is difficult and part of a "long and problematic tradition of colonial exploitation."{{cite:hist-field-smg-fire-methods}} We cite the museum for what it says about how the tools worked and who used them, and we name peoples exactly as the museum names them. We have not independently confirmed those attributions with the communities concerned.

The friction match: a dangerous convenience

Sulphur-coated pine splints that could be lit from an existing flame were made in China around 577 AD, according to the Science Museum. They could not ignite themselves. For centuries, inventors tried self-igniting chemical mixtures, many of which "indeed created explosions" but could not safely pass flame to slow-burning wood.Documented history{{cite:hist-field-smg-fire-methods}}

That changed, in the museum's telling, with a pharmacist in Stockton-on-Tees. The Preston Hall Museum's record, hosted by the BBC, says John Walker found in 1826 that a stick coated with chemicals burst into flame when scraped across his hearth.{{cite:hist-field-bbc-walker-light}} Tees Valley Museums adds that he was an independent chemist who had been looking for a way to ignite fire safely and easily, that he stirred the mixture with that stick, and that he opened his chemist's shop in 1819.{{cite:hist-field-tvm-walker}}

The two museums disagree on one date. The Preston Hall record says Walker sold his first "Friction Light" on 12 April 1827; Tees Valley Museums says he sold his first box on 7 April 1827.Uncertain{{cite:hist-field-bbc-walker-light}}{{cite:hist-field-tvm-walker}} We have not found a source that resolves it, and nothing in this guide depends on which is right. Early Walker matches, the Preston Hall record adds, were first made of cardboard and then of hand-cut wooden splints, and were later boxed with sandpaper to strike on.{{cite:hist-field-bbc-walker-light}}

The Science Museum describes the match as coated with sulfur and a chemical head and drawn over sandpaper to light, and says that "flaming balls" sometimes fell off the sticks, so they were considered dangerous.{{cite:hist-field-smg-fire-methods}} Editor's note The museum names the chemicals in the coating. We leave the list out on purpose. This is a history page, and nothing about a nineteenth-century match head belongs in a modern kitchen.

Walker never patented the match. Tees Valley Museums says Michael Faraday himself advised him to, and that he remained uncredited for decades; a statue was not unveiled in Stockton until 1977.{{cite:hist-field-tvm-walker}} Samuel Jones of London launched his own "Lucifers" in 1829, which the Preston Hall record calls a copy of Walker's design.{{cite:hist-field-bbc-walker-light}}

The price paid in factories

The Science Museum says the biggest change came in 1830, when the French chemist Charles Sauria replaced the match head's antimony compound with white phosphorus. Matches could now be struck on almost any surface. Match-making became big business. Bryant and May in East London was one of the leading producers, and the work was done mostly by women and children from poor surrounding neighbourhoods.Documented history{{cite:hist-field-smg-fire-methods}}

The museum is direct about the cost. White phosphorus caused "phossy jaw," a condition that destroyed the bones of the jaw, could lead to brain damage, and was often fatal. In 1888, around 1,400 mostly female and teenage workers walked out of Bryant and May in the "Matchgirl Strikes," which the museum says helped drive a new trade unionism. The company made small concessions, and it took more than ten years for it to stop using white phosphorus. The museum adds that the dangers of white phosphorus "were known long before" the strike, and that the safety match, which only lights on a specially prepared striking surface, was pioneered by Gustaf Erik Pasch in 1844.{{cite:hist-field-smg-fire-methods}}

Editor's note Notice what is and is not being said. The danger of the match is not that it lit fires in homes. The danger recorded here is to the people who made it. A safer product reached customers decades before the safer working conditions reached the factory floor. That gap is worth remembering whenever a convenient old technology is described as simply "better."

The Science Museum ends its survey at the lighter: in 1903, Carl Auer von Welsbach discovered the alloy ferrocerium, which throws a large spark when scraped, and lighters built on it, first fuelled with naphtha and from the 1950s mostly with butane, became the most popular fire-making tool today.{{cite:hist-field-smg-fire-methods}}

Fire today: the rules that matter more than the method

If the history has a modern lesson, it is that the method you use matters far less than where, when and how long you keep a fire. The U.S. Fire Administration says unintentional actions are the leading cause of outside fires.Official guidance{{cite:hist-field-usfa-outdoor-fire}} The National Fire Protection Association adds that even a small fire or fire pit can throw embers that ignite nearby structures or start a wildfire, and that some communities never allow outdoor fires, while many others restrict them by season or conditions, so the first step is to ask the local fire department.Official guidance{{cite:hist-field-nfpa-outdoor-fire}}

The National Park Service says the same thing in park terms: each park has its own rules on where and when campfires are allowed, and wind and dryness may mean a ban.{{cite:hist-field-nps-campfires}} The Smokey Bear campaign adds a checklist of things never to burn (trash, aerosols, pressurized containers, batteries, glass and aluminum) and says never to use flammable liquids to start a fire.{{cite:hist-field-smokey-campfire}}

Where the agencies differ

The sources do not agree on how far a fire should be from anything that can burn. That is not a mistake on our part; it is the sources. Different fires, different settings and different bodies give different distances.

Your local fire authority's rule is the one that governs, and if you cannot get an answer, the larger distance is the safer choice. Carbon monoxide and enclosed spaces

The CDC says carbon monoxide is produced any time fuel is burned in vehicles, small engines, stoves, lanterns, grills, fireplaces, gas ranges or furnaces, and that it can build up indoors and poison people and animals. People who are sleeping or intoxicated can die before they notice symptoms. The CDC says never burn charcoal indoors, never use a portable gas camp stove indoors, and recommends battery-operated or battery backup CO detectors near every sleeping area.Official guidance{{cite:hist-field-cdc-co-basics}} The U.S. Fire Administration adds that grills should be used only outdoors because indoor use can cause both fires and carbon monoxide poisoning.{{cite:hist-field-usfa-outdoor-fire}}

The National Weather Service winter-storm pages mention keeping matches and a candle in a vehicle survival kit, and give instructions for a stuck vehicle that involve exhaust and carbon monoxide. We do not reproduce them here, and we do not turn a history page into fuel and engine guidance. Read them at the source if you travel in winter.{{cite:hist-field-nws-winter-before}}{{cite:hist-field-nws-winter-during}}

Fire in the household

The U.S. Fire Administration says to store matches and lighters out of children's sight and reach, to watch children closely around a fire pit, to put a metal screen over wood-burning fires, and to turn off or put out fires before leaving.{{cite:hist-field-usfa-outdoor-fire}} The National Weather Service winter-storm page lists a smoke alarm, tested monthly, and a fire extinguisher among the supplies to have at home.{{cite:hist-field-nws-winter-before}}

Current safety verdict

Safe only under stated conditions

The old tools are history. The safe-use rules are current. No agency or museum page we read endorses any traditional method as a dependable way to start a fire in an emergency, and none gives a success rate. Where a method has a stated condition, it is spelled out below.

  • Flint, steel and charred cloth: context-dependent. A real historical method that the National Park Service describes. Using it still means following every current rule for outdoor fire, and the sources give no reliability figure.{{cite:hist-field-nps-flint-steel}}{{cite:hist-field-nps-campfires}}
  • Friction and fire-piston methods: not validated for modern household use. The Science Museum says they require great skill and knowledge of conditions, and no agency evaluates them as safe or dependable.{{cite:hist-field-smg-fire-methods}}
  • Early chemical matches and phosphorus-headed matches: unsafe by modern standards, for the user and for the maker. Do not try to make them.{{cite:hist-field-smg-fire-methods}}
  • Lighting any outdoor fire: only where the local fire authority and land manager allow it that day, with water and a way to put it out on hand, and never with flammable liquids or by burning trash.{{cite:hist-field-nfpa-outdoor-fire}}{{cite:hist-field-smokey-campfire}}
  • Anything that burns fuel indoors without proper venting: not acceptable. Carbon monoxide kills without warning.{{cite:hist-field-cdc-co-basics}}

Then: a skill and a risk

Documented history Fire was kept in a hearth or made with flint and steel, friction, compressed air or early chemistry. Each method took practice, and the first self-lighting matches could shed burning material while their phosphorus versions injured the people who made them.{{cite:hist-field-smg-fire-methods}}{{cite:hist-field-nps-flint-steel}}

Now: a permit and a plan

Official guidance A match or lighter is the ordinary tool; the hard part is deciding whether a fire is allowed at all, keeping it small and supervised, keeping flammable liquids out of it, and putting it fully out. Indoors, the risk is carbon monoxide, not flame.{{cite:hist-field-smokey-campfire}}{{cite:hist-field-nfpa-outdoor-fire}}{{cite:hist-field-cdc-co-basics}}

What we have not done

This history rests on one museum's long overview, two museum accounts of Walker's match, and two short government-site descriptions of flint and steel. We have not seen the Science Museum's object records for the individual tools it names, and we have not opened the day book of John Walker that the museum's page links to. We found no record in the sources we read of how many house or wildfire fires were caused by any of these methods, so we make no claim about it.

The safety panel stands on U.S. sources: the CDC, the U.S. Fire Administration, the National Park Service, the Smokey Bear campaign and a fire-code body (the NFPA, which writes the model fire code many jurisdictions adopt). The National Park Service's campfire page was last updated in 2017. The Smokey Bear page we read does not name its publisher in its text. The NFPA page is a staff article. For all of them your local fire authority's rules take priority. The sources also differ on distances and on whether lighter fluid is acceptable, which is why we name the difference instead of smoothing it over. We have no image of a tinder box, a match or a factory that we have the rights to publish, so this page has none.
