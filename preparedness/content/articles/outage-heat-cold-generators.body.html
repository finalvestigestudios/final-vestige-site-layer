What to do now

  1. Put every fuel-burning device outdoors, or do not use it. That means generators, charcoal and gas grills, camp stoves, lanterns and pressure washers. Never run them inside a home, basement, garage or carport, and never near doors, windows or vents. Opening a door or window does not make it safe.{{cite:shelter-cdc-power-outage}}{{cite:cdc-co-generators}}
  2. If you use a portable generator, keep it at least 20 feet from windows, doors and vents, with the exhaust pointed away from the house, and run a long heavy-duty extension cord to the things it powers.{{cite:shelter-cpsc-co}}{{cite:shelter-ready-power-outages}}
  3. Have a battery-powered or battery-backup carbon monoxide (CO) alarm working before the outage, not after.{{cite:shelter-cdc-co-basics}}
  4. Never heat a home with a gas oven or stovetop.{{cite:shelter-ready-power-outages}}{{cite:shelter-cdc-co-basics}}
  5. If it is dangerously hot or cold in your home, go somewhere that is not. A community location with power, a shelter, or the home of a friend or relative are the options the agencies name.{{cite:shelter-ready-power-outages}}{{cite:shelter-cdc-power-outage}}

Headache, dizziness, weakness, nausea, confusion or sleepiness in several people at once, or after running a generator or heater, can be carbon monoxide. Get everyone outside into fresh air first, then call 911.{{cite:shelter-cpsc-co}} Poison Control is 1-800-222-1222.{{cite:shelter-cdc-power-outage}} A person who is confused, collapsed or very hot or very cold to the touch needs emergency services, not this page.

Why carbon monoxide comes first

Most outage dangers announce themselves. A cold house feels cold. Carbon monoxide does not. The CDC describes it as an odorless, colorless gas that can cause sudden illness and death, and says it is produced whenever fuel is burned in cars, small engines, stoves, lanterns, grills, fireplaces, gas ranges or furnaces.{{cite:shelter-cdc-co-basics}} In an outage, households reach for exactly those things, often in a closed house, at night.

Two features of the CDC's description explain why guidance on this topic is so insistent. The early symptoms (headache, dizziness, weakness, upset stomach, vomiting, chest pain and confusion) are often described as flu-like, and people who are sleeping or have been drinking can die before they have any symptoms at all.{{cite:shelter-cdc-co-basics}} The CDC also lists infants, older adults and people with chronic heart disease, anemia or breathing problems as more likely to become ill from it.{{cite:shelter-cdc-co-basics}} The CPSC adds that CO poisoning is easily mistaken for a cold or flu and is often detected too late.{{cite:shelter-cpsc-heaters}} For rapidly developing, high-level exposures, such as a generator running in a living space, the CPSC says victims can become mentally confused and lose muscle control without first feeling milder symptoms, and will likely die if not rescued. It also says symptom severity depends on both the CO level and how long the exposure lasts.{{cite:shelter-cpsc-co-tips}}

How large is the problem? The agencies' own numbers differ, because they count different things:

Portable generators: the rules every agency repeats

The generator rules are unusually consistent across the CDC, the CPSC and FEMA's Ready.gov. Taken together they say:

  • Outdoors only, and far from the house. At least 20 feet from windows, doors and vents.{{cite:shelter-cdc-power-outage}}{{cite:shelter-cpsc-co}} FEMA's December 2023 power-outage sheet words it as outdoors, at least 20 feet away from "any building openings like windows, doors or garages," and applies the same distance to camp stoves and charcoal grills.{{cite:shelter-fema-power-outage-sheet}} Ready.gov names attached garages in the same sentence and says the fuel belongs outdoors too.{{cite:shelter-ready-power-outages}} The CPSC adds that the exhaust should face away from the home.{{cite:shelter-cpsc-co}}{{cite:shelter-cpsc-co-tips}}
  • Not in a garage or basement, even with the doors open. The CDC says never to run a generator, pressure washer or any gasoline-powered engine inside a basement, garage or other enclosed structure even if doors or windows are open, "unless the equipment is professionally installed and vented." That exception is about permanently installed, purpose-built equipment, not a portable unit someone has moved to a doorway.{{cite:shelter-cdc-power-outage}}
  • Use distance, not a closed door, as your protection. The CDC suggests an extension cord more than 20 feet long to keep the generator at a safe distance.{{cite:shelter-cdc-power-outage}} Ready.gov and the CDC both say to connect appliances with heavy-duty extension cords, and the CDC specifies individual, outdoor-rated cords.{{cite:shelter-ready-power-outages}}{{cite:shelter-cdc-winter-storm}}
  • Keep it dry. Ready.gov warns that touching a wet generator or devices connected to one can cause electric shock. The CDC adds that the generator should sit where rain and snow will not reach it, and that neither the generator nor the appliances should be used if wet.{{cite:shelter-ready-power-outages}}{{cite:shelter-cdc-winter-storm}}
  • Let it cool before you refuel. Fuel spilled on hot engine parts can ignite.{{cite:shelter-ready-power-outages}} Ready.gov's home-fire page says refuel outdoors in a well-ventilated area, and the CDC says not to store gasoline indoors where the fumes could ignite.{{cite:shelter-ready-home-fires}}{{cite:shelter-cdc-winter-storm}}
  • Follow the manufacturer's instructions carefully.{{cite:shelter-ready-power-outages}}
  • Do not connect a generator to your home's electrical system. Ready.gov says so in plain terms on its evacuation page.{{cite:shelter-ready-evacuation}}

Scale of the hazard

The CPSC says one portable generator can produce as much carbon monoxide as hundreds of cars.{{cite:shelter-cpsc-co}} That is why "I'll leave the garage door up" is not a compromise that any of these agencies recognizes. Editor's note Twenty feet is the distance the agencies give as the minimum. None of the pages we read describes 20 feet as full protection by itself, and the CDC pairs the distance with a battery CO alarm indoors for exactly that reason. Treat 20 feet as a floor, not a target.

Uncertain The distance rule is written in terms of your own home's openings: windows, doors, vents and attached garages. The pages we read do not address a neighbor's windows, a covered or enclosed porch, a small yard, or how wind or terrain moves exhaust, and they give no way to judge those. We therefore cannot say that a given yard is safe at any distance, and we do not try to. Keep the battery CO alarms working indoors, and if a location is doubtful, do not run the generator there.

Editor's note We are deliberately not giving a fuel-storage quantity, a run time, a generator size or a wiring method. None of the pages we read provides one, and these are the details where an invented number can hurt someone. If you want a generator that powers a furnace, a well pump or a medical device, the sources point you to the manufacturer's instructions and, for anything connected to house wiring, to a qualified professional. We cannot tell you more than that from what we verified.

Three smaller hazards come with generator use. The CDC says not to siphon gasoline: mouth siphoning can send fuel into the lungs or stomach, and the vapor can ignite from a lit cigarette or static electricity.{{cite:shelter-cdc-power-outage}} Downed power lines are a separate danger: the CDC's instruction is never to touch a fallen line and not to drive through standing water if downed lines are in it.{{cite:shelter-cdc-power-outage}} Ready.gov adds that downed lines can electrically charge floodwater.{{cite:shelter-ready-hurricanes}} And during an evacuation or return, Ready.gov repeats that generators run only outside and away from the home.{{cite:shelter-ready-evacuation}}

Carbon monoxide alarms: where the agencies differ

All three agencies want a working alarm with battery power. They describe where to put it a little differently:

Carbon monoxide alarm placement and upkeep, as each page words it
PagePlacementUpkeep and notes
CDC, CO basicsBattery-operated or battery backup detectors "near every sleeping area."{{cite:shelter-cdc-co-basics}}Check batteries when you change clocks in spring and fall. Replace following the manufacturer's instructions or every 5 years. A digital readout can show the highest level recorded.{{cite:shelter-cdc-co-basics}}
CPSCBattery-operated or battery-backup alarms on every level of the home and outside sleeping areas.{{cite:shelter-cpsc-co}}Interconnected alarms are best: when one sounds, they all sound.{{cite:shelter-cpsc-co}}
Ready.govWorking detectors with battery backup on every level of the home.{{cite:shelter-ready-power-outages}}Also in "central locations" of each level, per its outage page.{{cite:shelter-ready-power-outages}}

Uncertain The three wordings do not conflict, but they are not identical. The CDC's phrase is about sleeping areas; the CPSC and Ready.gov add every level, and FEMA's newer sheet says both. The practical reconciliation, which is ours and not an agency's, is to cover both: an alarm outside each sleeping area and one on each level. Whichever model you buy, read the package for its own placement rules, since the agencies point to the manufacturer for that.

The CDC also says to use a battery-powered or battery-backup detector specifically when running a generator.{{cite:shelter-cdc-power-outage}} Editor's note The reason is plain enough: a plug-in alarm depends on the same power that is out. Know the alarm's sound before you need to. If it goes off, the CPSC's instruction is to get outside to fresh air immediately and then call 911.{{cite:shelter-cpsc-co}}

Editor's note Renters. If you rent, the alarms in the building may be the landlord's responsibility and the service of the furnace or water heater almost certainly is. We did not research tenancy law and make no claim about what yours requires. The practical step is to ask in writing whether the home has working CO alarms and when the heating system was last serviced, and to buy a battery alarm yourself if the answer is unclear. A small battery unit is a modest cost compared with the other things in this guide.

Staying warm

The CDC's winter-storm page begins from the observation that staying indoors cuts the risk of crashes and falls, but that the home itself brings hazards of its own.{{cite:shelter-cdc-winter-storm}} The safest warmth comes from things that burn nothing.

Heat without fuel

For a power outage, the CDC recommends having at least one backup heat source: extra blankets, sleeping bags and warm coats are the first item on its list.{{cite:shelter-cdc-power-outage}} The CDC's tips for keeping the heat you have are plain and cheap:{{cite:shelter-cdc-winter-storm}}

  • Avoid unnecessarily opening doors or windows.
  • Close off rooms you do not need.
  • Stuff towels or rags in cracks under doors.
  • Close draperies, or cover windows with blankets, at night.

The CDC also says that eating well-balanced meals helps you stay warm, that alcohol and caffeine make the body lose heat faster, and that warm, sweet drinks or broth are better choices. People with dietary restrictions should ask their doctor.{{cite:shelter-cdc-winter-storm}}

Heaters: what the sources say and where they stop

For electric space heaters, which only apply if you have power or a generator, the CDC's rules are: use models with automatic shut-off switches and non-glowing elements; keep them at least 3 feet from drapes, furniture, bedding and other flammable materials; never cover them; never put them on furniture or near water; never leave children unattended near one; and do not use one with a damaged cord or one that sparks. The cord should not be a tripping hazard and should not run under carpets or rugs. The CDC says to avoid extension cords with space heaters.{{cite:shelter-cdc-winter-storm}} Ready.gov adds that heaters should carry a mark from a nationally recognized laboratory such as Underwriters Laboratories and should switch off automatically if tipped over.{{cite:shelter-ready-home-fires}}

For fireplaces, wood stoves and other combustion heaters, the CDC says to use them only if they are properly vented to the outside and do not leak gas from the flue or exhaust into the room, to have the heating system serviced by a qualified technician every year, not to burn paper in a fireplace, and to burn only the fuel the heater is designed for.{{cite:shelter-cdc-winter-storm}} Chimneys can be blocked by debris, which lets CO build up indoors, so the CDC says to have them checked or cleaned every year.{{cite:shelter-cdc-co-basics}} The CPSC adds that an open fireplace damper may help prevent a build-up of poisonous gases: open it before lighting a fire and keep it open until the ashes are cool.{{cite:shelter-cpsc-co-tips}}

Where the official pages are not tidy

The CDC's power-outage page lists "portable space heaters or kerosene heaters" among backup heat sources and says to check with your local fire department that kerosene heaters are legal where you live.{{cite:shelter-cdc-power-outage}} Its winter-storm page says to "make sure you have proper ventilation if you must use a kerosene heater."{{cite:shelter-cdc-winter-storm}} Ready.gov says to use only crystal-clear K-1 kerosene, never overfill, and use the heater in a well-ventilated room away from curtains.{{cite:shelter-ready-home-fires}} The CPSC adds water-clear 1-K grade kerosene, never gasoline.{{cite:shelter-cpsc-heaters}} Yet the CDC also says CO is produced any time fuel is burned in stoves, lanterns, grills, fireplaces or furnaces,{{cite:shelter-cdc-co-basics}} and the CPSC says never to use fuel-burning portable heaters or lanterns while sleeping in enclosed areas such as tents, campers and vehicles.{{cite:shelter-cpsc-heaters}} None of these pages gives a ventilation figure.

Editor's note We therefore do not give instructions for running a fuel-burning heater inside a home, and we do not suggest that "cracking a window" settles the question. The sources do not support that claim, and a missing number is a reason to stop, not to guess. The CDC's own fallback is to leave: "if conditions are too hot or too cold, seek shelter with friends or at a community shelter."{{cite:shelter-cdc-power-outage}} This disagreement in emphasis is one of the items flagged for the human safety reviewer.

Two more items are clear. The CDC says never use a gas range or oven to heat a home, and Ready.gov repeats it, adding that the practice also increases the risk of fires, burns and appliance damage.{{cite:shelter-cdc-power-outage}}{{cite:shelter-ready-power-outages}} FEMA's December 2023 sheet extends the rule to a gas stovetop, oven, grill or dryer, and says not to use gas stoves or candles to heat your home.{{cite:shelter-fema-power-outage-sheet}} And the CDC says never use a charcoal grill, hibachi, lantern or portable camping stove inside a home, tent or camper; burning charcoal gives off CO whatever its color.{{cite:shelter-cdc-power-outage}}{{cite:shelter-cdc-co-basics}} For light, the CDC prefers battery-powered flashlights and lanterns to candles, gas lanterns or torches. If you do use candles, keep them away from anything that can catch fire and never leave them unattended.{{cite:shelter-cdc-power-outage}}

Cold and the body

The CDC's hypothermia signs in adults are shivering, exhaustion, confusion, fumbling hands, memory loss, slurred speech and drowsiness. In babies the signs are bright red, cold skin and very low energy. If you notice them, take the person's temperature; below 95°F is an emergency and calls for medical attention immediately. If help is not available right away, warm the person up.{{cite:shelter-cdc-winter-storm}} Ready.gov's first actions are to go to a warm room, warm the center of the body first (chest, neck, head and groin), and keep the person dry and wrapped in warm blankets, including the head and neck.{{cite:shelter-ready-winter-weather}}

For frostbite, the signs are white or grayish-yellow skin, skin that feels unusually firm or waxy, and numbness, usually on the nose, ears, cheeks, chin, fingers and toes. The CDC says to seek medical care. Ready.gov's first actions are to go to a warm room, soak in warm water, and use body heat, and it says not to massage the area or use a heating pad.{{cite:shelter-cdc-winter-storm}}{{cite:shelter-ready-winter-weather}}

If cold water pipes are a worry, the CDC suggests leaving all taps slightly open so they drip, keeping the home warm and opening cabinet doors below sinks to let warm air reach the pipes. Thaw a frozen pipe slowly with warm air from an electric hair dryer, not a torch. If it cannot be thawed or has burst, use bottled water or get water from a neighbor.{{cite:shelter-cdc-winter-storm}} For when the water itself becomes the question, see {{link:making-water-safer|making water safer in an emergency}}.

If you are stranded in a car in a winter storm, the CDC says staying in the car is often the safest choice. It advises running the motor and heater for about 10 minutes each hour with one window opened slightly, and making sure snow is not blocking the exhaust pipe, to reduce the risk of CO poisoning.{{cite:shelter-cdc-winter-storm}} Editor's note This is a roadside instruction for a car in open air. It is not guidance for running any engine in a garage, which the CDC forbids even with the door open.{{cite:shelter-cdc-co-basics}} The National Weather Service gives the same figures for a stuck car: stay in the vehicle, run the motor about 10 minutes each hour for heat, open the window a little while the motor runs, and clear snow from the exhaust pipe.{{cite:shelter-nws-winter-during}} Both are written for a stuck car outdoors and depend on the exhaust pipe staying clear. Neither says what to do if someone in the car starts to feel unwell, and we do not invent an answer; if you can reach 911, call. FEMA's December 2023 sheet words the vehicle rule more generally: if you use your vehicle as a source of power or warmth, run it in a well-ventilated place outside, and never leave it running in a garage even with the door open.{{cite:shelter-fema-power-outage-sheet}}

Staying cool

Ready.gov defines extreme heat as a period of high heat and humidity, with temperatures above 90 degrees, lasting at least two to three days. It says extreme heat is responsible for the highest number of annual deaths among all weather-related hazards, and that older adults, children and people with certain illnesses and chronic conditions are at greater risk.{{cite:shelter-ready-heat}} A heat wave can also cause the outage, so the two problems often arrive together.{{cite:shelter-ready-heat}}

What the agencies say to do:

  • Do not rely on a fan alone. The CDC says to use fans only if the indoor temperature is below 90°F, because above that a fan can increase body temperature. Ready.gov says a fan creates airflow and a false sense of comfort without reducing body temperature or preventing heat illness. The CDC's outage page adds that when it is really hot, fans will not prevent heat-related illness.{{cite:shelter-cdc-heat}}{{cite:shelter-ready-heat}}{{cite:shelter-cdc-power-outage}}
  • Get to air conditioning. The CDC suggests dialing 2-1-1, using the National Center for Healthy Housing's cooling-center listings by state, or contacting your local health department or emergency management agency. It also names a mall or public library, and says even a few hours in air conditioning helps.{{cite:shelter-cdc-heat}}{{cite:shelter-cdc-power-outage}}
  • Cool the body directly. Take cool showers or baths.{{cite:shelter-cdc-power-outage}}{{cite:shelter-ready-heat}}
  • Drink fluids. The CDC suggests checking urine color: light yellow or clear usually means you are drinking enough.{{cite:shelter-cdc-heat}}
  • Lighten up. Ready.gov recommends loose, lightweight, light-colored clothing, using the oven less, avoiding high-energy activity in the midday heat, and shade and a wide-brimmed hat outdoors.{{cite:shelter-ready-heat}}
  • Never leave people or pets in a closed car on a warm day.{{cite:shelter-ready-heat}}

Symptoms of overheating, per the CDC, include muscle cramping, unusually heavy sweating, shortness of breath, dizziness, headaches, weakness and nausea.{{cite:shelter-cdc-heat}} Ready.gov separates the severe form: heat stroke, with signs of an extremely high body temperature (above 103°F), red, hot, dry skin with no sweat, a rapid, strong pulse, and dizziness, confusion or unconsciousness. For suspected heat stroke it says call 911 or get the person to a hospital immediately; if possible move them to a cool, shaded area, remove outer clothing, place a cold, wet cloth or ice pack on the head, neck, armpits and groin (or soak their clothing in cool water), and elevate their feet. For heat cramps or heat exhaustion it says go somewhere cooler, remove excess clothing, take sips of sports drinks or water, and call a health care provider if symptoms worsen or last more than an hour.{{cite:shelter-ready-heat}} The CDC's outage page says heat stroke can raise body temperature to 106°F or higher within 10 to 15 minutes and can cause death or permanent disability without emergency care.{{cite:shelter-cdc-power-outage}}

Uncertain Ready.gov's "above 103°F" and the CDC's "106°F or higher" are different statements about heat stroke, one a sign and one a trajectory, and we have not found a source reconciling them. Neither is a number to wait for. If someone is confused, faint or not sweating in the heat, call 911.

The CDC also tells people on medications to talk to a doctor before changing anything, since many medicines can contribute to dehydration or overheating, and some need to be kept out of hot places.{{cite:shelter-cdc-heat}}

People who need a different plan

  • Babies. The CDC says infants under one should never sleep in a cold room. It advises removing pillows and soft bedding, dressing the baby in footed pajamas, wearable blankets or sleep sacks, and, if you cannot keep the home warm, making temporary arrangements to stay elsewhere. In an emergency a parent's body heat can warm a baby, with precautions against rolling onto them if you sleep.{{cite:shelter-cdc-winter-storm}}
  • Older adults. The CDC says older adults often make less body heat; people over 65 should check the home temperature often in extreme cold, and neighbors should check on older friends.{{cite:shelter-cdc-winter-storm}} For heat, the CDC says to check on family, friends and neighbors, especially those with chronic medical problems or who live alone.{{cite:shelter-cdc-heat}}
  • People who rely on electric medical equipment or refrigerated medicine. Make the plan with your doctor or pharmacist before an outage. FEMA's December 2023 sheet says to talk to your doctor about how you will use electric medical devices, store medications and stay safe, and Ready.gov asks you to find out how long a medication can be stored at higher temperatures.{{cite:shelter-fema-power-outage-sheet}}{{cite:shelter-ready-power-outages}} For a long outage, the pages word it differently. FEMA's sheet says to replace refrigerated medications if the power is out for a day or more, unless the drug's label says otherwise; to call your doctor or pharmacist if you depend on a refrigerated medication that has been at room temperature; and to use the medicine only until you have a new supply.{{cite:shelter-fema-power-outage-sheet}} The CDC's outage page and Ready.gov's outage page use "throw away" and "discard" for the same situation, and the CDC adds that if a life depends on the drug, it should be used only until a new supply is available.{{cite:shelter-cdc-power-outage}}{{cite:shelter-ready-power-outages}} Editor's note None of this is a reason to stop taking, skip or throw out a medicine you depend on without asking. Call the pharmacist or prescriber for that specific drug; the CDC separately says not to stop or change your medicines until you talk to your doctor.{{cite:shelter-cdc-heat}} Ready.gov's disability page suggests asking your power provider to place you on a priority-restoration list and asking your doctor how to keep equipment running.{{cite:shelter-ready-disabilities}}
  • People with disabilities, in heat. Ready.gov notes higher risk for people with spinal cord injuries (who may not be able to cool by sweating), for people whose medications interfere with cooling, and for people with cognitive, intellectual or developmental disabilities, who may have trouble recognizing or explaining heat's effects. It suggests a support network that checks in regularly, accessible transportation to cooling options planned in advance, and contingency plans with local hospitals or nursing homes for people who cannot be accommodated at conventional cooling sites.{{cite:shelter-ready-disabilities}}
  • Households on a tight budget. Both the winter-weather and extreme-heat pages point to the Low Income Home Energy Assistance Program (LIHEAP) for help with heating or cooling costs and weatherization.{{cite:shelter-ready-winter-weather}}{{cite:shelter-ready-heat}} Blankets, closing off rooms and a community location are free or cheap and appear on every agency's list.
  • Pets. The CDC notes CO poisons animals as well as people.{{cite:shelter-cdc-power-outage}} For heat, Ready.gov says make sure outdoor pets have cool water and shade.{{cite:shelter-ready-heat}} Many shelters accept only service animals, so decide in advance where a pet would go.{{cite:shelter-ready-shelter}}

Food, water and the rest of the outage

This guide stays on heat, cold and combustion. Food safety has its own page in this library once it is built. The numbers agencies give for the first hours are worth knowing, though: a refrigerator will keep food cold for about four hours if the door stays closed, and a full freezer for about 48 hours (24 if half full).{{cite:shelter-cdc-power-outage}}{{cite:shelter-ready-power-outages}}{{cite:fsis-power-failure}} Ready.gov also advises disconnecting appliances and electronics to avoid damage from surges when power returns.{{cite:shelter-ready-power-outages}} For stored water, see {{link:two-week-water-storage|two-week water storage}}.

When to stay and when to leave

Ready.gov's outage page puts the decision in one sentence: go to a community location with power if heat or cold is extreme.{{cite:shelter-ready-power-outages}} The CDC similarly says that if conditions are too hot or too cold, seek shelter with friends or at a community shelter, and that if your home is damaged you should stay with friends or family or in a shelter.{{cite:shelter-cdc-power-outage}}{{cite:cdc-co-generators}}

Editor's note The practical test we suggest is whether your plan depends on a device that, if it fails, hurts someone: a heater left on overnight, a generator you cannot refuel in the dark, a space only one person in the house can keep warm. Pick the alternative that fails safe. For where to go and how to decide, see {{link:shelter-or-evacuate|shelter in place or evacuate}}, and for getting everyone out if a heater, stove or candle starts a fire, see {{link:home-fire-escape-plan|the home fire escape plan}}. What this guide has and hasn't done

Every instruction and figure here comes from a page we read in full on the date shown: two CDC pages on power outages and CO, the CDC winter-weather and heat pages, the CPSC's CO pages, and several Ready.gov pages. Where they differ, we say so: the alarm placement wording, the CO death figures, the heat-stroke temperature, and the treatment of kerosene heaters. We have not tested any appliance. We did not verify generator wiring methods, fuel storage quantities or run times, because none of the pages we read gave them, and so none appear here. We did not read the Red Cross's generator page or NFPA's heating pages. Where FEMA's December 2023 power-outage sheet and an older or differently worded page disagree on medication, we use the sheet's wording and say the pages differ. The CDC pages carry publication dates from 2024 and review or update dates in 2026; the Ready.gov pages we used were updated in 2026; the CPSC pages show no date.
