Read this first

This is a history, not a plumbing guide. It describes how households and forts collected rain, what the records show about how well it worked, and what current health agencies say. It gives no construction details and no treatment doses.

  • Rainwater is not necessarily safe to drink without first removing germs and chemicals from it. Even when it looks clean, it can contain things that could make you sick.{{cite:hist-home-cdc-rainwater}}
  • If you collect rainwater, the CDC suggests using it in ways that limit the chance you will swallow it, and using tap water from a public system or bottled water for drinking, cooking and brushing teeth.{{cite:hist-home-cdc-rainwater}}
  • If rainwater is your only source in an emergency, it must be treated first. The CDC lists rain among outdoor sources that need treatment. See {{link:making-water-safer|making water safer in an emergency}}.{{cite:cdc-find-clean-water}}
  • Collecting rainwater is not allowed in every place. Check with your state environmental or health department before you set anything up.{{cite:hist-home-cdc-rainwater}}

If you or someone in your home has diarrhea, vomiting or fever after drinking untreated water, call a health professional. If the person is seriously ill, call your emergency number.

The old idea: let the roof do the work

Rain falls on every roof. A household that cannot count on a nearby spring or a dependable well can catch that rain in gutters, send it down a pipe and keep it in a tank. The tank is a cistern. At household scale the smaller version is a barrel under a downspout.

The National Park Service and the foundations that run historic houses have recorded the practice in a handful of places, from a Virginia mountaintop to a New Mexico army post to the Caribbean. The records agree on the engineering idea. They are less forthcoming about how safe the water was to drink, and that gap is the subject of this guide.

Monticello: a plan, and a leak

Thomas Jefferson's house stood on a mountaintop, and the Thomas Jefferson Foundation records that he had four brick cisterns installed from 1809 to collect rainwater and ease the problem of supplying water to the house. Two on the north side were reserved for fire, and two on the south to serve the kitchen, laundry and garden.Documented history{{cite:hist-home-monticello-cisterns}} Jefferson did the arithmetic himself, multiplying average rainfall by the area of the roofs, and concluded that four brick cisterns would supply 600 gallons of fresh water each day.{{cite:hist-home-monticello-cisterns}}

The sums were not the problem. The brickwork was. By 1810 Jefferson was already writing that unless a cistern was done in time to dry, it would give way again in winter. By 1815 he admitted the cisterns would be useful only if he could make them hold water. In 1818 he called them "useless," for want of a proper cement to line them.{{cite:hist-home-monticello-cisterns}} The Foundation's summary is that they were never completely watertight and that only two reliably collected and held water over the years.{{cite:hist-home-monticello-cisterns}}

Notice what Jefferson did and did not say about the water. His own letter says that, "in order to have potable water," he had always intended to sink a sand-filled well beside a cistern, expecting the water to be "sufficiently purified" as it rose through the sand, and that he had not been able to do so.{{cite:hist-home-monticello-cisterns}} In other words, the owner of the best-documented cisterns in early America planned to drink from them only after an extra step, and never recorded completing it. The sand-well idea is discussed in {{link:water-purification-through-history|water purification through history}}.

Editor's note His arithmetic is a good reminder of a practical point that has not changed. A cistern is limited by roof area and rainfall, not by the size of the tank. A large tank under a small roof in a dry year is a large empty tank.

Fort Union: stored for fire

At Fort Union in New Mexico, the National Park Service records a different pattern. The fort's original water source was a stream, with troops hauling it in wagons; later it drilled a main well to about 85 feet, and in the mid-1880s indoor plumbing arrived.Documented history{{cite:hist-home-nps-fort-union-water}} The rain was a supplement, not the supply. The fort kept a large store of water on hand in case of fire, "using primarily cisterns," filled by collecting rain water off the roofs. Each held about 20,000 gallons, with two at the hospital and two at the depot.{{cite:hist-home-nps-fort-union-water}}

The same page shows what the water systems did not solve. It describes disposal of human waste at the fort in terms of privies and large pits, notes poor drainage and flooding at the second fort, and quotes a soldier's account of bathing in a borrowed tub.{{cite:hist-home-nps-fort-union-water}} The page does not say the cistern water was drunk, and it does not say how clean it was.Uncertain

Editor's note Fort Union is a useful corrective to a romantic picture of rain barrels as an off-grid water supply. A well-run institution with money and soldiers used cisterns where they made sense: as a large, simple store of non-drinking water for an emergency. Its drinking and cooking water came from other sources. We note that as a reading of the page, not a statement in it.

San Juan: the cistern that was feared

San Juan National Historic Site in Puerto Rico preserves the most dramatic cisterns in this guide. An archived National Park Service news release describes the Spanish-built rain-harvesting system beneath the plazas of El Morro and San Cristóbal: three cisterns at El Morro holding nearly a quarter of a million gallons and four at San Cristóbal holding more than half a million. It says the water they harvested was "crucial to the survival of the Spanish" when the cisterns were built in the mid-1700s, and that religious images were painted on the walls "for the protection of their water."Documented history{{cite:hist-home-nps-san-juan-cisterns}}

The same release records an episode that shows how a community with no way to test water behaves. After the Spanish-American War of 1898, U.S. Army personnel took over the fortifications and closed the cisterns, "believing the water had been poisoned by the defeated Spanish soldiers." Apart from one cistern, they remained unused from 1949, when the site was established, until a restoration in 2011.{{cite:hist-home-nps-san-juan-cisterns}} The release says the restored cisterns now supply non-potable water for restrooms, maintenance facilities and similar uses, with further work planned for potable water.{{cite:hist-home-nps-san-juan-cisterns}}

Editor's note The release does not say whether the 1898 suspicion was justified, and we do not know. The point is how the story reads from a modern safety perspective. A cistern's water quality is invisible, so the only options were trust, fear, or a test. The restoration chose the cautious option: non-potable use first.

What the historical record does not tell us

The sources above describe capacity, construction, ownership and use. They do not give a single measurement of what was in the water. There is no counted illness, no bacterial test and no chemical analysis in the pages we read. That is not because nobody cared. It is because the tools did not exist. It means a visit to a historic site can show you that cisterns were common and valued. It cannot show you that cistern water was safe to drink.Editor's note

Two hazards in particular would have gone unrecorded. The first is what comes off the roof. The CDC says rainwater can pick up germs and chemicals from the air and from the collection system, and that asbestos, lead and copper can get into it from roofing materials, gutters, piping, storage materials and other surfaces. Bird droppings on a roof can end up in the barrel or tank, and are especially likely to wash in when it rains after several days of dry weather.Official guidance{{cite:hist-home-cdc-rainwater}} The EPA's rain-barrel page makes the same observation in shorter form, noting bacteria from birds and other animals and chemicals from roof materials, and says to consider this before using rain barrel water on edible plants.{{cite:hist-home-epa-rain-barrels}}

The second hazard is what happens in the tank. The CDC advises regularly emptying rain barrels and keeping the system clean and in repair, and says that to stop mosquitoes laying eggs in a barrel you can add a screen where the water enters or empty it at least every 10 days.{{cite:hist-home-cdc-rainwater}}

What current agencies say

The CDC's position on rainwater

The CDC begins with the point that matters: "Rainwater is not necessarily safe to drink without first removing germs and chemicals from it."Official guidance{{cite:hist-home-cdc-rainwater}} It advises thinking about the use first, since rainwater you drink needs to be cleaner than rainwater you use to water flowers. For uses that limit swallowing, such as watering plants you do not eat or washing items you do not use for cooking or eating, rainwater is considered; for drinking, cooking, brushing teeth, rinsing produce and watering plants you will eat, it recommends public-system tap water or bottled water if possible.{{cite:hist-home-cdc-rainwater}}

It adds three cautions that a historian would not have thought of. Mixing rainwater with water that has already been treated, such as bottled or public-system tap water, can make the treated water less safe. Rainwater should not be able to enter the pipes that carry safe drinking water. And if you drink, cook or bathe with rainwater you should test it regularly, taking advice from your health department about which germs and chemicals to test for.{{cite:hist-home-cdc-rainwater}} It also describes a simple device called a first flush diverter, which sends the first rain of a storm, the part that picks up the most dirt, germs and chemicals, away from the collection system. How much it should divert depends on the size of the roof.{{cite:hist-home-cdc-rainwater}}

If your rainwater contains germs or chemicals, the CDC says to treat it with a method or home treatment system that removes what is in it. We do not describe those here; they are in {{link:making-water-safer|the treatment guide}} and, for improvised filters, in {{link:homemade-water-filters|why homemade filters can be dangerous}}.{{cite:hist-home-cdc-rainwater}}

After a storm or flood

The CDC's cistern page is written for places where cisterns are common, and its stance on untreated catchment cisterns is conservative. If a cistern stores untreated water and has open connections such as gutters or overflows, it may fill with storm debris or floodwater during a disaster, and the CDC's advice is to avoid using the water and ask your health department what to do. It says disinfecting cisterns filled with storm debris or floodwater does not work well, because it is very difficult to work out how much chlorine you need and mix it in correctly, and the water may be re-contaminated through the open connections.Official guidance{{cite:hist-home-cdc-cisterns}}

It also says that if a cistern's water has fuel or other toxic chemicals in it, treating it cannot make it safe, and that after a disaster only clean treated water should be used for drinking, cooking, making ice, washing dishes, bathing, brushing teeth and cleaning. Water contaminated with germs or chemicals can still be used to flush toilets.{{cite:hist-home-cdc-cisterns}} We deliberately do not reprint the CDC's cistern-disinfection table. That table applies to cisterns storing treated public-system water, and it is the CDC's to keep current; use the page itself.

Before you collect anything

The CDC notes that collecting rainwater is not allowed in all places, and that some states consider rainwater the property of the state and regulate its collection.{{cite:hist-home-cdc-rainwater}} The EPA's rain-barrel page points to state and local resources, and itself carries a note that it is no longer being updated.{{cite:hist-home-epa-rain-barrels}}

Current safety verdict

Not validated for modern household use

Do not treat collected rainwater as drinking water. That is the practice the history is silent on, and the one current guidance advises against without treatment and testing.

  • The CDC says rainwater is not necessarily safe to drink without first removing germs and chemicals, and that regular testing is advised if you drink, cook or bathe with it.{{cite:hist-home-cdc-rainwater}}
  • For untreated catchment cisterns hit by a storm or flood, the CDC says to avoid the water and ask the health department; disinfecting does not work well.{{cite:hist-home-cdc-cisterns}}
  • Collecting rainwater for uses that limit swallowing, such as watering plants you do not eat, is something the CDC discusses, with maintenance, first-flush and mosquito cautions, and local rules still apply.{{cite:hist-home-cdc-rainwater}}
  • In an emergency with no other source, rain is an outdoor source that must be treated before use. Follow {{link:making-water-safer|the current treatment guidance}}.{{cite:cdc-find-clean-water}}

We are not saying cistern systems are unsafe by nature; public systems also use them. We are saying the historical record contains no evidence about the quality of household cistern water, and current agencies treat it as water that needs testing and treatment before it is drunk.

Then: roof, gutter, brick tank

Documented history Households and garrisons caught roof rain in brick or plastered cisterns, sometimes leaky, kept some for fire, used some for the kitchen, laundry and garden, and relied on trust or tradition for drinking.{{cite:hist-home-monticello-cisterns}}{{cite:hist-home-nps-fort-union-water}}

Now: a use matched to the quality

Official guidance Use rainwater for purposes where swallowing is unlikely, keep it away from drinking-water pipes, test and treat it if people will drink or cook with it, and check your state's rules first.{{cite:hist-home-cdc-rainwater}} Keep a separate stored supply of safe drinking water. What we have not done

We read three site accounts: Monticello's encyclopedia entry, the National Park Service's Fort Union water page, and an archived 2011 National Park Service news release for San Juan. We did not read Jefferson's papers, the fort's quartermaster records, or Spanish colonial documents behind them. We did not find any measured water-quality data for historical cisterns. The Fort Union page is a summary for visitors and says what the cisterns held and how they were filled, not who drank from them.

We did not find a source that explains how Indigenous communities or other peoples collected and stored rain or other water in the places named here, and we do not generalize about it. If you can point us to a reliable source with proper attribution, we would add it. We also did not cover rainwater rules in each state, nor the CDC's guidance on private wells, and we have not checked the further CDC pages that those pages link to.
