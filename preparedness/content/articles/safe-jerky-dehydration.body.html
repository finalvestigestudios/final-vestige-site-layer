What to do now

  1. Do not assume drying makes raw meat safe. USDA says pathogenic bacteria are likely to survive the dry heat of a warm oven and especially the 130°F to 140°F of a food dehydrator, and that illnesses from Salmonella and E. coli O157:H7 have been traced to homemade jerky.{{cite:food-fsis-jerky}}
  2. Heat the meat to a safe internal temperature, and prove it with a thermometer. The USDA Meat and Poultry Hotline's current recommendation is to heat meat to 160°F and poultry to 165°F before dehydrating, checked with a food thermometer. NCHFP and Kansas State Extension offer two other routes to 160°F: heat the strips in the marinade before drying, or heat the dried strips in an oven afterwards. The sources disagree about the order; see the table below.{{cite:food-fsis-jerky-qa}}{{cite:nchfp-jerky}}{{cite:food-ksu-jerky}}
  3. Slice no thicker than ¼ inch, trim off the fat, and keep the meat cold (40°F or below) and the equipment clean until it goes in.{{cite:nchfp-jerky}}
  4. Pork and wild game: NCHFP says to treat the meat for the parasite Trichinella first, by freezing a portion 6 inches or less thick at 0°F or below for at least 30 days, before slicing and marinating. Freezing does not remove bacteria.{{cite:nchfp-jerky}}
  5. Store home-dried jerky cold if you want it to last. The sources give different shelf lives (2 weeks to 2 months) for room temperature; all agree that refrigerating or freezing extends it.{{cite:nchfp-jerky}}{{cite:food-fsis-jerky}}{{cite:food-ksu-jerky}}
  6. If in doubt, throw it out. If someone becomes seriously ill after eating any home-preserved food, call your emergency number or a clinician.

The USDA Meat and Poultry Hotline (1-888-674-6854, Monday to Friday 10 a.m. to 6 p.m. Eastern) answers questions about making jerky safely.{{cite:food-fsis-emergencies}} For a ready-made supply, commercially made jerky is produced under federal inspection and is listed by FSIS as shelf-stable for 12 months.{{cite:food-fsis-jerky}}

  • Uncertain A question where the sources disagree or are silent.
  • Editor's note Our reasoning. It is not a statement from any source and is marked so you can weigh it differently.

Why jerky is not automatically safe

The comfortable story about jerky goes like this: drying removes the water, bacteria need water, therefore dried meat is safe. The first half is right. FSIS explains the principle: removing moisture stops the enzymes and organisms that spoil food from acting, which is why drying is the world's oldest and most common method of preservation, and why commercially dried jerky is shelf-stable.{{cite:food-fsis-jerky}}

The weakness is the word safe. Drying stops bacteria growing. It does not reliably kill the ones already on the raw meat. FSIS's page spells out the physics. Inside a dehydrator or low-temperature oven, evaporating moisture absorbs most of the heat, so the meat itself does not begin to rise in temperature until most of the moisture has gone. By then the bacteria have become more heat resistant, and more likely to survive, and if they are pathogens, the person eating the jerky can get sick.{{cite:food-fsis-jerky}}

FSIS reports two pieces of research to back this up, and we relay them as FSIS summarizes them; we did not read the journal articles.{{cite:food-fsis-jerky}}

  • A University of Georgia and Colorado State study (Journal of Food Protection, 2004) found that marinating alone did not significantly reduce the pathogen on beef slices. Some samples had total bacterial destruction and others some survival, especially jerky made with ground beef. In further experiments, pathogenic E. coli in lab-inoculated venison could survive drying times of up to 10 hours at temperatures up to 145°F.
  • An earlier University of Georgia study (1998) of ground beef jerky found greater bacterial destruction with a curing mix containing salt and sodium nitrite than without, and the highest destruction when the meat was also heated before drying. Its authors concluded that for ground beef jerky made at home, E. coli O157:H7 concerns "are minimized if the meat is precooked to 160°F prior to drying".

The practical lesson is that the heating step, not the drying, is the safety step, and that the marinade is for flavor and tenderness. FSIS says marinating does not make raw meat safe, and NCHFP describes marinating as something done for flavor and tenderness.{{cite:food-fsis-jerky}}{{cite:nchfp-jerky}}

The methods the sources give

Three sources, from USDA, the National Center for Home Food Preservation, and Kansas State Extension, give three versions of "heat to 160°F, then dry". They agree on the temperature and disagree on the sequence. The table sets them side by side with every condition each source attaches. None of these is an invitation to make up your own timing: the internal temperature is the control, and the times are only how a source got there.

Ground-meat jerky, made with a jerky gun or press, is harder. NCHFP says disease-causing organisms are more difficult to eliminate in ground meat, and that an internal temperature of 160°F is necessary to eliminate bacteria such as E. coli O157:H7. Kansas State Extension adds that after dehydrating, ground-meat jerky should be heated for 10 minutes in an oven preheated to 275°F.{{cite:nchfp-jerky}}{{cite:food-ksu-jerky}}

Where the sources differ: before or after

FSIS says to heat before dehydrating, and notes that after drying, bacteria become much more heat resistant. NCHFP offers heating after drying as an acceptable alternative. Its conditions are strips sliced no thicker than ¼ inch and reaching 160°F "in such a way as to prevent case hardening".{{cite:food-fsis-jerky}}{{cite:nchfp-jerky}}

Editor's note All three routes aim at the same temperature, but the FSIS reasoning (bacteria in dried meat are more heat resistant) is at least in tension with the NCHFP post-drying step. We have not found either source addressing the other. We list both and flag this for the reviewer. If you want one rule to follow, the FSIS sequence is the more conservative.

Dehydrators, ovens and thermometers

  • Most dehydrator instructions do not include the heating step, and a dehydrator may not reach 160°F or 165°F, FSIS says.{{cite:food-fsis-jerky}} So do the heating step separately.
  • The dehydrator should have an adjustable temperature dial and hold at least 130°F to 140°F throughout drying.{{cite:food-fsis-jerky-qa}}
  • Do not raise the temperature to rush the process. Kansas State says higher temperatures cause case hardening, which can trap moisture inside and cause spoilage.{{cite:food-ksu-jerky}}
  • Use a metal stem-type food thermometer to check several strips.{{cite:food-fsis-jerky-qa}}{{cite:nchfp-jerky}}
  • Poultry: FSIS gives a 165°F procedure for poultry. NCHFP and Kansas State say raw poultry is generally not recommended for jerky because of the texture and flavor of the finished product. That is a quality reason, not a safety one.{{cite:food-fsis-jerky-qa}}{{cite:nchfp-jerky}}{{cite:food-ksu-jerky}} NCHFP lists smoked turkey breast among the meats that can be used.{{cite:nchfp-jerky}}

Uncertain None of the sources tells you how to confirm that your own dehydrator actually holds 130°F to 140°F. They say the temperature must be constant; they name no check. A separate thermometer placed with the trays would be our suggestion, and we flag it as untested advice rather than official guidance.

Contamination controls, in order

These are the handling steps USDA and NCHFP list, which are the same across sources.{{cite:food-fsis-jerky-qa}}{{cite:nchfp-jerky}}

  1. Wash your hands with soap and running water for at least 20 seconds before and after handling raw meat.
  2. Use clean equipment and utensils.
  3. Keep meat and poultry refrigerated at 40°F or below. Use ground beef and poultry within 2 days and red meats within 3 to 5 days, or freeze them for later.
  4. Thaw frozen meat in the refrigerator, not on the counter.
  5. Marinate in the refrigerator. Do not save and reuse the marinade.
  6. Partially freeze the meat to make slicing easier. Slice no thicker than ¼ inch; thickness makes a difference to safety.
  7. Heat to 160°F (poultry 165°F), measured, by whichever method above you follow.
  8. Dry at a steady temperature until a test piece cracks but does not break when bent; pat off beads of oil with clean towels and cool before packaging.

We do not reprint a marinade recipe. NCHFP and Kansas State give one, and it is for flavor; the marinade does not make the meat safe.{{cite:nchfp-jerky}}

Curing, smoking and "old-fashioned" jerky

Many recipes call for a cure containing nitrite, or for smoking. We give no curing formula, ratio, time or temperature on this page, and we do not endorse any improvised cure.

  • FSIS's summary of the 1998 study found that a curing mix containing salt and sodium nitrite improved bacterial destruction in ground beef jerky.{{cite:food-fsis-jerky}} That is a laboratory finding, not a home recipe.
  • USDA's food-safety service records that many botulism cases have followed home-canned, preserved, cured, smoked or fermented foods contaminated with toxin, and points consumers to the National Center for Home Food Preservation for tested methods.{{cite:fsis-home-cure-qa}}
  • The CDC lists improperly preserved homemade foods among the common sources of foodborne botulism, says you cannot see, smell or taste the toxin, and says: "If in doubt, throw it out".{{cite:cdc-botulism-prevention}}
  • NCHFP describes curing as using salt, and sometimes sugar and seasonings, to draw moisture out of meat, and as a matter of balancing time, temperature and ingredients. It has its own pages for curing and smoking, and that is where tested instructions live.{{cite:nchfp-cure-smoke}}
  • Uncertain Smoking. The sources we read for this guide do not evaluate smoke, cold or hot, as a way to make jerky safe. We therefore do not treat it as a safety step.

Salt, sugar, smoke, low temperatures, vacuum packaging and "it has always worked" are not, on the evidence of the sources, a dependable route to safe meat. For the history of these methods and the reasons they are not a recipe, see {{link:salt-beef-salt-pork-age-of-sail|salt beef and salt pork at sea}} and, when it is written, {{link:pemmican-and-dried-meats|pemmican and dried meats}}.

Dehydrating foods other than meat

This guide is about meat jerky because that is where the documented illnesses and the sharpest disagreements are. Drying fruit and vegetables is covered by the drying section of the National Center for Home Food Preservation; we did not read it for this guide and give no figures for it. FSIS describes sun drying as used mainly for high-acid fruits and vegetables such as apricots, tomatoes and grapes, and says perishable low-acid foods such as meat and poultry may not be dried by those methods.{{cite:food-fsis-shelf-stable}}

Is this for you?

  • Buying instead of making. Commercially made jerky is made under federal inspection and, FSIS says, keeps 12 months. Oregon State Extension lists beef or turkey jerky among pantry proteins.{{cite:food-fsis-jerky}}{{cite:food-osu-survival-food}} For an emergency supply that you will not make fresh, buying may be both safer and simpler.
  • Budget. A dehydrator with an adjustable temperature dial and a metal stem thermometer are the equipment the sources name. We have not priced them.
  • Renters and small kitchens. Heating meat to 160°F, drying for hours, and keeping raw meat apart from other food all take counter space and attention. NCHFP's procedure allows an oven in place of a dehydrator, with a baking sheet and a thermometer.{{cite:nchfp-jerky}}
  • Children, older adults, pregnancy and weakened immune systems. The CDC says people 65 and older, children under 5, people with weakened immune systems and pregnant women are at higher risk of severe illness from food poisoning.{{cite:food-cdc-risk-factors}} The jerky sources we read give no separate rule for them, and we did not read CDC's "Safer Food Choices" pages. Editor's note We would not serve home-dried jerky to anyone in those groups without checking with a clinician or the USDA Hotline first.

What this guide has and hasn't done

Every instruction and figure here comes from the USDA, NCHFP and Extension pages cited, read in full on the date shown. Where they disagree, we say so. We did not read the two journal studies that FSIS summarizes, only FSIS's summary of them. The FSIS fact sheet we read is dated November 2016; the newer Ask USDA article (February 2025) gives the same figures. Kansas State cites an FSIS fact sheet dated September 2023 that we could not retrieve, so we cannot say whether it differs. We did not reprint the marinade recipe. We did not read NCHFP's separate pages on drying fruit or vegetables, on curing, or on smoking beyond their descriptions, and we make no claim about them. We did not dry any meat ourselves, and we cannot tell you how long your dehydrator needs.

Pemmican and other traditional dried meats have their own history and attributions, which we leave for the guide that will cover them rather than generalize here.
