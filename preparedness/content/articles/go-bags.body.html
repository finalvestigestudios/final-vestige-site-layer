What to do now

  1. Decide where you would go and how you would get there before you pack anything. Ready.gov asks you to know your evacuation destination, whether a friend's or relative's home or a designated shelter, and to choose places in different directions.{{cite:start-ready-evacuation}}
  2. Build one bag per person from the portable part of your kit, so you do not have to scramble. Ready.gov says to build a go-bag with the essentials, and to make sure everyone knows where it is kept and who is responsible for it.{{cite:start-ready-evacuation}}
  3. Add what is particular to each person: medicines, glasses, hearing-aid batteries, a mobility spare, a baby's formula, a pet's carrier. Those are the items that cannot be replaced from a shelter's supplies.{{cite:start-ready-kit}}{{cite:start-ready-disability}}
  4. Lift every bag. If someone cannot carry theirs, decide now who will, or what comes out.

If you are told to evacuate, go. Local officials declare mandatory evacuations and may also advise leaving without requiring it; FEMA says to follow their orders and advice.{{cite:ready-are-you-ready}} If anyone is in immediate danger, call 911 or your local emergency number.

What a go-bag is, and what the official sources actually say about it

Ready.gov uses the word in its evacuation guidance. It says to prepare a "go-bag" you can carry when you evacuate on foot or by public transportation, plus supplies for traveling longer distances if you have a car.{{cite:start-ready-evacuation}} It does not list what goes in the bag. It sends you to the Build A Kit page for the supplies list.{{cite:start-ready-evacuation}} Ready.gov's kit page, in turn, speaks of a kit "ready in case you have to leave your home quickly."{{cite:start-ready-kit}} The printable checklist recommends one full kit at home and smaller portable kits elsewhere.{{cite:start-ready-supply-list}}

Uncertain That means there is no single official go-bag list for us to reproduce, no official weight limit, and no official split between what stays and what goes. HUD's multifamily planning template for property owners lists a "Your Go Bag Checklist" resident handout in its table of contents, but we read only the first 12 of its 79 pages and did not read that handout, so we do not describe it.{{cite:start-hud-multifamily-template}}

Editor's note So what follows is built this way: the official kit lists give the contents, and the choices about what to carry are ours and are labeled. A sensible way to think about it is that the {{link:three-day-kit|three-day kit}} is the household's stock and a go-bag is one person's portable share of it, sized for what a person can carry on foot.

Before you leave: the official list for the minutes you have

Ready.gov's evacuation page gives a short list for the moment of leaving. If time allows: call or email the out-of-state contact in your family communications plan and say where you are going; close and lock doors and windows; unplug small electrical equipment but leave freezers and refrigerators plugged in unless there is a risk of flooding; leave a note saying when you left and where you are going; wear sturdy shoes and protective clothing such as long pants, long sleeves and a hat; check with neighbors who may need a ride; and follow recommended routes without shortcuts, since they may be blocked.{{cite:start-ready-evacuation}} It also says: listen to a battery-powered radio for local instructions, leave early enough to avoid being trapped by severe weather, and do not drive into flooded areas.{{cite:start-ready-evacuation}}

FEMA's 2020 guide adds guidance for cars: keep a full tank if an evacuation seems likely and never let the tank drop below half full; plan to take only one car per family to reduce traffic.{{cite:ready-are-you-ready}} Ready.gov's evacuation page says the same about keeping a half tank at all times, because stations may be closed or unable to pump fuel during power outages.{{cite:start-ready-evacuation}} If you do not have a car, plan with family, friends or local emergency management how you would leave.{{cite:start-ready-evacuation}}

For the vehicle itself, Ready.gov's car page lists extras to keep in it: jumper cables, flares or a reflective triangle, an ice scraper, a car phone charger, a blanket, a map, and cat litter or sand for traction.{{cite:start-ready-car}}

Children

The CDC's emergency kit checklist for pregnant women, infants and children adds specific items for a young child (1 to 5 years): nutritious snacks, an age-appropriate water bottle, transportable activities such as coloring supplies, extra clothes and shoes, a play yard, potty-training supplies, and disposable feeding items.{{cite:start-cdc-family-kit}}

It also makes a point that is easy to skip. A child can be separated from you, so give them identification they cannot lose. The CDC suggests a durable emergency contact tag attached to a child's clothing or backpack, and a wristband or bracelet with the child's name and your contact information, and says to help older children memorize important names and phone numbers.{{cite:start-cdc-family-kit}} It lists, as well, personal identification and current photos of your family, including one of the whole family together, which can help with reunification.{{cite:start-cdc-family-kit}}

Editor's note A child can carry a small share, and should, because a child who has packed part of a bag is a child who knows where it is. Let them choose one comfort item. Practice the exercise at home: ask your child to find the out-of-state contact on the card in the backpack. Ready.gov's disability guidance for people with intellectual or developmental disabilities includes comfort snacks, charged handheld devices with videos and activities, and noise-canceling headphones,{{cite:start-ready-disability}} and the same ideas may help a child who finds a crowded shelter overwhelming. That second reading is ours, not Ready.gov's.

School matters too. FEMA's guide asks parents to find out how their children's school or daycare will communicate in a crisis, whether it can shelter in place, whether it has designated evacuation locations and whether it has plans to reunite families.{{cite:ready-are-you-ready}}

Babies and toddlers

The CDC publishes a separate emergency list for families with infants and young children, built around how the baby is fed.{{cite:start-cdc-infant-checklist}} The key points:

  • Breast milk. One or two boxes of disposable nursing pads, not reusable ones that have to be washed. If you rely on a pump: milk storage bags or containers, a small cooler with ice packs, the pump and its parts, power cords and extra batteries, and a manual pump in case of an outage.{{cite:start-cdc-infant-checklist}}
  • Formula. Ready-to-feed formula in single-serving cans or bottles. The CDC calls ready-to-feed the safest formula option in an emergency, because it needs no water and comes in sterile single-use containers. If you must use powder, the CDC lists the materials: formula containers, a liquid measuring cup, a food-grade container with a lid for mixing, bottled water, bottles and nipples.{{cite:start-cdc-infant-checklist}} Ready.gov says similarly to use ready-to-feed formula, and if you must mix, to use bottled water or boiled water as a last resort.{{cite:start-ready-food}}
  • For every baby. A diaper bag with at least one large pack of diapers, at least two packs of wipes, diaper rash cream, baby wash, and resealable gallon bags for dirty diapers and clothes; disposable cups, bibs and spoons; burp rags; at least two pacifiers if used; nonperishable baby food; a baby carrier; extra clothes and at least two blankets; infant acetaminophen pain reliever; an infant thermometer; the child's medical records including vaccinations; a portable crib; and bottled water.{{cite:start-cdc-infant-checklist}}

What to skip if you cannot clean it

Official guidance The CDC marks several items with an asterisk, such as pump parts, powdered-formula supplies, pacifiers and a bulb syringe, and says that if you cannot clean them with safe water and soap, do not use them.{{cite:start-cdc-infant-checklist}} The CDC also suggests a small camp stove, fuel and a pot for boiling water to make water safe and sanitize feeding supplies.{{cite:start-cdc-infant-checklist}} Cook outdoors only; see the {{link:three-day-kit|three-day kit guide}} for the cooking rules.

Babies grow, so a bag packed in spring may be wrong by autumn. The CDC says to check an infant's kit monthly to be sure you have enough formula for the baby's current needs for several days, and to replace formula that is nearly expired with newer supplies.{{cite:start-cdc-infant-checklist}}

Editor's note We would not carry the infant's whole kit as a go-bag. The diaper bag you already use is a natural base; stock it, and keep the larger items (portable crib, bulk formula) in the car or the home kit. If you are pregnant, the same CDC checklist has a separate list, including medications, extra water, ID photos, a plan agreed with your health care provider for where you will receive prenatal care or deliver if your provider's office or hospital is closed, and emergency birth supplies.{{cite:start-cdc-family-kit}} Talk to your clinician about that list; we have not reviewed it clinically.

Pets

Ready.gov's pet guidance says that if local officials ask you to evacuate, your pet should evacuate too, because pets left behind may end up lost, injured or worse. It asks you to have two kits, a larger one if you are sheltering in place and a lightweight version for evacuating.{{cite:ready-pets}}

Items it suggests include: several days of food in an airtight, waterproof container; a water bowl and several days' supply of water; extra regular medicine in a waterproof container; a first aid kit appropriate to your pet, which your veterinarian can advise on; a collar with ID tag and a harness or leash, plus a backup; copies of registration and other documents, in a waterproof container and available electronically; a crate or sturdy carrier, ideally one for each pet; grooming items; sanitation needs such as litter, paper towels and trash bags; a picture of you and your pet together; and familiar items such as toys or bedding.{{cite:ready-pets}}

The same page suggests a buddy system with a neighbor, friend or relative who can care for or evacuate your pets if you cannot, and microchipping with up-to-date contact information that includes an emergency contact outside your area.{{cite:ready-pets}} The CDC repeats the microchip advice.{{cite:start-cdc-pyh-connected}} Because many public shelters and hotels do not allow pets, find a pet-friendly destination in advance.{{cite:ready-pets}}{{cite:start-ready-evacuation}}

Ready.gov suggests making the carrier familiar by leaving it out and open, putting treats and a favorite blanket in it, and noting where your pets hide so you can find them quickly.{{cite:ready-pets}}

Uncertain Ready.gov gives no quantity of water or food per animal, and we will not invent one. Ask your veterinarian for a daily figure and write it on the bag. See {{link:water-math|water math}} for why this gap is real.

Mobility, disability and access needs

Ready.gov's guidance for people with disabilities treats the go-bag as part of a larger arrangement: a support network, a plan for accessible transport, and knowledge of what you will need that a shelter may not have. It asks you to plan ahead for the accessible transportation you may need for evacuation, checking with transit providers and the emergency management agency, and to plan how you will evacuate with assistive devices or replace them if lost.{{cite:start-ready-disability}} The CDC adds a warning: even if you plan for accessible transport, it may not be available in an emergency, so contact your paratransit provider or local government in advance to learn who provides it.{{cite:start-cdc-disability-evacuate}}

Specific items Ready.gov lists

  • Power wheelchair users: a lightweight manual chair as a backup if possible; show others how to assemble, disassemble and operate the chair; an extra battery, kept charged, or find out what agencies or charities might help buy one; a patch kit or sealant and an extra inner tube if the tires are not puncture-proof; a portable air pump; and an extra cushion if you use one to protect your skin or maintain your balance.{{cite:start-ready-disability}}
  • Cane or walker users: an extra mobility device if you use one.{{cite:start-ready-disability}}
  • Deaf or hard of hearing: a weather radio with text display and flashing alert, extra hearing-aid batteries, pen and paper, and a battery lantern so that sign language or lip reading remains possible in the dark.{{cite:start-ready-disability}}
  • Blind or low vision: Braille or large-print labels on supplies and a list of supplies kept on a flash drive or as an audio file; communication devices suited to your needs.{{cite:start-ready-disability}}
  • Speech disability: a plan for evacuating with an augmentative communication device or replacing it, including its model information and who paid for it; laminated cards with phrases or pictograms.{{cite:start-ready-disability}}
  • Alzheimer's and related dementia: do not leave the person alone; bring a pillow, blanket or other comforting item; at a shelter, stay away from exits and choose a quiet corner.{{cite:start-ready-disability}}

The CDC's kit guidance for people with disabilities includes batteries and portable chargers for adaptive equipment such as hearing aids, communication aids, at least one week's supply of prescription medications with a cooler for those that need to be chilled, medical supplies and health documents, and a contact list in a watertight container.{{cite:start-cdc-disability-kit}} Ready.gov also suggests a list of the style and serial number of your medical devices with instructions for operating them, copies of Medicare, Medicaid and insurance cards, and, for older adults, making sure at least one person in your support network has a key and knows where your supplies are.{{cite:start-ready-disability}}{{cite:start-ready-older-adults}}

The HHS Administration for Community Living offers two printable tools to put in the bag: a Medical Information Card for emergency responders, and a Communication Assistance Card for first responders helping a person whose disability makes it hard to communicate needs.{{cite:start-acl-emergency}}

Shelters and service animals: the CDC says shelters must be accessible for people with disabilities under the Americans with Disabilities Act, must allow service animals, and that under the Fair Housing Amendments Act disaster shelters are also required to allow emotional support animals. It suggests telling shelter workers your needs when you arrive and keeping health-needs information with you, including medical alert tags.{{cite:start-cdc-disability-evacuate}}

Editor's note The practical difficulty is weight and dependence. A spare battery, a manual chair and a cushion are not things a person can always carry alone, which is exactly why Ready.gov frames this as a network: someone who knows where the items are, has a key and can help you out of the building. Write down the order of who comes first, who is the backup, and what each one is responsible for carrying. A go-bag that depends on a person who is not home at that hour is a go-bag with a gap. For more on building that network and on apartments, see {{link:preparedness-real-households|preparedness in real households}}.

Keeping it ready

Where the bag is kept matters as much as what is in it: Ready.gov says everyone should know where the bag is and who is responsible for it.{{cite:start-ready-evacuation}} How often to check it is where the sources differ. The CDC's checklists for people with disabilities and for pregnancy, infants and children say every six months; FEMA's 2020 guide says to check needs every six months;{{cite:start-cdc-disability-kit}}{{cite:start-cdc-family-kit}}{{cite:ready-are-you-ready}} Ready.gov's current kit page says to re-think needs every year.{{cite:start-ready-kit}} Editor's note For go-bags, which hold clothes, medicines and a child's items that change, we would use the shorter interval, and the monthly check for a baby that the CDC recommends.{{cite:start-cdc-infant-checklist}}

Go-bags are also cheap to start. Ready.gov's low-cost advice for kits applies here: thrift stores for bags and gear, and older clothing that still fits.{{cite:start-ready-low-cost}} Editor's note A school backpack the child has outgrown, a duffel from a thrift shop, or a sturdy tote you already own is all a go-bag needs to be. No product is better than the one you will actually keep packed.

What this guide has and has not done

The contents come from the Ready.gov evacuation, kit, pets, disability, older-adult, food, car and low-cost pages, the Ready.gov printable checklist, the FEMA "Are You Ready?" guide (September 2020), the CDC infant and family checklists and disability pages, and the ACL page, each read on the date shown. We could find no official go-bag weight limit or standalone go-bag list, so the carrying notes, the idea of a portable share, and the packing order are editorial. We did not read the go-bag checklist in HUD's multifamily template, and we have not reviewed any pregnancy or infant item clinically. Evacuation advice depends heavily on the hazard (flood, wildfire, hurricane, chemical release), and we have not covered hazard-specific instructions; follow your local officials. We tested none of these bags with a real household.
