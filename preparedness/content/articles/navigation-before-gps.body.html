Read this first

This is a history of how people found their way, plus an honest account of where those methods stop being reliable. It does not teach you to navigate by the stars or the sun, and it should not be used as a substitute for a map, a compass you know how to use, and a plan someone else knows about.

  • The National Weather Service's winter-travel list includes a compass and road maps, with the reminder not to depend on mobile devices that have limited battery life.{{cite:hist-field-nws-winter-before}}
  • A compass does not point to true north in most places. The difference, called declination, changes with where you are and with the year, so check it for your location and date.{{cite:hist-field-usgs-declination}}{{cite:hist-field-noaa-declination}}
  • Tell someone where you are going and what route you will take. If something happens, they will know where a search should start.{{cite:hist-field-nws-winter-before}}

If you are lost, injured or in danger, call your emergency number (911 in the United States). Do not rely on any method on this page to rescue yourself from a situation that needs help.

Two questions: which way, and where?

Finding your way involves two different problems. One is direction: which way is north, and which way am I heading. The other is position: where exactly am I on the map. A traveler with a good direction and a poor sense of position can walk a straight line into a river. For centuries the sea made the second problem deadly.

The Royal Museums Greenwich opens its account of the search for longitude with the human cost. Navigational errors cost lives in shipwrecks, and long voyages killed crews through disease, most notably scurvy.{{cite:hist-field-rmg-why-longitude}} On the evening of 22 October 1707, five of Admiral Sir Cloudesley Shovell's ships struck the rocks off the Isles of Scilly. Within hours four went down, and more than 1,400 men died, including the admiral. RMG says navigational errors were the cause.Documented history{{cite:hist-field-rmg-why-longitude}}

The museum adds a careful point that popular versions leave out. There is no evidence of a public outcry after the disaster urging government action, and it seems the Longitude Act of 1714 came instead from lobbying by mathematicians.{{cite:hist-field-rmg-why-longitude}} The Act, passed on 9 July 1714, offered a public reward of £20,000 for a method of finding longitude at sea, a sum RMG says would be worth at least £1.5 million today.{{cite:hist-field-rmg-why-longitude}} Spain had begun the practice in 1567, and the States of Holland and other governments followed.{{cite:hist-field-rmg-why-longitude}}

Editor's note A reward that large is a good measure of how little the existing methods were trusted. The records we read describe the longitude problem as unsolved at the time; they do not say how any individual ship fared.

Before the answer: tries that did not work

Galileo found the moons of Jupiter in 1610 and realized they could work as a clock visible from different places on Earth. He tried to persuade the Spanish and then the Dutch to invest in the method. RMG says the practicalities were overwhelming. Even a century later, Newton noted that, because of the length of telescope needed and the motion of a ship at sea, those eclipses could not yet be observed there.Documented history{{cite:hist-field-rmg-why-longitude}}

That is a good first lesson. A method can be correct in principle and still useless in the place you most need it. This is true of several methods below.

The first answer: a clock that kept time at sea

The clock method is simple to state. If you know the time at a fixed reference point and you can measure the local time where you are, the difference tells you how far east or west you have come. The difficulty was building a clock that would keep time aboard a pitching ship. RMG says John Harrison set out to make a portable clock that kept time to within three seconds a day, far better than the best watches of the time.{{cite:hist-field-rmg-harrison}}

His first marine timekeeper, H1, was taken to Lisbon and back aboard Royal Navy ships in 1736. On the return trip, RMG says, Harrison announced that a headland the officers believed was the Start was in fact the Lizard, which meant the ship was 60 miles off course. He was correct.Documented history{{cite:hist-field-rmg-harrison}} His fourth, H4, was tried on voyages to Jamaica in 1761 and to Barbados in 1764. In February 1765 the Board of Longitude confirmed that it had kept time within the most stringent limits of the 1714 Act.{{cite:hist-field-rmg-harrison}}

The sources are unusually frank that this was not the end of the story. The Board recommended paying £10,000 on a demonstration of H4's principles, with the remaining £10,000 awarded only once other makers could produce similar timekeepers, because the Board needed to be sure Harrison's was not a one-off. The Harrisons felt that the full reward was already due under the Act and that the Commissioners had changed the rules. Further testing at the Royal Observatory from 1766 lasted ten months, and RMG says H4 "did not perform well" in it, a result Maskelyne published and Harrison challenged.Uncertain{{cite:hist-field-rmg-harrison}}

RMG says plainly that, although Harrison is remembered for having "solved" longitude, marine chronometers were at first very expensive and the roll-out was slow. Cheaper, simplified designs allowed wider adoption only at the start of the 1800s.{{cite:hist-field-rmg-harrison}}

The second answer: measuring the Moon

The rival approach used no clock at all. It used the sky as one, by measuring the angle between the Moon and a star or the Sun and looking up the result in tables. RMG describes the astronomer Tobias Mayer's lunar tables and says James Bradley concluded that, with a good instrument, they could give a ship's longitude to within 1 degree. The instrument developed in sea trials from a repeating circle became, in RMG's account, the first marine sextant.{{cite:hist-field-rmg-lunar}} The Smithsonian holds a sextant by Jesse Ramsden from the last quarter of the eighteenth century and notes that the sextant became the symbol of navigation. It is named for its 60-degree scale, one sixth of a circle.{{cite:hist-field-si-sextant}}

In 1761 Nevil Maskelyne tested the method on the voyage to St Helena. By the time they anchored, he reported, his longitude by lunars was only about 1½ degrees in error, against errors of up to 10 degrees from dead reckoning on the same voyage.Documented history{{cite:hist-field-rmg-lunar}} Editor's note Dead reckoning means working out where you are from your starting point, your heading and how far you think you have travelled.

The catch was labour. Maskelyne told the Royal Society that anyone with enough time and ability could work out longitude at sea, but RMG says the calculations were "decidedly lengthy." Pre-computed tables fixed that problem, and Maskelyne created the Nautical Almanac to supply them, with the observations processed by assistants known as computers, whose work RMG describes as tedious and poorly paid. Mary Edwards is named as one of the few women among them.{{cite:hist-field-rmg-lunar}}

Even then, RMG says, the new methods were not quickly or fully adopted. Marine timekeepers stayed rare and expensive, and it took decades before seafarers trusted them. Methods that needed mathematical ability and patience were arguably harder to spread. One thing became clear quickly: dead reckoning would not be abandoned any time soon, and people interested in the new techniques might use all methods rather than choose one.Documented history{{cite:hist-field-rmg-lunar}}

How much to trust the numbers

The figures above (3 seconds a day, within 1 degree, 1½ degrees against 10) come from one museum's account of specific tests and design aims in the eighteenth century. They are not a statement of how accurate navigation was in general, how accurate your own readings would be, or how those angles convert into distance on the ground. The RMG pages we read do not make that conversion and we do not either. If a modern source ever claims a star or Moon method is accurate to some distance, ask what instrument, what conditions and what training stand behind it.

On land: the compass and the thing it does not do

For a traveler on land, the compass is the oldest widely carried tool. It also has an old, well-documented weakness. The U.S. Geological Survey says that at most places on the Earth's surface the compass does not point exactly toward geographic north, and that the difference, declination, has been "a nuisance to navigators for centuries" because it varies with both location and time. At very high latitudes, the page adds, a compass can even point south.Official guidance{{cite:hist-field-usgs-declination}}

NOAA's National Centers for Environmental Information explains the mechanics. Declination is the angle between magnetic north and true north, counted positive east of true north and negative west. A compass does not point at a single spot such as the magnetic pole. It points along the direction of the local magnetic field. To get a true bearing from a compass bearing, NOAA says you add the declination to the magnetic bearing, provided you follow its convention that west is negative, and it provides a calculator for any place and date.Official guidance{{cite:hist-field-noaa-declination}}

The same problem sits inside paper maps. A diagram at the bottom of most USGS topographic maps shows true north, grid north and magnetic north and the angles between them, though some maps, especially very old ones, do not have it. The magnetic arrow shows where a compass would have pointed when the map was published, and the page notes that this changes over time and with position.Official guidance{{cite:hist-field-usgs-north-arrows}} USGS also says the collar of its topographic maps states the declination at the center of the map in the year it was made.{{cite:hist-field-usgs-declination}}

Editor's note Put those statements together and the practical point is this. A map and compass are a system, and the system has a date on it. An old map and a modern compass will not agree about north unless you correct for the years in between.

What a nineteenth-century handbook assumed

The Library of Congress holds a digitized copy of Captain Randolph B. Marcy's The Prairie Traveler, a handbook for overland expeditions "published by authority of the War Department" and aimed at emigrants and others crossing the continent. The LOC copy is the 1861 Harper & Brothers printing. Its preface says it draws on a quarter-century of frontier experience and on the official reports of army topographical engineers for its descriptions of the routes.Documented history{{cite:loc-prairie-traveler}}

The title page promises "maps, illustrations, and itineraries," and the two pages of the contents list describe seven chapters: routes, wagons and provisions, marching and water, river crossings, packing, camp life, guides, and hunting.{{cite:loc-prairie-traveler}} None of the chapter descriptions mentions finding direction by the sun, stars or compass. We read the title page, preface and contents, not the chapters, so that is a fact about the contents list and not a claim about the book: we do not know what its chapters say on compasses or stars. The companion guide on {{link:pioneer-supply-chains-failed|how pioneer supply chains failed}} shows what happened to one party when that route knowledge turned out to be wrong.

The night sky: what it can and cannot tell you

NASA's public-engagement page on the North Star is the clearest official statement we found. Polaris is not the brightest star in the sky but is usually easy to spot. It sits close to the point in the sky where Earth's rotation axis points. Because it is so close to that point, it traces only a small circle over a night and so stays in roughly the same place, which makes it a reliable way to find the direction of north in the Northern Hemisphere. NASA says that the direction is true north, not magnetic north, and that the two end stars of the Big Dipper's cup point to it.Official guidance{{cite:hist-field-nasa-north-star}}

The same page states the limits. Polaris is near the pole, not exactly at it. The Southern Hemisphere has no bright star marking the south celestial pole, and people there use other methods such as the stars of the Southern Cross. The pole star also changes over thousands of years because of a wobble in Earth's axis of about 26,000 years.{{cite:hist-field-nasa-north-star}}

Editor's note Read what that statement does and does not promise. It promises a way to find the direction of north on a clear night, in one hemisphere. It gives no figure for how close the result is to true north, says nothing about finding position, and does not claim it works in cloud, forest cover or in the first moments of panic. We will not supply an accuracy that the page does not.

Sun and shadow methods: what we could not verify

Popular survival lore includes sun-and-shadow tricks for finding direction. We searched for a government agency or museum source that specifies such a method together with its accuracy and found none that we could read and verify. We therefore describe no sun or shadow method here and make no claim about how well any of them works.Uncertain That is a statement about our evidence, not a finding that the methods fail. Current safety verdict

Safe only under stated conditions

The tools are sound; the conditions matter. None of these methods is a reason to leave a road, a trail or a marked route without a plan. The verdict is by method.

  • Paper map and compass: consistent with current official guidance as a backup, with one condition: you must correct for declination and know the date of your map. The National Weather Service lists a compass and road maps in its winter-travel kit and warns against depending on devices with limited battery life.{{cite:hist-field-nws-winter-before}}{{cite:hist-field-noaa-declination}}{{cite:hist-field-usgs-north-arrows}}
  • Polaris for direction: context-dependent. NASA calls it a reliable way to find the direction of north in the Northern Hemisphere, which means a clear night, a view of the sky and the right hemisphere. It gives no accuracy figure.{{cite:hist-field-nasa-north-star}}
  • Sun and shadow methods: not validated here. We found no official or museum source to check them against.
  • Lunar distances, sextants and chronometers: historical and specialist. They worked for trained navigators with almanacs, tables and long practice, and are not something to attempt from a short description.{{cite:hist-field-rmg-lunar}}
  • Leaving your route and telling no one: not recommended. Tell someone your route and timetable, and call for help early.{{cite:hist-field-nws-winter-before}}

Then: skill, tables and trust

Documented history Navigators combined dead reckoning with a compass, lunar tables, a sextant and, once they could afford one, a chronometer. Each method had known failures, and no one method won quickly.{{cite:hist-field-rmg-lunar}}{{cite:hist-field-rmg-harrison}}

Now: a system with a plan

Official guidance Carry a paper map and a compass, know the declination for your area and the age of your map, tell someone your route, and do not depend on a phone alone.{{cite:hist-field-nws-winter-before}}{{cite:hist-field-usgs-declination}}

What we have not done

This guide leans on three Royal Museums Greenwich stories for the history of longitude and on U.S. agency pages for declination and Polaris. We have not read Harrison's, Mayer's or Maskelyne's own publications, the Board of Longitude's papers, or the 1714 Act itself. The Smithsonian pages are short exhibit captions. We read the title page, preface and contents of Marcy's handbook but not its chapters, so we make no statement about its advice on compasses, stars or shadows.

We did not cover magnetic compass deviation caused by iron on a ship or in a vehicle, map projections, GPS itself, or Indigenous and Polynesian wayfinding traditions, because we have not yet read sources we are willing to cite for them and we do not want to generalize about peoples we have not read. We have no image of a compass, chart or sextant that we have the rights to publish, so this page has none.
