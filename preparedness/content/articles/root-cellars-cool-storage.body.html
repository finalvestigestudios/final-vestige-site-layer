Read this first

This is mostly a history, with one practical thread. Cool storage of whole, sound garden produce is one of the oldest ways to stretch a harvest, and university Extension still publishes guidance for it. It is not a way to keep meat, dairy, leftovers or anything cooked.

  • University of Missouri Extension describes root-cellar storage for vegetables and fruit as a matter of temperature, humidity and what you store together. It says not to let produce freeze, and not to store fruits with vegetables.{{cite:hist-home-mu-root-cellars}}
  • For perishable foods, the USDA's benchmark is a refrigerator at 40 °F or below. A cellar is a different tool.{{cite:hist-home-fsis-refrigeration}}

If you or someone in your home has stomach illness, fever or vomiting after eating stored food and is seriously ill, call your emergency number or poison control, not this page. If you are unsure whether a stored food is safe, the CDC's rule for home-preserved food is simple: if in doubt, throw it out.{{cite:cdc-botulism-prevention}}

The problem a cellar solved

Most gardens produce their food in a few weeks and the household has to eat for months. Before refrigerators, a family could dry food, salt it, pickle it, or keep it cold. Of those, keeping it cold was the only one that left the food close to the way it came out of the ground.

The National Park Service puts the idea in a sentence at its Oxon Hill Farm site in Maryland. The root cellar there was one of the original buildings put up by the DeButts family, built in 1830 and set partially underground "to keep its contents cool." The Park Service adds that, before refrigeration, root cellars stored starchy vegetables that could last over the winter without spoiling.Documented history{{cite:hist-home-nps-oxon-hill-root-cellar}} The wider Park Service account of home preservation in the 1940s says the same about root vegetables generally: they stored well in cool, dark places like root cellars, or even stored in the ground over winter.{{cite:hist-home-nps-ww2-canning}}

Editor's note Both accounts are short and describe what the structure was for. Neither gives losses, temperatures or the foods that failed. We treat them as evidence that the practice was common and valued, not as evidence about how well any cellar performed.

Why underground works

The Oxon Hill description is the whole principle: put the room partly in the ground and the ground does the work.{{cite:hist-home-nps-oxon-hill-root-cellar}} Our sources do not explain the physics, and we do not invent an explanation for them. What the Extension guidance gives instead is the target the cellar has to hit.

University of Missouri Extension's quick-reference chart sorts crops into groups. Most root crops and the cabbage family belong in cold and very moist storage. Potatoes want cold and moist, with a note not to store them with fruits. Onions, garlic and dry beans are cool and dry. A few crops are different again: sweet potatoes want warm and moist storage and the chart warns not to let them drop below 50 °F, and winter squash and pumpkins want it warm and dry.Official guidance{{cite:hist-home-mu-root-cellars}}

Read the table for what it shows about the design problem. A single cellar room cannot be cold, very moist, warm and dry at the same time. Households that stored all of these foods had to find different places: a damper corner, a drier shelf, a warmer spot near the house. The Extension publication does not tell you how to arrange that. It says it is a quick reference and points to other references for building and using a cellar.{{cite:hist-home-mu-root-cellars}} Those references are two books, published in 1979 and 1982, so we do not rely on them here.

Three other rules from the same page matter as much as the numbers. Do not let fruits and vegetables freeze. Do not store fruits and vegetables together even when the temperatures match, because ripening apples and pears give off ethylene gas, which decreases the storage life of vegetables, and potatoes stored near certain fruits sprout early. And keep strong-smelling vegetables such as turnips and cabbage away from other food, in a place where the odor cannot waft into the house.{{cite:hist-home-mu-root-cellars}}

Finally, how long a crop keeps is not a fixed number. The chart's note says it depends on whether the crop matured early or late, on storage conditions (less than ideal conditions shorten storage life), and on the condition of the produce when it went in: proper curing of damage-free produce lasts longer.{{cite:hist-home-mu-root-cellars}}

Editor's note That third point is the one old accounts leave out. A cellar did not make a poor crop keep. It slowed the clock for sound produce put in at the right time. Anyone reading "it lasted all winter" should add "if it went in sound."

What a 1798 cook book says about the cellar

Amelia Simmons's American Cookery, in the 1798 printing transcribed by Michigan State University Libraries, is an instruction book rather than a diary, so it records what a cook was told to do, not what households managed. It still shows the cellar in daily use. Potatoes, it says, should be dug before the autumn rains, well dried in the sun, "kept from frost and dampness" through the winter, and moved from the cellar to a dry loft in spring, where they are spread thin and stirred, or they will grow and be spoiled for cooking.Documented history{{cite:hist-home-msu-simmons-1798}} Butter packed in May is to be set in the coldest part of the cellar or in the ice-house, and preserved fruit in a cool, dry cellar.{{cite:hist-home-msu-simmons-1798}}

The book also records the opposite of cellar storage for parsnips: they may be kept almost anywhere that avoids heat and frost, and are richer in flavor if left in the ground over the winter and dug in April, though they do not last long after.{{cite:hist-home-msu-simmons-1798}} That matches the Extension chart's list of crops that can stay in the ground under mulch, and the cook's remark that they "will not last long after" is a reminder that some storage is a timing trick, not a preservation method.{{cite:hist-home-mu-root-cellars}}

Editor's note One detail differs in emphasis. The 1798 text says to keep potatoes from dampness, while the Extension chart describes cold and moist storage at a relative humidity of 80 to 90 percent for them. Those are not necessarily in conflict, because wet and humid are different conditions, but we have no source that reconciles the two and we do not try to. The practical point is the same one the chart makes: the target is a range, and a cellar can miss it on either side.

Or leave it where it grew

The same chart lists a few crops, horseradish, Jerusalem artichoke and salsify among them, that may be left in the ground undisturbed until needed, with digging possible unless the soil is frozen hard, and says a thick layer of mulch may extend the harvest season.{{cite:hist-home-mu-root-cellars}} This matches the Park Service note about root vegetables stored in the ground over winter.{{cite:hist-home-nps-ww2-canning}} The oldest cold store is the garden itself.

The other cold: ice and snow

A root cellar keeps cool by being cool. An ice house keeps things cold by being a store of winter. The two are not the same thing, and the records of the second show what it cost.

The Thomas Jefferson Foundation's account of the Monticello ice house says Jefferson designed it to store ice or snow, harvested from the Rivanna River, for refrigerating fresh dairy and meats. It was built by enslaved craftsmen under the direction of an Irish house joiner, and filling it needed freezing weather and a labor force of enslaved workers and hired wagoners to cut ice and haul it up the mountain. Jefferson recorded that it took 62 wagon loads to fill, at a cost of $70 for the hire of wagons and food and drink for the drivers.Documented history{{cite:hist-home-monticello-ice-house}}

The record also shows the losses. At the Italian ice houses Jefferson visited, his note records, about a third of the ice was lost by melting. A record dated September 14, 1810 notes "the ice in the ice house fails," and in 1815 the ice lasted until October 15.{{cite:hist-home-monticello-ice-house}} The Foundation notes the ice house may have held more than ice: it quotes a description of another ice cellar, where "dishes of butter, cold dressed provisions, sallads" were kept, and says Monticello's may have been used in a similar way.{{cite:hist-home-monticello-ice-house}}

The National Park Service records the same practice at the other end of the country's history, at Fort Union in New Mexico. Its first fort had two subterranean ice houses topped with low roofs. Small dams were built in the creek, and when the water froze in winter, troops cut blocks of ice out of the ponds and hauled them to the ice houses. The ice provided for food preservation, cold drinks and even ice cream in the summer.Documented history{{cite:hist-home-nps-fort-union-water}}

Editor's note Taken together, the records describe cold that was seasonal, expensive and leaky. A third lost to melting in one account, a store that failed in September in another, and a team of wagons for a single fill. That is not a failure of the people who built them. It is simply what a store of winter looks like in July, and it is a good reason not to confuse an ice house with a refrigerator.

The records also remind us who did the work. Monticello's account of its ice house states that enslaved craftsmen built it and that enslaved laborers took part in harvesting the ice.{{cite:hist-home-monticello-ice-house}} Editor's note The convenience of cold storage on a large estate was built on labor that was not freely given.

Why a cellar is not a refrigerator

The USDA's Food Safety and Inspection Service says that bacteria grow most rapidly between 40 and 140 °F, a range it calls the danger zone, and that some double in number in as little as 20 minutes. It advises keeping the refrigerator at 40 °F or below, using an appliance thermometer, and not eating food held above 40 °F for more than two hours.Official guidance{{cite:hist-home-fsis-refrigeration}}

Notice how the two sets of numbers sit side by side. The Extension chart gives a target range for whole produce, with an upper end at 40 °F for the coldest crops. The USDA rule is for perishable foods, with a line at 40 °F and a clock of two hours above it.{{cite:hist-home-mu-root-cellars}}{{cite:hist-home-fsis-refrigeration}} A cellar's temperature changes with the seasons, and none of our sources say how a given cellar behaves across a year.

Editor's note This is our reasoning, not a statement from either agency: a space that holds whole produce acceptably is not thereby fit for cooked food, meat or dairy, because the produce guidance does not claim to protect those foods. If a cellar's temperature is not measured, you do not know which side of 40 °F it is on. A thermometer is the difference between a guess and a reading, and FSIS makes the same point about refrigerators.

If you do not have a cellar

Most households in 2026 do not, and renters in particular may have no space they can change. The Extension chart groups cool and dry storage for dry beans, onions, garlic and similar foods separately, and notes that home and commercially prepared foods also need a cool, dry storage place.{{cite:hist-home-mu-root-cellars}} The University lists two companion publications, on storing food in the cupboard and in the refrigerator.{{cite:hist-home-mu-root-cellars}} We have not read those, and we do not summarize them. The main practical lesson of the cellar for a modern apartment is the one on the chart: different foods want different conditions, so storing them all in one place compromises some of them.Editor's note

Current safety verdict

Safe only under stated conditions

Cool storage of sound whole produce is consistent with current Extension guidance when the conditions are met. Nothing else on this page is validated.

  • Whole, sound, properly cured produce: storage in the temperature and humidity ranges Extension publishes, with no freezing, and fruit kept apart from vegetables.{{cite:hist-home-mu-root-cellars}}
  • Meat, dairy, cooked and other perishable food: not covered by the produce guidance. Use refrigeration at 40 °F or below with a thermometer.{{cite:hist-home-fsis-refrigeration}}
  • Anything home-preserved that you cannot vouch for: do not rely on a cool room to make it safe. If in doubt, throw it out.{{cite:cdc-botulism-prevention}}
  • Ice houses as models for modern food storage: not validated. The records show storage that failed in summer.{{cite:hist-home-monticello-ice-house}}

We found no source that evaluates unmonitored home cellars for food safety. That is a limit of what we read.

What we have not done

We read one Extension publication in full, a quick-reference chart, and one USDA page on refrigeration last updated in 2015. We did not read a second Extension source to see whether other states give different ranges for the same crops, and the Missouri page's own references are books from 1979 and 1982. We did not find a source on constructing a cellar, on ventilation, or on what mold, rodents or flooding do to stored produce, and we say nothing about them.

The historical accounts are short interpretive pages from the National Park Service and the Thomas Jefferson Foundation. We did not read Jefferson's papers, the Oxon Hill site's records, or any diary of a household that kept a cellar; the 1798 cook book we cite is an instruction book, and we read it by keyword search and in the cellar-related passages, not cover to cover. We found no figures for how much produce households lost from storage. We do not discuss ground storage techniques, which belong to sources we have not read. We have no image of a cellar that we have the rights to publish, so this page has none.
