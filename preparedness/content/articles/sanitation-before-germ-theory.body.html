Read this first

This is a history, not a hygiene manual. It describes how ships, forts and towns tried to stay healthy before anyone knew that germs cause infectious disease, and what the surviving records can and cannot tell us. It describes no fumigation, disinfection or waste-handling method, and it should not be used to improvise one.

  • Washing hands with soap and clean, running water is one of the best ways to stay healthy and prevents the spread of respiratory and diarrheal infections. If soap and water are not available, the CDC says to use a hand sanitizer with at least 60% alcohol.{{cite:hist-home-cdc-clean-hands}}
  • If your water or sewage service fails, follow your local health department. For the water side, see {{link:water-advisories-explained|what drinking-water advisories mean}} and {{link:making-water-safer|making water safer in an emergency}}.
  • Old rules like "keep the air fresh" and "keep it clean" were attached to a wrong theory of how disease spreads. Some of the practices helped for other reasons; others may not have. We say which where the sources let us.

If someone in your home has severe diarrhea, vomiting, fever or signs of dehydration, call a health professional. If the person is seriously ill, call your emergency number.

The theory everybody used

For most of the nineteenth century, the dominant explanation of epidemic disease was not germs. It was bad air. A recent scholarly review of the public health reformer Edwin Chadwick describes the theory in a sentence: miasma theory held that ailments such as cholera were spread by "foul air" or miasmas originating from decomposing organic matter.Documented history{{cite:hist-home-chadwick-cureus}}

The theory was wrong about the mechanism and, as it happens, useful in practice. The same review notes that although germ theory had not yet been established, miasma theory encouraged efforts to clean up urban environments as a method of disease prevention.{{cite:hist-home-chadwick-cureus}} If you believe disease rises from rot and filth, you remove rot and filth, and removing rot and filth sometimes removes the real cause too.

Editor's note That is the shape of this whole subject. People were not foolish. They had a theory that fit what they could observe, they acted on it with energy, and the theory was incomplete in ways that sometimes mattered and sometimes did not. The honest question to ask of each old practice is whether it worked, and the answer is not always the same as whether the reason given for it was right.

Aboard ship: "cleanse or die"

A warship is a hard case for any theory of health. The crew lives close together below decks, in a damp, enclosed space, for months. A scholarly study of British naval hygiene by Elise Juzda Smith, published in Medical History in 2018, begins by describing the Royal Navy's reputation for cleanliness. The continuous washing and fumigating of the decks may have been a ritual of order and discipline for officers and sailors, the study says, but surgeons upheld it as a life-saving necessity. In 1846 the surgeon Norman Chevers half-jokingly suggested that the Navy's motto should be "cleanse or die!"Documented history{{cite:hist-home-smith-naval-hygiene}}

The study's main subject is the steam-age Navy of 1840 to 1900, but it traces the practice back to the eighteenth century, and that earlier history is what we use here. From the late eighteenth century the recommendations of maritime health authorities, among them James Lind, Thomas Trotter and Gilbert Blane, led the Admiralty to reform sanitation and ventilation, diet and drink, clothing and bedding, and exercise and rest. The goal was a clean, dry and well-ventilated environment in spaces that were invariably overcrowded, damp and enclosed.{{cite:hist-home-smith-naval-hygiene}} Admiralty instructions throughout the nineteenth century repeatedly reminded surgeons that "dryness, cleanliness, and ventilation" were the preferred means of preventing disease.{{cite:hist-home-smith-naval-hygiene}}

The theory behind the instructions was the miasma view. The study says noxious emanations from decomposing animal or vegetable matter were thought responsible for infectious illness, and gives an 1819 Mariner's Medical Guide in which "unwholesome effluvias" were thought to come from spoilt cargo, dirty bilge water, and the general humidity and filthiness of the sailors' living quarters.{{cite:hist-home-smith-naval-hygiene}} The same study notes that the competing contagionist model, which argued for the role of human contact in spreading sickness, always had adherents, and that naval surgeons tended to favor the sanitarian position.{{cite:hist-home-smith-naval-hygiene}}

What did it achieve? The author's assessment is balanced. She writes that the benefits of prioritizing sanitary and dietary concerns were particularly shown during James Cook's exploratory voyages of the 1770s, which boasted such low levels of illness and death that the value of preventive medicine was, in her phrase, established beyond all doubt.Documented history But she also concludes that it proved almost impossible to eradicate sickness at sea, that the enclosed nature of naval vessels showed the limits of enforcing absolute environmental controls, and that the efforts did produce a decline in casualties in the second half of the nineteenth century.{{cite:hist-home-smith-naval-hygiene}}

The Royal Museums Greenwich offers a complementary point about the other half of shipboard health. In its overview of life at sea in the age of sail, poor storage, poor ventilation and vermin are among the reasons food deteriorated, and poor diet caused malnutrition and diseases such as scurvy.{{cite:rmg-life-at-sea}} That is a reminder that a sanitation program on a ship was fighting on several fronts, not all of which were about filth. For the dietary one, see {{link:salt-beef-salt-pork-age-of-sail|salt beef and salt pork at sea}}.

Editor's note A crude summary of the evidence: cleaning and airing a crowded ship probably helped, and the records do not let us say how much, or through which mechanism. The surviving statistics were compiled by surgeons who believed in the program. The study we read describes disagreement among practitioners about the causes of disease, which is a polite way of saying the data were being read through a theory.

On land: the garrison and the pit

Ships left good records because the Navy counted everything. Households did not. The best land-based picture we found is institutional. The National Park Service's account of the water supply at Fort Union in New Mexico describes how a nineteenth-century army post managed what we now call sanitation.Documented history{{cite:hist-home-nps-fort-union-water}}

Indoor plumbing arrived at Fort Union in the mid-1880s, but, the page says, bathroom facilities "remained rudimentary," and bathing facilities were "woefully inadequate" for a garrison of 200 to 300 troops. A private's letter home describes taking a Saturday-evening bath in a tub borrowed from a laundress. Officers and their families relied on chamber pots and privies. The garrison used what the page calls "sinks," which were basically very large pits, and the page describes this as an improvement over the second fort, where the ditch around the fort and nearby fields were used instead.{{cite:hist-home-nps-fort-union-water}}

The same page describes a water-and-drainage failure that no theory of air explains. The second fort's drainage was poor, its barracks partly below ground level were prone to flooding, and after a severe storm in 1866 the troops moved out of the fort into tents.{{cite:hist-home-nps-fort-union-water}} The page does not say that anyone fell ill in connection with these arrangements, and we do not claim they did.Uncertain

Editor's note These are the conditions the sanitary reformers were reacting to elsewhere: waste disposed of near where people lived and drank, little water for washing, and no running system to carry it away. The page gives a vivid picture of an ordinary place doing its best. It is silent on cause and effect, and we keep it that way.

Chadwick and the argument that filth could be prevented

In 1842, Edwin Chadwick published his "Report on the Sanitary Condition of the Labouring Population." The scholarly review we read says it documented the conditions faced by the working class and the link between poor sanitation and disease, and that he argued illness and death were not unavoidable results of poverty but could be largely prevented through appropriate hygiene measures: sewage systems, clean water and proper waste disposal.Documented history{{cite:hist-home-chadwick-cureus}}

The politics were as important as the idea. The review notes that the report's proposed improvements had one major limitation, their cost, which brought Chadwick into conflict with influential people reluctant to spend money on the poor. The government of 1842 effectively rejected the report, and the position persisted until 1847. A Public Health Act in 1848 then established local health boards and empowered local authorities to build sewers, provide clean water and regulate waste disposal.{{cite:hist-home-chadwick-cureus}}

The review also credits Chadwick's emphasis on clean water, efficient sewage systems and waste management with reducing the spread of diseases like cholera and typhoid.{{cite:hist-home-chadwick-cureus}} That is a large causal claim made in a general-audience narrative review in an open-access journal, and we treat it as the review's judgment rather than a measured result.Uncertain We did not read the 1842 report itself.

Snow's challenge to the bad-air theory

While Chadwick was working from the premise of foul air, another London doctor was running the numbers that would undo it. John Snow's cholera work did not prove germs. It demonstrated, by counting, that the water people drank mattered more than the air they breathed. In one comparison of households supplied by two water companies, deaths in the first four weeks among customers of the company drawing from a sewage-contaminated part of the Thames were 14 times those among customers of a company whose intake was upstream.Documented history{{cite:hist-home-tulchinsky-snow}} Snow described the comparison in his own text as an experiment "on the grandest scale."{{cite:hist-home-snow-mode-of-communication}}

The Wellcome Collection's catalogue entry for Snow's report to the St James parish vestry shows how broad the inquiry was. The report covers cholera outbreaks in central London in 1832, 1848 to 1849, 1851, 1852, 1853 and 1854, with a public pump in Broad Street prominent, and it includes meteorological conditions and information about population, housing, sanitation, sewerage, cess pools and the water supply of Soho.{{cite:hist-home-wellcome-snow-map}} In other words, the record of a pump and a map sits inside a wider sanitary survey of a neighborhood.

The full account of Snow, the pump and the water companies is in {{link:water-purification-through-history|water purification through history}}. What matters here is the logical consequence. If water carries the disease, then a sanitation program that works on air can succeed by accident, and one that works on water can succeed by design.

Current safety verdict

Safe only under stated conditions

The goals of the old sanitation programs were sound; their methods were chosen for a wrong theory, and we cannot tell you which of them worked.

  • Keeping living spaces clean and dry: consistent in spirit with current practice, though the sources we read do not measure its effect.{{cite:hist-home-smith-naval-hygiene}}
  • Clean water and proper waste disposal: the heart of the reform movement and the subject of current guidance. Use current agency instructions, not historical ones.{{cite:hist-home-chadwick-cureus}}{{cite:cdc-water-safe}}
  • Handwashing: validated by the CDC as one of the best ways to stay healthy. Wet, lather, scrub for at least 20 seconds, rinse and dry.{{cite:hist-home-cdc-clean-hands}}
  • Fumigation and air-based measures as disease prevention: not validated. The sources we read name the practice, but none evaluates it, and we give no method.

Where a historical practice and a current agency instruction disagree, follow the agency.

Then: wash, air and dry the ship; clean up the town

Documented history Sailors scrubbed and fumigated decks; surgeons demanded dryness and ventilation; a town reformer argued for sewers, clean water and waste disposal. All of it rested on the belief that foul air carried disease.{{cite:hist-home-smith-naval-hygiene}}{{cite:hist-home-chadwick-cureus}}

Now: break the route of infection

Official guidance Wash hands with soap and clean, running water, and use clean water for drinking. In a failure of services, follow your health department's instructions.{{cite:hist-home-cdc-clean-hands}}{{cite:cdc-water-safe}} See {{link:water-advisories-explained|water advisories explained}}.

What we have not done

We read one scholarly study of British naval hygiene, but only its opening sections, which cover 1840 to 1900 and therefore describe the steam age more than the age of sail. We read one narrative review of Chadwick's career, not his report. We read a national park's account of a single fort and an archive's catalogue entry for Snow's report. We did not read any account of ordinary household sanitation: privies, cesspits, wells and their placement, or the habits of families.

We tried to retrieve Wellcome Collection records for Chadwick's report and Gilbert Blane's Observations on the Diseases of Seamen. The site returned a rate-limit error, so we did not read them and do not cite them; they are recorded in our notes for a later pass. We also found no source that evaluates fumigation or measures the effect of deck washing. The Cureus review is a secondary summary in a general journal and we use it only for broad outline.

We have no image of a ship's deck, a privy or a pump handle that we have the rights to publish, so this page has none.
