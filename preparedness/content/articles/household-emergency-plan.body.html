What to do now

  1. Sit down once with everyone in your household and answer five questions: how will we get emergency alerts, where do we shelter, what is our evacuation route, how do we reach each other, and is our kit up to date? Those five are Ready.gov's own starting questions.{{cite:start-ready-plan}}
  2. Write the answers on one page and give everyone a copy to carry. Ready.gov offers a free fillable form and wallet card; the 2020 FEMA guide says everyone should carry a digital or wallet copy and a copy should be posted somewhere central at home.{{cite:start-ready-plan}}{{cite:ready-are-you-ready}}
  3. Choose one person outside your area as the contact everyone reports to, and record two ways to reach them.{{cite:ready-are-you-ready}}
  4. Put the rehearsal on the calendar before you close this page. Both FEMA and the CDC say to practice the plan; a plan nobody has walked through is a document, not a plan.{{cite:start-ready-plan}}{{cite:start-cdc-disability-plan}}

If there is an emergency right now, call 911 or your local emergency number and follow the instructions of local officials. This page is for the calm before, not the middle of one.

What a plan is for

A household emergency plan is a short list of decisions you make while calm, so that you do not have to make them while frightened. It does not predict the event. It answers the questions that every event raises: how will we hear about it, do we stay or go, where do we meet, how do we find each other, and who needs extra help?

Official guidance FEMA's 2020 guide sorts preparation into three steps: know your risks, make a plan, and take action, which includes practicing the plan.{{cite:ready-are-you-ready}} It also points out that many disasters come with little or no warning, so you need to be ready to act "often before you have instructions from authorities."{{cite:ready-are-you-ready}}

Editor's note That last line is the case for writing things down. In the first few minutes of a surprise there is no time to consult anyone, and the people who help most are usually those who already know where they are going.

The first hour: assess, then decide to stay or go

FEMA's guide gives a two-part opening for an event that arrives without warning. First, assess the situation: take a few seconds to work out what has happened and whether there is immediate danger, such as damaged buildings or downed power lines. Second, decide whether it is safe to stay where you are or whether you should change location. It gives the example that if you are indoors during a tornado you must stay indoors, while in an active-shooter situation you may try to run.{{cite:ready-are-you-ready}}

The same guide stresses that you should always follow the orders and advice of local authorities and first responders.{{cite:ready-are-you-ready}} That includes evacuation orders: local officials declare mandatory evacuations, and may also advise evacuation without requiring it.{{cite:ready-are-you-ready}}

Editor's note What follows is our framing, not FEMA's: in the first hour a household needs to do only a handful of things. Make sure everyone is accounted for and safe. Find out what is happening from a source that is likely to be reliable (see the alerts section below). Decide stay or go. If you stay, check that your kit and water are where you said they would be. If you go, take the go-bag and follow the instructions you were given. Nothing in this list requires buying anything.

The five decisions

Ready.gov's first step is a conversation, built on five questions.{{cite:start-ready-plan}} Here is what each one asks of you, with the official detail where there is some.

  1. How will we receive emergency alerts and warnings? See the alerts section.
  2. What is our shelter plan? FEMA says the best protection in high winds, tornadoes and many other events is an interior room away from windows, doors and flood waters, and that you should always follow local authorities' advice.{{cite:ready-are-you-ready}} Which room that is in your home is a fact only you can supply.
  3. What is our evacuation route? FEMA's guidance is to choose meeting places in several different directions, one within your neighborhood and another farther away such as a nearby town, and to learn alternate routes and backup transport.{{cite:ready-are-you-ready}} Ready.gov adds that you should identify several places you could go and, if you need a place that will accept pets, find one in advance, because most public shelters allow only service animals.{{cite:start-ready-evacuation}} More on leaving, and what to carry, is in the guide to {{link:go-bags|go-bags}}.
  4. What is our communication plan? See the communication section.
  5. Do we need to update our kit? The {{link:three-day-kit|three-day kit guide}} covers what goes in it and how to build it without unnecessary spending.

Who is in your household, really

Ready.gov's second step is to customize the plan to your daily needs and responsibilities, and to discuss how people in your network can help each other.{{cite:start-ready-plan}} The list it offers is useful as a checklist, because most gaps in a plan come from forgetting someone or something.

How you will hear: alerts and their limits

An alert plan is the first of the five questions, and it is the one most households have never actually checked. Ready.gov describes four overlapping systems.{{cite:start-ready-alerts}}

  • Wireless Emergency Alerts (WEA) are short messages to WEA-enabled phones in a targeted area. They come with a distinct sound and vibration, need no subscription, cost nothing and are not slowed by network congestion. But older phones may not be WEA capable, and some models require you to turn WEA on.{{cite:start-ready-alerts}}
  • The Emergency Alert System carries warnings over broadcast television and radio and other services.{{cite:start-ready-alerts}}
  • NOAA Weather Radio All Hazards is a nationwide radio network that broadcasts official warnings, watches and forecasts around the clock, including some non-weather emergencies.{{cite:start-ready-alerts}}
  • The FEMA App delivers National Weather Service alerts for up to five locations and can show open shelters.{{cite:start-ready-alerts}}

Ready.gov also says local alerting authorities decide what to send and that FEMA does not review, edit or approve alerts.{{cite:start-ready-alerts}} FEMA's guide adds that you should sign up for your community's own alert and warning systems, which can send text or email messages about local emergencies.{{cite:ready-are-you-ready}}

Editor's note Our practical reading: check your phone's alert settings once; find out whether your county or city runs its own sign-up; and give each person at least one alert path that does not depend on mobile service, such as a battery-powered or hand-crank radio with NOAA Weather Radio, which the Ready.gov kit list includes.{{cite:start-ready-kit}} A person who is deaf or hard of hearing needs a path that does not depend on sound; Ready.gov suggests a weather radio with a text display and flashing alert.{{cite:start-ready-disability}}

How you will find each other

This is the part of a plan that matters most when the household is not together. FEMA's guide describes a Family Emergency Communication Plan: collect contact details for family and for other important people and organizations such as doctors, schools and service providers, and choose an out-of-state point of contact through whom everyone can share updates if local communication is down. It asks for at least two forms of contact for that person.{{cite:ready-are-you-ready}}

The CDC adds three small points. Identify at least two emergency meeting places where your family can reunite if separated. Have more than one way to communicate, because text messages will often go through when a phone call will not. And make sure the plan accounts for children, older adults and pets, including microchipping pets and keeping the registration current.{{cite:start-cdc-pyh-connected}}

For anyone who relies on care or support, the CDC's disability guidance asks you to list the people who support your physical, mental or emotional health, to think about how you will reach them if the power is out, and to arrange for them to check on you directly.{{cite:start-cdc-disability-plan}} It also suggests using pictures in the plan if they help.{{cite:start-cdc-disability-plan}}

Editor's note The test of a contact plan is whether the youngest person who can read it, and the oldest person who might be confused by stress, can follow it. If a child cannot recite the out-of-state contact's number, or cannot find it on a card, the plan is not finished. Neighbors belong on the page too: FEMA's guide asks you to check on neighbors who may need a ride, and to leave a note telling others when you left and where you are going.{{cite:ready-are-you-ready}}

Practice: the part that makes it a plan

Ready.gov's fourth step is a single line: practice your plan with your household.{{cite:start-ready-plan}} FEMA's guide is more specific. It says it is vital that a family practice the communication plan "just like you would a fire drill," and it recommends regular household meetings to go over the plan and the post-disaster meeting place.{{cite:ready-are-you-ready}} The CDC's disability guidance asks you to practice, review and update your plan every year, and to share it with the people who support you.{{cite:start-cdc-disability-plan}}

FEMA's guide goes as far as a schedule. It lists the things to do regularly: test the evacuation and shelter plan, test the communication plan, refresh supplies at least every six months, hold a drill, keep documents current, review your inventory and have an insurance checkup. It also proposes a one-year timeline that spreads these over twelve months, for example testing the evacuation and shelter plan in month 4, the communication plan in month 5, and safeguarding documents in month 6.{{cite:ready-are-you-ready}}

What we cannot tell you

Uncertain The agencies recommend practice but the pages we read do not cite a study of how much it helps. We are not going to invent one. Our reasons for rehearsing are practical, not statistical, and we label them so.

Editor's note A rehearsal does not need to be an event. The version most likely to survive a busy year is small and attached to something you already do. Some ideas, all ours:

  • Ten-minute walk. Once a quarter, ask each person where they would meet and show the route. Time it.
  • The quiz at dinner. Ask a child for the out-of-state contact's name. Ask an adult where the water is. A wrong answer is a finding, not a failure.
  • The simulated-outage evening. Pretend the power is out for a few hours: switch off nonessential lights, the television and other devices, and use the kit's flashlights and radio. Leave on everything that keeps people safe, including the refrigerator and freezer, smoke and carbon monoxide alarms, any sump pump, security systems, and all medical or safety-critical equipment. Do not switch off the main breaker as a drill. You will learn what is missing faster than any checklist can tell you.
  • The go-bag lift. Pick up each person's bag and see whether they can carry it.
  • The seasonal check. Pair the six-month supply check with a task you already do twice a year, such as changing smoke-alarm batteries or the clocks.

Paper, money and insurance

A plan also has to survive the loss of ordinary conveniences. FEMA's guide says to collect household identification (including leases or deeds), financial and legal documents, medical and insurance information, and emergency contact numbers. It recommends keeping paper copies in a fire- and waterproof box or safe, a bank safe deposit box or with a trusted person, and electronic copies in a password-protected, encrypted form on removable storage or a secure cloud service.{{cite:ready-are-you-ready}} Ready.gov's financial page lists the same kinds of records, mentions an external drive or the cloud, and recommends getting property (homeowners or renters), health and life insurance if you have none.{{cite:start-ready-financial}}

On cash, both agencies recommend keeping some in small bills at home. Ready.gov says ATMs and credit cards may not work in a disaster, and FEMA recommends a mix of ones, fives and tens with a twenty as the largest, so that no one has to find change.{{cite:start-ready-financial}}{{cite:ready-are-you-ready}} FEMA's guide separately suggests enough cash for food, fuel and a few nights in a hotel if you must evacuate.{{cite:ready-are-you-ready}}

Uncertain No source we read gives a dollar figure for emergency cash. Choose an amount your household can set aside without strain. For people on a tight budget, Ready.gov's low-cost list starts with the free items: a free communication plan, free preparedness resources, signing up for local alerts and a family evacuation plan.{{cite:start-ready-low-cost}}

If you receive federal benefits, Ready.gov points out that disasters can disrupt mail for days or weeks and that the Treasury recommends direct deposit or the Direct Express card as safer ways to be paid.{{cite:start-ready-financial}}

Where the sources disagree

How often to review: six months or a year

FEMA's 2020 guide says to refresh supplies at least every six months and to check your needs every six months after assembling the kit.{{cite:ready-are-you-ready}} The CDC's disability-kit guidance and its pregnancy-and-children checklist also say to check kits every six months.{{cite:start-cdc-disability-kit}}{{cite:start-cdc-family-kit}} The Red Cross says to review, practice and refresh the plan, supplies and documents every six months.{{cite:start-redcross-older-adults}} But Ready.gov's current Build A Kit page says to "re-think your needs every year,"{{cite:start-ready-kit}} and the CDC's plan page says to review the plan every year.{{cite:start-cdc-disability-plan}}

Editor's note These are compatible if you read them as two jobs. Check the kit's contents (expiry dates, batteries, children's sizes) every six months, since that is the shorter interval and several sources agree on it. Review the plan itself (who is where, who has changed address or school) at least once a year. This reconciliation is our judgment and is not any agency's statement.

What a plan cannot do

A household plan does not remove risk, and it does not replace emergency services. Some situations will outrun any plan: a fast-moving fire, a flood that arrives faster than the warning, a medical event in the family. For those, the plan's job is smaller than it sounds. It makes sure that people who can leave, leave in good order, and that people who cannot leave are known to someone who can ask for help.

It also has limits of its own. A plan that depends on a phone depends on the phone working. A plan that depends on one car depends on that car. A plan that needs a specific person to be home at three o'clock fails when they are not. Writing those dependencies down is half the value of the exercise, because each one becomes a question you can fix: a second contact, a backup route, a neighbor with a spare key.

Finally, plans are written for a household as you understand it today. Ready.gov's own list of considerations includes ages, health, languages and pets, all of which change.{{cite:start-ready-plan}} Revisit the page when a baby arrives, when someone moves in or out, when a diagnosis changes, or when you move.
