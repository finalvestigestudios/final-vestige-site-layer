What to do now

  1. Do not rely on a homemade filter to make unknown water safe to drink. No official source we read describes one that does, and nothing at home can show you that yours worked.
  2. If the water is cloudy, clear it first. Pour it through a clean cloth, paper towel or coffee filter, or let it settle and draw off the clear water on top. That step is endorsed. It only prepares the water.{{cite:cdc-water-safe}}{{cite:ready-water}}
  3. Then treat it. Boiling is the best method. After it, a rolling boil for 1 minute, or 3 minutes above 6,500 feet, is the CDC's instruction.{{cite:cdc-water-hiking}}
  4. If you use a commercial filter, read its rating (see below) and follow it with a disinfectant, because filters do not remove viruses.{{cite:cdc-water-hiking}}
  5. If fuel, chemicals or radioactive material may be in the water, stop. No filter or boiling fixes that. Use bottled water or another source.{{cite:cdc-water-safe}}

The rest of this guide explains why, so you can resist the persuasive video that says otherwise.

Clear is not the same as safe

The most appealing thing about a homemade filter is the demonstration: murky water goes in the top, clear water drips out the bottom. The trouble is that clearness is not the property that matters.

The CDC puts it directly. Water containing germs that can make you sick, including bacteria, viruses and parasites, sometimes looks clean, and drinking water straight from a clear mountain stream is generally not safe.{{cite:cdc-water-hiking}} The National Park Service says never to drink water from a natural source that you haven't purified, even if it looks clean, because water in a stream, river or lake can still be full of bacteria, viruses and parasites.{{cite:nps-purify-water}}

Editor's note This is why homemade filters are not just useless but risky. They produce a convincing signal (clear water) that has no reliable connection to the safety of the water. A person who would hesitate to drink cloudy water may drink the clear water from a bottle of sand and charcoal without a second thought.

What a filter is, and what officials ask of one

The National Park Service describes most water filters as a screen with many tiny holes in it. Such filters can remove protozoa and some bacteria, but they cannot filter out viruses because viruses are too small.{{cite:nps-purify-water}} They also remove bigger contaminants such as leaves, silt, dirt and sand. And the NPS says plainly: filtration by itself does not purify water. It must be followed by boiling or disinfection.{{cite:nps-purify-water}}

The CDC and the NPS specify what a filter has to be, in terms a buyer can check:{{cite:cdc-water-hiking}}{{cite:nps-purify-water}}

  • An absolute pore size of 1 micron or smaller, or certification to NSF standard 53 or 58 for cyst reduction or removal, will remove parasites, but not viruses or all bacteria.
  • An absolute pore size of 0.3 micron or smaller will remove bacteria and parasites, but not viruses.
  • Reverse osmosis filters remove parasites, bacteria and viruses, and they also remove salt from water.{{cite:cdc-water-hiking}}

The NPS adds that you should use and care for any filter according to the manufacturer's instructions, since filters do not work as well if they are not taken care of.{{cite:nps-purify-water}}

Notice the language. The standards are about an absolute rating, meaning a guaranteed maximum hole size, and about independent certification. A layer of sand and charcoal has neither. Its hole sizes are unknown and uneven, and no one has certified it.

What improvised straining is good for

There is a legitimate version of the homemade filter, and it is the humble one. Cloudy water is harder to treat, and several of the treatment methods depend on it being clear. The CDC says UV light does not work well in cloudy water because small particles can block germs from the light, and that water should be filtered before UV.{{cite:cdc-water-hiking}} Ready.gov advises letting suspended particles settle or straining them through coffee filters or layers of clean cloth before treating.{{cite:ready-water}} The NPS says if the water is cloudy or has floating material you should filter it even if you plan to boil or disinfect it.{{cite:nps-purify-water}}

The sources differ a little on whether that step is necessary for boiling. The NPS says boiling will also effectively treat water that is still cloudy or murky.{{cite:nps-purify-water}} The CDC's emergency pages, by contrast, tell you to start the boiling steps with clear water.{{cite:cdc-water-safe}} Editor's note A household in an emergency can sidestep the difference by clearing the water first (which costs little) and then boiling.

What to do instead, in order

  1. Use sealed bottled or stored water first. That is where {{link:water-math|water math}} and {{link:two-week-water-storage|water storage}} earn their keep.
  2. Look for other clean water in the house before looking outside: the water heater tank, melted ice cubes and similar sources.{{cite:cdc-find-clean-water}}
  3. Boil. It is the method every source ranks first.{{cite:cdc-water-hiking}}{{cite:nps-purify-water}} See {{link:making-water-safer|making water safer}} for the steps and the bleach alternative.
  4. If you cannot boil, filter with a rated product and then disinfect, which the CDC calls the next best option.{{cite:cdc-water-hiking}}
  5. For chemical concerns, use another source. FEMA's Ready.gov describes distillation as the method that removes microbes, heavy metals, salts and most other chemicals, collecting only the condensed vapor from boiled water, and gives a stovetop method on its water page.{{cite:ready-water}} Note that Ready.gov says "most" other chemicals, not all. Editor's note Distillation also involves boiling water, which carries a burn risk, and we have not verified any source that supports it for water known to be contaminated with fuel. In that case, use another source.

A caution on pool tablets and shortcuts

The National Park Service warns not to use pool-cleaning tablets to disinfect drinking water, because they are not intended to be consumed.{{cite:nps-purify-water}} The same logic applies to any product not made for drinking water: if it is not labeled for it, do not use it.

Current safety verdict

Homemade sand or charcoal filters: not validated

Do not use a homemade filter as the only step before drinking. The official sources require that water be treated by boiling or disinfection after filtering, and they define a filter by a rated pore size or certification that an improvised one cannot show.{{cite:nps-purify-water}}{{cite:cdc-water-hiking}}

  • Clearing cloudy water with cloth, paper towels or coffee filters is endorsed, as preparation.{{cite:cdc-water-safe}}
  • Boiling remains the best method, and no filter replaces it.{{cite:cdc-water-hiking}}
  • Nothing here makes water contaminated with fuel, toxic chemicals or radioactive material safe.{{cite:cdc-water-safe}}

If you buy a filter: a checklist

A commercial filter is a different proposition from a homemade one, because it comes with a rating you can check. Before relying on one, work through what the official sources ask for.

  1. Find the absolute pore size or the certification on the label. One micron or smaller, or NSF 53 or 58 certified for cyst reduction or removal, for parasites; 0.3 micron or smaller for bacteria as well.{{cite:cdc-water-hiking}} A label that does not state a rating is a label that cannot be checked.
  2. Assume viruses are not removed. Plan a disinfection step after filtering, such as chlorine dioxide, chlorine or iodine.{{cite:cdc-water-hiking}}{{cite:cdc-water-safe}}
  3. For the disinfectant, follow its label. The NPS recommends NSF-approved products and notes that contact time varies by product, for example from 30 minutes to 4 hours. Pre-filter cloudy water so the disinfectant works better.{{cite:nps-purify-water}}
  4. Check the iodine cautions. If anyone is pregnant, has thyroid problems or is hypersensitive to iodine, avoid iodine-disinfected water, and no one should drink it for more than a few weeks at a time.{{cite:cdc-water-hiking}}
  5. Maintain it as directed. The NPS says filters do not work as well if they are not taken care of.{{cite:nps-purify-water}}
  6. If you use UV, use it on small quantities of clear water, check the battery, and allow the manufacturer's contact time.{{cite:nps-purify-water}}

Editor's note None of this requires knowing anything about how the filter works inside. It requires reading the label, and treating the absence of a rating as an answer.

A worked scenario

A storm has taken out the water supply. Your stored water is gone. A nearby creek is running, brown with silt. A neighbor shows you a bottle filled with gravel, sand and charcoal and says it works. How should the household think about it?

  1. Stop and ask what might be upstream. If there is any chance that fuel, chemicals or waste are in the creek, the answer is another source, not a better method.{{cite:cdc-find-clean-water}}
  2. Collect carefully. The NPS suggests a clean container, clean hands, and collecting from moving water rather than stagnant water, dipping just under the surface.{{cite:nps-purify-water}}
  3. Clear it. Let the silt settle, or strain through a clean cloth or coffee filter, and draw off the clear water.{{cite:cdc-water-safe}} The neighbor's bottle can be used as part of this straining, if you want, as long as you do not treat the result as safe.
  4. Boil it. A rolling boil for 1 minute, or 3 minutes above 6,500 feet, then let it cool and store it in a clean container.{{cite:cdc-water-safe}}

The creek water, boiled, is considerably safer than the creek water that has been through a bottle of sand. That is the whole argument, and the only extra tool it needs is a pot.
