What to do now

  1. Use sealed, commercially bottled water first for drinking, cooking and brushing teeth. The CDC calls it the safest and most reliable source in an emergency.{{cite:cdc-water-store}}
  2. No bottled water, and your tap water is unsafe or suspect? Boil it. Start with clear water. Bring it to a rolling boil for 1 minute, or for 3 minutes at high elevation. Let it cool and keep it in clean, sanitized containers with tight lids.{{cite:cdc-water-safe}} (The CDC puts "high elevation" at 6,500 feet and the EPA at 5,000 feet. See the boiling section: if you are anywhere near those heights, boil 3 minutes.){{cite:epa-emergency-disinfection}}
  3. Can't boil? Disinfect with plain household bleach, following the label first and the table further down this page second. Stir, then wait at least 30 minutes before drinking.{{cite:cdc-water-safe}}
  4. Stop if fuel, chemicals or radioactive material might be in the water. Boiling and disinfecting cannot fix that. Use bottled water or a different source and call your health department.{{cite:cdc-water-safe}}

If your local health department gives instructions that differ from this page, follow theirs. They know what is in your water. If someone has swallowed something harmful, or is seriously ill, call your emergency number or poison control, not this page.

First, name the problem

"Unsafe water" is two different problems wearing one label, and the fixes for one do nothing for the other.

The first is germs: bacteria, viruses and parasites that make you sick. They are alive, they are small, and the common household treatments are built to kill or remove them. The second is chemistry: fuel, industrial or agricultural chemicals, radioactive material. None of that is alive, so nothing that targets living things touches it. The CDC says so in plain language: you cannot make water containing fuel, toxic chemicals or radioactive materials safe by boiling or disinfecting it.{{cite:cdc-water-safe}}

Editor's note This is why the first question after a flood, a spill or a main break is not "how do I treat it?" but "what could be in it?" If the honest answer includes anything from a fuel tank, a farm, a factory or a damaged industrial site, the treatment methods below are the wrong tools and the right move is a different water source.

Cloudy water: clear it before you treat it

Every method on this page works better, and some only work, on water you can see through. If your water is cloudy, the CDC's instruction is to pour it through a clean cloth, paper towel or coffee filter, or to let it settle and carefully draw off the clear water on top. Then treat that clear water.{{cite:cdc-water-safe}}

Editor's note Read the structure of the CDC's page closely. Boiling begins "clear water." Bleach begins "clear water." UV begins "clear water." Clearing is a preparation step. It never replaces the treatment step that follows it, and clear-looking water can still carry germs you cannot see.

Boiling, done properly

  1. Start with clear water (see above).
  2. Bring it to a rolling boil and hold it there for 1 minute. At high elevation, hold it for 3 minutes.
  3. Let it cool without adding anything to it.
  4. Pour it into clean, sanitized containers with tight covers.

All four steps are the CDC's.{{cite:cdc-water-safe}} The EPA's emergency page gives the same procedure.{{cite:epa-emergency-disinfection}}

Two agencies, two altitude lines

The CDC says to boil for 3 minutes above 6,500 feet.{{cite:cdc-water-safe}} The EPA says above 5,000 feet.{{cite:epa-emergency-disinfection}} We checked both pages on the same day and they genuinely differ.

Editor's note A longer boil costs only fuel and time. If you live or are stuck anywhere above roughly 5,000 feet, boil for 3 minutes and you satisfy both. This reconciliation is our judgment, not either agency's statement, and it is one of the items the human safety reviewer is asked to confirm.

Boiled water can taste flat. The CDC suggests pouring it back and forth between two clean containers and letting it stand a few hours, or adding a pinch of salt per quart or liter.{{cite:cdc-water-safe}}

Boiling removes germs, not everything

Editor's note Boiling turns some of the water into steam and leaves everything dissolved behind. If a pot of water holds a chemical that does not boil away, boiling can only make the remaining water more concentrated, never cleaner. That is the mechanism behind the CDC's rule that boiling cannot fix chemical contamination.

Official guidance The EPA states it directly: boiling or disinfection will not destroy contaminants such as heavy metals, salts and most other chemicals.{{cite:epa-emergency-disinfection}}

Never use water from a radiator or from a boiler that is part of your home heating system for drinking, cooking or hygiene, even boiled. The CDC warns it can make you sick.{{cite:cdc-water-safe}}

When you can't boil: household bleach

Bleach is the fallback most households already own. It is also the method where a casual guess does the most damage, so this section is deliberately strict.

Read before you measure

  • The bleach label comes first. The CDC tells you to follow the label's instructions for disinfecting drinking water.{{cite:cdc-water-safe}}
  • If the label has none, find the "active ingredient" line and read the sodium hypochlorite percentage. In the United States it is typically between 5% and 9%.{{cite:cdc-water-safe}} The table below is only for bleach in that range.
  • The CDC specifies unscented liquid household chlorine bleach when it advises keeping bleach for water.{{cite:cdc-water-store}} The EPA is more specific: do not use scented or color-safe bleach, or bleach with added cleaners, and use fresh bleach or bleach stored at room temperature for less than a year.{{cite:epa-emergency-disinfection}}
  • For bleach at other strengths, such as a 1% solution, use the CDC's own table at the page linked in the sources below. We do not reprint it, so there is one authoritative copy to keep current.

If the water is cloudy, murky, colored or very cold, the CDC says to add double the amount listed.{{cite:cdc-water-safe}} Stir the mixture well and let it stand at least 30 minutes before drinking. Store it in clean, sanitized containers with tight covers.{{cite:cdc-water-safe}}

The EPA's independent page matches the dose for 6% bleach (8 drops a gallon, double if the water is cloudy, colored or very cold) and adds a check the CDC page does not: after 30 minutes the water should smell faintly of chlorine. If it doesn't, the EPA says to repeat the dose and wait another 15 minutes.{{cite:epa-emergency-disinfection}} If the chlorine taste is strong, pouring the water between clean containers and letting it stand for a few hours helps.{{cite:epa-emergency-disinfection}}

The tiny quantities are the point. This is a few drops, not a splash. At the scale of the "tiny amount" row, a dropper or an oral syringe is far more reliable than a spoon.

Editor's note If the bleach is old, or you cannot tell its strength, the table does not apply. Use bottled or boiled water instead.

Tablets and drops

Commercial tablets and drops, whether chlorine, chlorine dioxide or iodine, are made to treat a specific volume of water. The CDC's instruction is simply to follow the label or package exactly.{{cite:cdc-water-safe}} Chemical disinfectants kill viruses and bacteria but may not kill parasites. Iodine tablets and chlorine tablets will not kill Cryptosporidium. Chlorine dioxide tablets will, as long as the instructions are followed correctly.{{cite:cdc-water-safe}}

Iodine carries extra cautions. The CDC says people who are pregnant, who have thyroid problems, or who are hypersensitive to iodine should avoid water disinfected with iodine, and that no one should drink it for more than a few weeks at a time.{{cite:cdc-water-safe}}

Filters: useful, but only half the job

A portable filter is a physical sieve. Whether it stops a given germ depends on the size of the holes. The CDC's numbers: to remove bacteria, the filter needs an absolute pore size of 0.3 micron or smaller; to remove parasites such as Giardia or Cryptosporidium, 1 micron or smaller. Filters do not remove viruses.{{cite:cdc-water-safe}}

That last sentence is why the CDC's instruction does not end at the filter. After filtering, add a chemical disinfectant (iodine, chlorine or chlorine dioxide) to the filtered water to deal with viruses and bacteria.{{cite:cdc-water-safe}} The marketing on a filter box is not a substitute for reading its pore rating, and "removes 99%" is not a number the CDC's guidance uses.

Ultraviolet light and sunlight

Portable UV units can kill germs in small amounts of clear water. They need the water clear because floating particles cast shadows that germs hide in. Follow the manufacturer's instructions.{{cite:cdc-water-safe}}

The CDC also describes solar disinfection as an emergency measure: fill clean, clear plastic bottles with clear water and lay them on their sides in the sun for 6 hours if it is sunny, or 2 days if it is cloudy. A dark surface underneath helps. Note the careful verb: the sun "can improve the quality of your water by reducing the number of germs."{{cite:cdc-water-safe}} That is a smaller promise than "makes water safe," and this guide treats it as one.

When nothing at home is enough

There is a list of situations in which you should stop treating and start finding other water:

  • Fuel or toxic chemicals are known or suspected. Use bottled water or a different source and contact your health department for specific advice.{{cite:cdc-water-safe}}
  • Radioactive material may be present. Same answer. The CDC points to its own radiation-emergency guidance rather than offering a home method.{{cite:cdc-water-safe}}
  • The water came from a radiator or heating boiler. Never drink it.{{cite:cdc-water-safe}}

The CDC's emergency-water pages also cover finding other safe water in or near your home, drinking-water advisories, cisterns and wells. Those are separate pages with their own steps, and we point to them instead of summarizing from memory. They can be reached from the pages in the source list.

Editor's note We are not describing homemade sand, charcoal or cloth filters as a way to make unknown water safe, because none of the CDC methods above involves one, and there is no way to know at your kitchen table whether an improvised filter removed anything. If you clear cloudy water through any improvised material, treat the result as still unsafe and run it through a method above.

Storing and handling the water you've made safe

Treating water well and then pouring it into a dirty jug undoes the work. The CDC's container steps are short:{{cite:cdc-water-store}}

  1. Wash the container with soap and rinse it completely.
  2. Sanitize it with 1 teaspoon of unscented liquid household chlorine bleach (5%-9% sodium hypochlorite) in 1 quart (4 cups) of water.
  3. Cover and shake so the solution touches every inside surface. Wait at least 30 seconds, then pour it out.
  4. Let it air-dry. Fill it, cap it tightly, and label it "drinking water" with the date.

Keep stored water cool (the CDC gives 50°F to 70°F), out of direct sunlight, and away from gasoline, pesticides and other toxic substances. Replace water you stored yourself every six months. When you draw water out, use a clean scoop or pour it. Never dip a hand in.{{cite:cdc-water-store}} The CDC's preferred containers are food-grade water-storage containers, and it says never to reuse a container that once held bleach, pesticides or other toxic chemicals.{{cite:cdc-water-store}}

People who need extra care

  • Infants. The CDC lists baby formula among the things you should not make with water you know or suspect is unsafe.{{cite:cdc-water-safe}} For feeding infants in an emergency, ask your pediatrician in advance. The CDC maintains a separate toolkit for it.
  • Pregnancy and thyroid conditions. Avoid iodine-disinfected water, as above.{{cite:cdc-water-safe}}
  • Quantity. The CDC's baseline is at least 1 gallon per person per day, and it suggests storing more for pregnant women, people who are sick, pets, and hot climates.{{cite:cdc-water-store}} Planning the amounts is its own guide.

Then: a frigate's water, 1813

Documented history U.S. Navy regulations of 1802 allowed each man at least half a gallon of water a day for drinking on foreign voyages. A 450-man frigate like USS Constitution left port in 1813 with roughly 47,000 gallons aboard.{{cite:usscm-brenckle-2019}}

Captain David Porter of USS Essex wrote in his journal that the one precaution needed for good water at sea was casks of well-seasoned staves, cleaned and filled with pure water, and rinsed thoroughly with fresh water if they had ever held seawater for ballast.{{cite:usscm-brenckle-2019}} That is a container-hygiene rule, and it still reads like one.

Now: a household's water

Official guidance At least 1 gallon per person per day for drinking, cooking and brushing teeth, for at least three days and ideally two weeks, stored in food-grade containers that have been washed and sanitized, replaced every six months.{{cite:cdc-water-store}}

The two figures are not like for like. The Navy's half gallon covered drinking only; cooking water was extra, and the museum's study estimates it at up to 150 gallons a day for a crew of that size.{{cite:usscm-brenckle-2019}} The shared lesson is not the number. It is that clean containers matter as much as clean water. For what else the sailors ate and drank, see {{link:salt-beef-salt-pork-age-of-sail|salt beef and salt pork at sea}}.
