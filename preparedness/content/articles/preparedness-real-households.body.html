What to do now

  1. Learn your building's exits and practice the route. The U.S. Fire Administration's messages for high-rise residents are to know the fire-safety features and exits, practice your exit route, never prop stair or corridor doors open, and not use elevators during a fire.{{cite:start-usfa-high-rise}}
  2. Do the free things first. Ready.gov's low-cost list starts with a family evacuation plan, a free communication plan, free preparedness resources and local alert sign-up.{{cite:start-ready-low-cost}}
  3. Build a small support network and give one person a key. Ready.gov tells people with disabilities, and older adults, to build a network of helpers who know where supplies are, and to consider giving a trusted person a key.{{cite:start-ready-disability}}{{cite:start-ready-older-adults}}
  4. If you rent, check your insurance. Ready.gov says to obtain property (homeowners or renters), health and life insurance if you have none, and to read the policy to see which hazards it covers.{{cite:start-ready-financial}}
  5. If anyone depends on powered medical equipment, call your clinician and your power provider now, not during an outage.{{cite:start-ready-disability}}

If someone is in danger now, call 911 or your local emergency number. Anyone who cannot get out of a building unaided should call 911 and say so.{{cite:start-usfa-high-rise}}

Why standard advice does not fit every household

Most preparedness lists assume a household that owns or can easily get storage space, a car, a stocked pantry, spare money and the ability to move through a building without help. Many households have none of these, and some have more than one gap at once: a renter on a fixed income who is also caring for a parent, or a person who uses a wheelchair on the sixth floor of an apartment building. For those households a list that begins "buy a case of water and a battery radio" is not wrong, but it is incomplete and sometimes discouraging.

Editor's note Our aim here is not to replace the basic guides. The {{link:household-emergency-plan|household emergency plan}}, the {{link:three-day-kit|three-day kit}} and the {{link:go-bags|go-bags}} still apply. This guide takes what the official sources say about specific situations, says where those sources run out, and suggests who to ask. We found no single official page devoted to renters, and where an official source was written for property owners or professionals, we say so.

One principle runs through the federal advice. Ready.gov's older-adult page frames preparing as "simple, low-cost steps that help you get better prepared."{{cite:start-ready-older-adults}} FEMA's guide likewise notes that most of the recommended kit items are inexpensive and easy to find.{{cite:ready-are-you-ready}} Neither promises that this is easy for every household. We will not either.

Renters and apartment residents

What is in your hands, and what is not

A renter controls their own unit's supplies, plan and documents. A renter does not control the building's wiring, elevators, fire systems, generator or evacuation procedures. Those belong to the owner or manager. That split is why two questions matter: what can I do myself, and what should I ask the building?

Fire and exits

The USFA says tall buildings present particular challenges, such as fire and smoke spreading upward and long evacuation times. Its advice to occupants is to be familiar with the building's fire-safety features, especially exits; to practice the exit route; not to prop stairway or corridor doors open; to report a broken fire-protection feature such as a door closer; and, in a fire, to go to the nearest stair exit and close doors behind you, not use elevators, call 911 and tell any building staff you meet.{{cite:start-usfa-high-rise}}

The USFA also says that not all tall buildings meet modern standards, and that someone who cannot self-evacuate or use the stairs should call 911 and be prepared to shelter in place. That may mean staying in a room with the door closed and a wet towel sealing the cracks, or, if the exit stair is clear or nearly clear of smoke, waiting on the landing for assistance.{{cite:start-usfa-high-rise}} Uncertain That page is a fire-educator's post about fires in tall buildings. We did not find an equivalent official page for other hazards in apartments, and it does not say what to do about an elevator that fails in a power outage.

The Red Cross, writing for older adults, adds a general rule worth applying in any building: identify two ways out of every room.{{cite:start-redcross-older-adults}}

Power and the elevator

Ready.gov's power-outage page lists what an extended outage can disrupt: communications, water and transportation, retail businesses, gas stations, ATMs and banks, and it can cause food spoilage and prevent use of medical devices.{{cite:start-ready-power}} Editor's note In an apartment tower there is a further, practical consequence: if the elevators stop, every trip up and down is by stairs, including carrying water. We have not found an official source that says whether a given building's elevators have emergency power. That is a question for your building manager, and it matters most to anyone who cannot use the stairs.

Storage and space

The CDC baseline is 1 gallon of water per person per day for at least 3 days.{{cite:cdc-water-store}} For a household of four, that is 12 gallons, and, as {{link:water-math|water math}} notes, water weighs a little over 8 pounds a gallon. That is manageable in a small unit. A two-week supply is not, and the {{link:two-week-water-storage|storage guide}} deals with containers and placement. Editor's note If you have no room for more than three days of water, that is a perfectly sensible stopping point, and a reason to know where else you could get water. Nothing in the sources says you must store two weeks.

Ask the building

HUD's Multifamily Disaster Preparedness Plan Template (2026) is written for owners and managers, not tenants. It says an owner's plan should cover resident protection and safety, building protection and business continuity, and that it should be updated annually. Its table of contents lists resident handouts, an evacuation planning checklist and an evacuation special-assistance form.{{cite:start-hud-multifamily-template}} The template says properties converting through HUD's Rental Assistance Demonstration (RAD) program must create a property-wide disaster plan that includes an evacuation plan.{{cite:start-hud-multifamily-template}} Uncertain We read only the first 12 of its 79 pages. We do not know what a particular owner is legally required to provide to tenants, which differs by state, building type and lease, and we are not giving legal advice. What we can say is that a plan template for this exact situation exists, so asking whether your building has a plan is a reasonable question.

Insurance and paperwork

Ready.gov says to obtain property (homeowners or renters) insurance if you do not have it, to review the policy so the amount and types of coverage meet the requirements for all possible hazards, and notes that homeowners insurance does not typically cover flooding.{{cite:start-ready-financial}} FEMA's guide advises keeping household identification documents such as leases or deeds.{{cite:ready-are-you-ready}} Uncertain Neither page says whether a typical renters policy covers flood, and we did not read a policy. Ask your insurer directly.

Tight budgets

The honest limit of "prepare on a budget" advice is that a household with no margin cannot always set aside money. FEMA's guide advises setting aside a certain amount from each pay period for an emergency reserve,{{cite:ready-are-you-ready}} and Ready.gov suggests considering an emergency savings account and keeping a small amount of cash in small bills at home.{{cite:start-ready-financial}} Neither gives a dollar figure, and neither claims it is easy.

If you are paid by federal benefits

Ready.gov points out that a disaster can disrupt mail for days or weeks, and that the Treasury Department recommends direct deposit or the Direct Express prepaid card as safer ways to receive federal benefits, which also removes the risk of stolen checks.{{cite:start-ready-financial}}{{cite:start-ready-older-adults}} Its list of household identification includes a photo ID, a birth certificate and a Social Security card, the last of which it says is needed to apply for FEMA disaster assistance.{{cite:start-ready-financial}}

Help after a declared disaster

FEMA says it may provide money and other services for uninsured losses caused by a presidentially declared disaster. Housing help includes rent money if you are displaced and reimbursement of emergency lodging costs, and it lists "Serious Needs" payments for essentials such as food, water, baby formula, breast-feeding supplies and medication. You can apply online at disasterassistance.gov, by phone at 1-800-621-3362 or at a Disaster Recovery Center, and you may qualify even if you have insurance, provided you file a claim and submit the settlement or denial letter.{{cite:start-fema-housing}}

Limits of that help

Official guidance FEMA lists general eligibility conditions: you must be a U.S. citizen, non-citizen national or qualified alien, FEMA must be able to verify your identity, and your needs must be directly caused by a declared disaster and not met by insurance or other assistance. It also says Serious Needs money is not a reimbursement for loss of power or for replacing food.{{cite:start-fema-housing}}

Editor's note Read that page for your own situation. Help is only available after a declaration, and an outage that does not lead to one will not trigger it. We did not verify local charities, food banks or state programs, and they differ widely by place.

Ready.gov also warns that scammers may try to take advantage of financial fears. The government will not call or text about owing money or receiving payments, and you should be cautious about sharing bank account, Social Security or card numbers.{{cite:start-ready-financial}}

Older adults and the people who care for them

The Administration for Community Living notes that about half of people aged 65 and older have two or more chronic health problems, which can make them more vulnerable during periods without food, water, shelter or adequate rest.{{cite:start-acl-emergency}} Ready.gov's older-adult page points to its "Take Control in 1, 2, 3" guide: assess your needs, make a plan and engage your support network.{{cite:start-ready-older-adults}}

  • Support network. Include family, friends, neighbors, caregivers and others who can help, and practice the plan with them. At least one person should have a spare key, know where your supplies are, and know how to use lifesaving equipment or give medicine.{{cite:start-ready-older-adults}}
  • Treatments away from home. If a clinic or hospital gives you routine treatments, ask about its emergency plans and identify back-up providers.{{cite:start-ready-older-adults}} For dialysis or other life-sustaining treatment, Ready.gov says to know the location and availability of more than one facility.{{cite:start-ready-disability}}
  • Staying or leaving. The Red Cross says to plan to stay home for at least two weeks or evacuate, to find out about local registries if you need help to evacuate, and to make a back-up plan if you need power for medical devices or to keep medicine cold.{{cite:start-redcross-older-adults}}
  • Medicines and papers. The Red Cross recommends at least 30 days of medications and extra assistive items such as a cane or eyeglasses; see {{link:three-day-kit|the kit guide}} for how the sources differ on days. Ready.gov suggests copies of Medicaid, Medicare and other insurance cards.{{cite:start-redcross-older-adults}}{{cite:start-ready-older-adults}}
  • Transport. Plan transportation if you need help evacuating.{{cite:start-ready-older-adults}}
  • Pets and service animals. Not all shelters accept pets, so plan alternatives, such as asking loved ones outside your immediate area to help.{{cite:start-ready-older-adults}}

The ACL also points people to the Eldercare Locator (1-800-677-1116), which connects callers to services for older adults and their families, and offers a printable Medical Information Card.{{cite:start-acl-emergency}} For emotional strain, it lists the SAMHSA Disaster Distress Helpline at 1-800-985-5990.{{cite:start-acl-emergency}}

Caregivers

The ACL says that long-term care providers must train staff in emergency actions including evacuation, lockdown and sheltering in place.{{cite:start-acl-emergency}} Editor's note If your relative lives in a facility or receives home care, it is reasonable to ask for the written emergency plan: who evacuates them, where to, how families will be told, and what happens to medicines and equipment. If you are a caregiver who lives elsewhere, ask the plan to name who checks on them, and give that person your number. The CDC's disability guidance suggests arranging for support contacts to check on you directly, and asks you to think about how you would reach each other if the power is out.{{cite:start-cdc-disability-plan}}

Heat

Ready.gov notes that people with disabilities, access and functional needs, and older adults may be at increased risk in extreme heat. Some medications may interfere with the body's ability to cool itself, and people with cognitive or developmental disabilities, including dementia, may have trouble explaining how the heat affects them. It advises asking caregivers and friends to check in regularly, discussing with a doctor how heat interacts with medications, identifying air-conditioned places and the accessible transport to get there, and asking your utility for priority access in case of power loss.{{cite:start-ready-disability}}

Disability and access needs

Ready.gov says that disabilities can be visible or invisible and affect people in many ways, so each person should consider their own circumstances.{{cite:start-ready-disability}} A few themes recur across its page and the CDC's guidance.

  • Powered medical equipment. Talk to your doctor or provider about keeping it running during an outage, and ask your power provider to put you on a list for priority power restoration.{{cite:start-ready-disability}} The ACL also links to emergency power planning resources, and notes that the HHS emPOWER tool uses de-identified Medicare data so that communities can plan for people who rely on electricity-dependent equipment.{{cite:start-acl-emergency}} Uncertain No source we read promises that such lists lead to a response in a particular event.
  • Local registries. Many city and county emergency management agencies keep voluntary registries for people with disabilities to receive targeted help. Contact your local office to find out.{{cite:start-ready-disability}}
  • Transport and shelters. The CDC warns accessible transport may not be available in an emergency, so ask your paratransit provider or local government who provides it. It also states that shelters must be accessible under the Americans with Disabilities Act and must allow service animals, and that disaster shelters are required to allow emotional support animals under the Fair Housing Amendments Act.{{cite:start-cdc-disability-evacuate}}
  • Communication. Carry printed cards or store information on your devices to tell responders how to communicate with you, and wear medical alert tags.{{cite:start-ready-disability}} Ready.gov asks you to consider languages spoken in the household,{{cite:start-ready-plan}} and the Ready.gov pages we read are offered in many languages.{{cite:start-ready-alerts}}
  • Alerts. Wireless Emergency Alerts rely on sound and vibration and on a compatible phone; a person who is deaf or hard of hearing is advised to use a weather radio with a text display and flashing alert.{{cite:start-ready-alerts}}{{cite:start-ready-disability}}

For the go-bag items particular to each disability, including spare wheelchair batteries, patch kits and communication cards, see {{link:go-bags|the go-bag guide}}.

Editor's note People with disabilities are the real experts on their own needs, and the agencies' lists are starting points. The pattern that serves best is to write down what you need, who will provide it and what the backup is, and to share that with the people named. A plan that exists only in your head cannot help when someone else must act on it.

What this guide has and has not done

Every instruction and fact above comes from the Ready.gov, FEMA, USFA, HUD, CDC, ACL and American Red Cross pages cited, read on the date shown, or from the September 2020 FEMA guide. We found no dedicated official page for renters, and HUD's planning template is written for owners; we read only part of it. We did not verify state or local programs, charities, food banks, legal duties of landlords, immigration-related eligibility details beyond what FEMA's page states, or what any given insurance policy covers.
