Read this first

This is a history, not a recipe. This page gives no method, no ratios and no storage times for pemmican, and it should not be used as a guide to making or storing it.

  • Pemmican is a food of Indigenous peoples of the northern Plains and beyond, with its own names, cultures and living makers. It is not a prepper invention, and we have tried to name the peoples the sources name.
  • We found no regulator or Extension source that evaluates pemmican. The closest official guidance is for jerky, and it is more cautious than the historical record: meat is heated to a stated internal temperature, and home-dried jerky is given a short shelf life.{{cite:hist-food-fsis-jerky}}{{cite:hist-food-nchfp-jerky}}
  • Home-preserved, cured and dried foods have been linked to botulism cases, and you cannot see, smell or taste the toxin.{{cite:fsis-home-cure-qa}}{{cite:cdc-botulism-prevention}} If you want to dry meat at home, use a method published by USDA or the National Center for Home Food Preservation.

If someone develops blurred vision, difficulty swallowing or speaking, or growing weakness after eating a preserved food, treat it as a medical emergency and call your emergency number.

  • Documented history Recorded in a museum, heritage-site, encyclopedia or primary source, as reported by the source cited.
  • Official guidance A current statement from a government food-safety body or university Extension service.
  • Uncertain A question the surviving sources cannot settle, or sources that differ.
  • Editor's note Our reasoning. It is not a statement from the source.

Whose food it is

The history of pemmican is usually told as a story about fur traders. The sources we read, including the ones written or interpreted by Indigenous organizations, tell a different order of events: the food belonged to Indigenous peoples first, and the fur trade learned to depend on it. Here is who the sources name, and in their words where we can.

  • Cree. The Canadian Encyclopedia gives the word as Cree: pimikan, "manufactured grease". It defines pemmican as dried meat, traditionally bison, pounded into coarse powder and combined with fat, occasionally with berries.{{cite:hist-food-ce-pemmican}}
  • Lakota and Dakota (wasna) and Anishinaabe (pemmican). NATIFS, an Indigenous foodways organization, says the food is known as wasna to the Lakota and Dakota people and as pemmican to Anishinaabe peoples, and that Indigenous peoples across the Plains and beyond have long relied on it during hunts, seasonal travel and winter, with many still making and sharing it today.{{cite:hist-food-natifs-wasna}}
  • Blackfoot (Siksika, Kainai and Piikani). The Head-Smashed-In Buffalo Jump World Heritage Site in Alberta says that in the fall, after communal bison kills, "the people would prepare dry meat and pemmican for winter", and that such dry food stores were kept as emergency supplies for times when the bison were not near. It identifies the Blackfoot as three tribes: the Siksika, the Kainai (Bloods) and the Piikani (Peigan).{{cite:hist-food-headsmashedin}}
  • Chipewyan. The Canadian Encyclopedia credits the fur trader Peter Pond with introducing pemmican to the trade in 1779 after obtaining it from the Chipewyans in the Athabasca region.{{cite:hist-food-ce-pemmican}}
  • Métis. The Virtual Museum of Métis History and Culture says the Hudson's Bay Company's constant need for provisions gave Métis bison hunters and pemmican provisioners the only steady market for their work.{{cite:hist-food-metis-fur-trade}}

Two notes on that list. First, the sources overlap but do not give a single origin story, and none of them gives a date for when pemmican was first made. We do not give one either. The earliest date in this article, 1779, is the date a European trader first acquired it for the trade, not the date of its origin. Second, the sources differ in kind: an encyclopedia, a heritage site, a museum document and an organization's lesson. We cannot show that any of them speaks for every nation named, and we have not found a Dene-authored source for the Chipewyan attribution or a Plains Cree-authored one for the word's meaning. We mark those gaps in the limits section.

The word itself is a small example of why care is needed. The Canadian Encyclopedia gives pimikan, "manufactured grease". A search excerpt of the Métis museum's document glosses the word as two Cree words, pimmi (fat) and konn (meat), which we could not confirm from the document text we read. We use the encyclopedia's form and do not repeat the other gloss as fact.Uncertain

More than a food

The National Museum of the American Indian's Native Knowledge 360 education resource says the American bison is "much more than an important historical source of food" to the Northern Plains Native Nations. In its words, tribal histories, cultures, traditions and spiritual lives were deeply connected to the buffalo in a reciprocal relationship, and people honored and cared for the bison through ceremonies and cultural protocols.{{cite:hist-food-nk360-bison}} That context matters for reading anything below. Dried meat and pemmican were the product of a relationship with the animal and with seasonal hunts, not simply a preservation technique.

At Head-Smashed-In, the page describes the hunt as a communal seasonal activity: the Blackfoot gathered, communal kills occurred as bison moved into an area in the fall, and the people prepared dry food for winter.{{cite:hist-food-headsmashedin}} The page notes that the Blackfoot pursued their traditional lifeways until the near extinction of the buffalo in 1881, and records that after Treaty No. Seven in 1877 and the buffalo's demise, the Blackfoot settled on reserves in southern Alberta.{{cite:hist-food-headsmashedin}} That is a very condensed summary of a hard history, and we repeat it only to make the point that the end of the great hunts was not a technological change. It was a catastrophe imposed on the peoples who relied on them.

The fur trade and the Métis provisioners

The Canadian Encyclopedia describes pemmican as a dense, high-protein, high-energy food that could be stored and shipped with ease to provision voyageurs in the fur trade travelling in prairie regions where food, especially in winter, could be scarce, and notes that it was cooled and sewn into bison-hide bags for shipping.{{cite:hist-food-ce-pemmican}} It says that after Pond's 1779 acquisition, posts along the Red, Assiniboine and North Saskatchewan Rivers were devoted to acquiring pemmican from Aboriginal peoples living in the region as well as from the Métis.{{cite:hist-food-ce-pemmican}}

The Métis museum's document gives a sense of scale. It reports the Métis writer Alexander Ross's account of a single hunt in which over 2,500 animals were killed, which, when processed, equalled 375 bags of pemmican and 240 bales of dried meat after the hunting group was fed. It describes provisioning as very lucrative, in one period earning more than all the money agriculturalists made from selling their produce, and says that by the mid-1870s few Métis lived off the hunt, which sharply raised pemmican prices.{{cite:hist-food-metis-fur-trade}}

The same economic weight explains one of the more dramatic episodes in the sources. In 1814, the Canadian Encyclopedia says, Governor Miles Macdonell of the Red River Colony passed the "disastrous but short-lived" Pemmican Proclamation, which forbade the export of any food supplies, including pemmican, from the colony and nearly started a war with the Métis.{{cite:hist-food-ce-pemmican}} Dates for the proclamation vary in popular accounts; we use the encyclopedia's.

The food travelled beyond its homeland. The Canadian Encyclopedia notes that the Royal Navy provisioned several Arctic expeditions with beef pemmican made in England.{{cite:hist-food-ce-pemmican}} In the United States, Captain Randolph Marcy's 1859 handbook for overland travellers, digitized by the Library of Congress, says pemmican "constitutes almost the entire diet" of the Fur Company's men in the Northwest and reproduces a table of the daily allowance of Dr. Rae's party on a spring journey in the Arctic in 1854, in which pemmican was the largest item; he says this allowance of a little over two pounds of the most nutritious food was found barely sufficient in that cold climate.{{cite:hist-food-marcy-prairie-traveler-text}}{{cite:loc-prairie-traveler}} Marcy is a U.S. Army officer and an outsider to the peoples who made the food; he describes the product and its use by the fur companies, and he gives a short account of its composition that we do not repeat.

What the sources say about keeping it

The claims about shelf life in the sources are consistent in kind and thin in evidence. Marcy writes that pemmican "will keep fresh for a long time", with no duration given.{{cite:hist-food-marcy-prairie-traveler-text}} NATIFS calls the food energy-dense, lightweight and shelf-stable.{{cite:hist-food-natifs-wasna}} The Canadian Encyclopedia says it could be stored and shipped with ease.{{cite:hist-food-ce-pemmican}} The Head-Smashed-In page says dry food stores were emergency supplies.{{cite:hist-food-headsmashedin}}

None of these sources gives a measured storage time, a storage condition, or an account of spoilage, and none tests safety. Editor's note That does not mean the claims are false. It means that centuries of skilled practice in specific climates, with specific meat, fat, drying conditions and storage, is knowledge that these web sources do not transmit and that we have no standing to turn into a rule. The sources describe a food prepared by people who knew how; they do not teach a stranger how to do the same thing safely. We would not try to extract that from a page like this one.

What modern food-safety guidance says about dried meat

No regulator or Extension source we read addresses pemmican. The nearest guidance is for jerky, and it makes several points that apply to dried meat in general.

  • USDA's current recommendation for making jerky safely is to heat meat to 160°F and poultry to 165°F before the dehydrating process. USDA explains that most dehydrator instructions omit this, that a dehydrator may not reach those temperatures, and that after drying bacteria become much more heat resistant.{{cite:hist-food-fsis-jerky}}
  • The National Center for Home Food Preservation gives a similar target of 160°F but allows the heating to be done either in a marinade before drying or in an oven after drying, and says properly dried jerky keeps at room temperature two weeks in a sealed container.{{cite:hist-food-nchfp-jerky}} USDA says home-dried jerky can be stored one to two months, commercially packaged jerky twelve.{{cite:hist-food-fsis-jerky}}
  • Both agencies flag wild game. NCHFP says pork or wild game used for jerky should be treated against the Trichinella parasite before slicing and notes that freezing will not eliminate bacteria; USDA, quoting its source, notes that venison can be heavily contaminated with fecal bacteria, depending on the hunter's skill and wound location.{{cite:hist-food-nchfp-jerky}}{{cite:hist-food-fsis-jerky}}

The two agencies disagree on details. The 160°F target is shared, but USDA says to apply the heat before dehydrating and gives a one-to-two-month home storage time, while NCHFP permits heating after drying and gives two weeks at room temperature. We report both and flag the difference for the human safety reviewer rather than choosing between them.Official sources differ

Pemmican differs from jerky in the way that matters most for storage: it is dried meat combined with fat, and the sources we read give no regulator view on how that changes the risk. Fat is not mentioned in the USDA or NCHFP jerky guidance except that NCHFP says to trim and discard fat from meat because it becomes rancid quickly.{{cite:hist-food-nchfp-jerky}} We take no position on what that implies for pemmican.

Current safety verdict

Not validated for modern household use

Do not make or store pemmican from this page. We found no regulator or Extension guidance that validates a home method, and this page gives none by design. This is a statement about the absence of tested guidance, not a judgment of the tradition.

  • For dried meat generally, follow USDA and NCHFP jerky guidance, including the 160°F heat step, their handling advice for pork and wild game, and their short storage times for home-dried jerky.{{cite:hist-food-fsis-jerky}}{{cite:hist-food-nchfp-jerky}}
  • The CDC lists improperly preserved foods among the most common sources of botulism, says you cannot see, smell or taste the toxin, and says to throw out food you cannot vouch for.{{cite:cdc-botulism-prevention}}
  • USDA's food-safety service records many botulism cases following home-canned, preserved, cured, smoked or fermented foods and points consumers to the NCHFP.{{cite:fsis-home-cure-qa}}

Editor's note If you want pemmican for its own sake, the respectful route is to buy it from people who make it and sell it under food-safety rules, or to learn it from a community that holds the knowledge. We did not verify any particular producer and recommend none.

Then: the hunt and the cache

Documented history Communal seasonal hunts, dried meat and pemmican made for winter and kept as emergency stores, later sold to fur-trade posts by Métis and other provisioners, with the whole system collapsing as the bison were destroyed.{{cite:hist-food-headsmashedin}}{{cite:hist-food-metis-fur-trade}}

Now: tested steps and short shelf lives

Official guidance A stated heat step, careful handling of game, and storage times of weeks, not years, for home-dried jerky.{{cite:hist-food-fsis-jerky}}{{cite:hist-food-nchfp-jerky}} Our companion pieces on {{link:smokehouses-salting-curing|smokehouses and curing}}, {{link:pioneer-travel-rations|pioneer travel rations}} and {{link:history-of-food-preservation|the history of food preservation}} cover related ground.

What we have not done

We have not found, and so do not cite, a source authored by a Dene (Chipewyan), Plains Cree, Anishinaabe, Blackfoot or Lakota knowledge holder about the origin and making of pemmican, apart from NATIFS's lesson and the heritage-site and museum pages above. We read one 42-page Métis museum document only in part. The Canadian Encyclopedia article and NATIFS's lesson both appear to include preparation details; we deliberately drew nothing from them for this page. We read the Head-Smashed-In page but not its booklet Buffalo Tracks.

No Canadian federal source (Parks Canada, Library and Archives Canada) and no Smithsonian page on pemmican itself was read, because our research access ran out before we reached them. Before this page is published we recommend that someone with standing in the communities named read it. We have no image of pemmican or of the hunts that we have the rights to publish, so this page has none. The page stays a draft until the human safety reviewer has read the verdict.
