Read this first

This is a history, not a method. It surveys how people kept food from spoiling, from drying and salting to canning and refrigeration. It contains no recipe, quantity, time or temperature for any preservation method.

  • For any food you preserve at home, use a procedure published and tested by USDA or the National Center for Home Food Preservation, and follow it exactly.{{cite:nchfp-cure-smoke}}
  • Low-acid foods, including meat and most vegetables, need pressure canning; the CDC says it is the only recommended home canning method for them. Botulism toxin cannot be seen, smelled or tasted.{{cite:cdc-botulism-prevention}}
  • If you do not know whether a food was safely preserved, the CDC's advice is short: if in doubt, throw it out.{{cite:cdc-botulism-prevention}}

Symptoms of botulism can include blurred vision, difficulty swallowing or speaking, and weakness. It is a medical emergency; call your emergency number.

The problem every method answers

The National Center for Home Food Preservation's historical overview opens with a claim worth keeping: food preservation "permeated every culture at nearly every moment in time". Its author, Brian Nummer, writes that food begins to spoil the moment it is harvested, and that preservation let people settle in one place and form communities, because they no longer had to eat a kill or harvest at once.{{cite:nchfp-historical-origins}} The overview is a short 2002 piece, and several of its early claims are stated without citation, so we treat the deepest dates as uncertain and rest this survey on the parts of the record that can be dated and checked.

Drying

The NCHFP overview says that in ancient times the sun and wind would have dried foods naturally, and that evidence shows Middle Eastern and Asian cultures actively dried foods as early as 12,000 B.C. It adds that in the Middle Ages purpose-built "still houses" dried fruits, vegetables and herbs where sunlight was not strong enough, using a fire for heat and sometimes smoke.{{cite:nchfp-historical-origins}} The 12,000 B.C. date is the overview's; it does not cite a specific source for it, so we give it as the NCHFP's statement and not as established fact.Uncertain

Drying appears throughout the rest of the record. The Head-Smashed-In site in Alberta describes the Blackfoot preparing dry meat and pemmican for winter, which the {{link:pemmican-and-dried-meats|pemmican piece}} covers with the peoples named.{{cite:hist-food-headsmashedin}} The Navy's bread was baked until thoroughly dry, as the {{link:ships-biscuit-and-hardtack|ship's biscuit piece}} describes.{{cite:usscm-brenckle-2019}} And in 1859 Captain Randolph Marcy wrote that desiccated vegetables, pressed into a hard cake and dried, had been "extensively used in the Crimean war" and by the U.S. Army in Utah, and that he regarded them as the best preparation for prairie travel he knew.{{cite:hist-food-marcy-prairie-traveler-text}} He also said canned vegetables were very good for campaigning but "not so portable".{{cite:hist-food-marcy-prairie-traveler-text}}

Salt and curing

The NCHFP overview says the earliest curing was "actually dehydration", with early cultures using salt to help desiccate foods, and that salting could be culinary, with different raw salts chosen for flavor. It records that in the 1800s it was discovered that certain salts gave meat a red colour rather than an unappetizing grey, that those salts contained nitrites (saltpeter), and that as the microbiology of Clostridium botulinum was elucidated in the 1920s it was realized that nitrites inhibited it.{{cite:nchfp-historical-origins}} USDA's food-safety service adds that it began authorizing nitrite in meat and poultry products in 1925 and that concern about nitrosamines was raised in the early 1970s.{{cite:fsis-botulism}}

The documented practice sits between those dates. In 1812 the Navy's Boston agent asked for "a sufficient quantity of good Salt and Salt Petre" in barrelled beef and pork.{{cite:usscm-brenckle-2019}} At Monticello, enslaved workers covered cuts of pork in salt to draw moisture out and hung them in the smokehouse over a low hardwood fire.{{cite:hist-food-monticello-meat}} In the winter of 1805-1806 the Corps of Discovery sent men to the Oregon coast to boil seawater for salt, a story told in our {{link:lewis-and-clark-salt-works|salt-works piece}}. All of this happened before anyone could explain why nitrate-containing salt mattered for botulism. The NCHFP also notes that salting shaped what people ate: choosing among rock salt, sea salt and spiced salts was part of the cooking, not only the keeping.{{cite:nchfp-historical-origins}}

Sugar, honey, pickles and ferments

The overview's account of sweet and sour preservation is short and mostly undated. It says preservation with honey or sugar was well known to the earliest cultures, that in ancient Greece quince was mixed with honey and packed in jars, and that the trade that brought sugar cane to Europe brought pickled foods too. Pickling, it says, is preserving foods in vinegar or another acid, which may have begun by placing food in wine or beer, and fermentation "was not invented, but rather discovered".{{cite:nchfp-historical-origins}} We have not tested any of those origin stories against other sources and flag them as the overview's account only. What can be said is that jams, pickles and ferments appear in this record as separate families of method with separate logic: sugar and acid change what microbes can grow, and fermentation relies on microbes you cannot see. Ferments and pickles also have their own safety history, which we have not covered here.

Canning

The NCHFP calls canning "the newest of the food preservations methods", pioneered in the 1790s by the French confectioner Nicolas Appert, who found that heat applied to food in sealed glass bottles kept it from deteriorating. In about 1806 the French Navy trialed his principles on a range of foods, and in 1810 the Englishman Peter Durand used tin cans. Appert "did not fully understand it": it was thought that excluding air was responsible, and it was not until 1864 that Louis Pasteur's work on microorganisms clarified why. In 1851 Raymond Chevalier-Appert patented the pressure retort to can at temperatures above 212°F, though the overview says the significance of that in relation to C. botulinum was not known until the 1920s.{{cite:nchfp-historical-origins}}

That last sentence is the heart of this history. Canning worked before anyone knew why. For high-acid foods, boiling-water canning was enough; for low-acid foods, as we now know, it is not. Both USDA and the CDC say spores of C. botulinum survive boiling and that low-acid foods need pressure.{{cite:fsis-botulism}}{{cite:cdc-botulism-prevention}} A hundred and more years of practice does not equal a hundred years of safety; the gap between what worked and what was understood is where botulism cases came from.

Editor's note Marcy's 1859 handbook gives a small example of the same gap. It describes a way of preserving butter in sealed tins and says the method "is found to keep sweet for a great length of time".{{cite:hist-food-marcy-prairie-traveler-text}} We do not reproduce the method. We cite it to show that nineteenth-century confidence in "it keeps" rested on observation, not on an understanding of botulism.

Cold

The Smithsonian's National Museum of American History describes early American cold storage as underground: a pit or a hut built over one to trap cold air, with meat packed in ice and straw. It notes an octagonal stone-lined icehouse pit built in the 1780s at the President's House in Philadelphia. Natural ice harvesting, it says, began to take off in the early 1800s, with Frederic Tudor, who earned the nickname "Ice King", seeking to build a national supply chain. By the end of the 1800s many households kept perishables in an insulated "icebox" lined with tin or zinc, and the museum's view is that cold became "the clear choice" because salting, drying and canning erased any appearance of freshness and took longer to prepare.{{cite:hist-food-si-cold}}

The National Park Service adds figures from its account of an icebox in a Kennedy family kitchen: in 1847, nearly 52,000 tons of ice moved by ship or train to 28 U.S. cities, nearly half from Boston and most of it Tudor's. Ice-making factories gradually replaced harvesting, the first widely popular electric refrigerator was General Electric's in 1927, and by 1944 about 85 percent of American households had a refrigerator instead of an icebox.{{cite:hist-food-nps-icebox}} The Smithsonian dates an earlier small electric device, the DOMELRE, to 1914, and says that the 1927 GE Monitor top became the first refrigerator widely popular in American homes.{{cite:hist-food-si-cold}}

The NCHFP's account of freezing is brief and partly informal. It says that anywhere with freezing temperatures for part of a year used them to preserve food, that mechanical refrigeration was invented in the 1800s, and that Clarence Birdseye's quick-freezing process revolutionized freezing.{{cite:nchfp-historical-origins}} The overview adds that cellars, caves and cool streams were "put to good use" to prolong storage times.

The pattern across all of it

Editor's note Reading these sources against each other, three patterns stand out, and we offer them as our reading, not the sources' conclusions.

  • Practice ran ahead of explanation. Appert did not know why heat worked. Navy contractors included saltpetre without the sources recording that they knew why. Nitrite's effect on botulism was understood in the twentieth century, long after the practice was centuries old.
  • Each method traded one cost for another. Salt and drying kept food but changed it, so the Smithsonian says cold won as a method that left food looking fresh and cost less labour. The Navy's salt provisions and biscuit kept sailors fed and still left them short of what fresh food provides, as the RMG overview of life at sea records.{{cite:rmg-life-at-sea}}
  • The people doing the work are easy to miss. The Monticello and Mount Vernon pages name enslaved workers as the curers of meat for the household; Indigenous peoples in the pemmican and salt-works pieces were the source of knowledge and hospitality the record depends on.{{cite:hist-food-monticello-meat}}

Current safety verdict

Safe only under stated conditions

Modern descendants of these methods are safe when done by a tested procedure; the historical versions are not a guide. This page endorses no method. The verdict is about the history as a source of advice: it is not one.

  • For low-acid foods, including meat, the CDC says pressure canning is the only recommended home canning method. Spores of C. botulinum survive boiling.{{cite:cdc-botulism-prevention}}{{cite:fsis-botulism}}
  • For curing and smoking, the NCHFP is the place to find tested methods, and USDA says botulism cases have followed home-canned, preserved, cured, smoked and fermented foods.{{cite:nchfp-cure-smoke}}{{cite:fsis-home-cure-qa}}
  • For refrigeration and freezing, follow current USDA and FDA guidance, which this page does not reproduce.

This survey leans on a 2002 extension overview for several early claims, and we have labelled the uncited ones. The verdict will need review if sourced pieces on canning and pickling are added.

Then: observation and craft

Documented history Methods that worked, judged by whether food kept, passed along in households and navies, and explained only later. Failures went unrecorded or unexplained.{{cite:nchfp-historical-origins}}{{cite:usscm-brenckle-2019}}

Now: tested processes and known mechanisms

Official guidance Pressure for low-acid canning, tested curing methods, and a clear rule for doubt: throw it out.{{cite:cdc-botulism-prevention}}{{cite:nchfp-cure-smoke}}

What we have not done

This is a survey, and it leans on a short NCHFP overview (2002), two museum or park pages on refrigeration and the sources already cited in our other history pieces. We did not read primary documents on Appert's 1810 prize or on the early ice trade, and we found no Library of Congress or Smithsonian page on canning's origins in the time we had. For fermentation, pickling, sugar preserves and root-cellar storage, the overview's undated claims are the only source we used, and each of those topics deserves its own sourced piece.
