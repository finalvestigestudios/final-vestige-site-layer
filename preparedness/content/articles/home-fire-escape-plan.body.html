What to do now

  1. Press the test button on every smoke alarm tonight. USFA's own drill starts that way: push the test button to sound the alarm and begin the drill.{{cite:usfa-fire-escape}} Alarms belong in every sleeping room, outside each sleeping area and on every level, and the whole unit should be replaced 10 years after its manufacturing date.{{cite:shelter-nfpa-smoke-alarms}}
  2. Draw a map of your home that shows every door and window, and mark two ways out of every room.{{cite:usfa-fire-escape}}
  3. Check that both ways out actually work: doors and windows are not blocked, windows are not stuck, screens come out quickly and any security bars open from the inside.{{cite:usfa-fire-escape}}{{cite:shelter-ready-home-fires}}
  4. Choose one meeting place outside, in front of the home, a safe distance away, and mark it on the map.{{cite:usfa-fire-escape}}{{cite:shelter-nfpa-escape}}
  5. Practice with everyone who lives there, twice a year, and when a real alarm sounds get out immediately and stay out. Call 911 from outside.{{cite:shelter-nfpa-escape}}{{cite:shelter-ready-home-fires}}

If there is a fire right now, do not read on. Get out, stay out, and call 911 or the fire department from outside. The U.S. Fire Administration says residents could have less than 2 minutes to escape once the smoke alarm sounds.{{cite:usfa-fire-escape}} For burns, smoke inhalation or anyone not breathing normally, emergency services come before anything on this page.

Why the plan has to exist before the fire

A home fire plan is unusual among preparedness tasks because the time you have to use it is measured in seconds. USFA says residents could have less than 2 minutes to escape once the smoke alarm sounds, and ties that to modern building contents and open floor plans, which it says make home fires more severe.{{cite:usfa-fire-escape}} NFPA says you may have as little as one or two minutes.{{cite:shelter-nfpa-escape}} Ready.gov puts the same idea more vividly: in less than 30 seconds a small flame can become a major fire; thick black smoke can fill a house, or a residence can be engulfed, in minutes; and smoke and toxic gases kill more people than flames do, because they leave people disoriented and drowsy.{{cite:shelter-ready-home-fires}}

Editor's note Nobody makes good decisions in the dark in a smoke-filled hallway for the first time. The point of a plan is to turn the decisions (which way, who helps whom, where we meet) into habits you already have. Everything below is built around that.

Step 1: Alarms you can trust

The escape plan starts with the thing that wakes you. NFPA's guidance, which USFA and Ready.gov echo on most points, is:{{cite:shelter-nfpa-smoke-alarms}}

  • Install alarms inside and outside every bedroom and sleeping area (a hallway, for instance), and on every level of the home, including the basement.
  • Mount them on the ceiling or high on a wall, and at least 10 feet from the stove to cut down on nuisance alarms. Do not put them in kitchens or bathrooms.
  • Use interconnected alarms where you can, so that when one sounds, they all sound.
  • Test every alarm once a month by pressing the test button. If it does not sound, change the battery, or replace the entire unit if it is more than 10 years old.
  • Buy alarms listed by a nationally recognized testing laboratory. Only a qualified electrician should install hardwired alarms.

Ready.gov agrees on every level, adds that batteries should be replaced twice a year unless the alarm uses a 10-year lithium battery, and warns never to disable an alarm while cooking.{{cite:shelter-ready-home-fires}} NFPA says either a yearly-battery unit or a 10-year unit will give protection.{{cite:shelter-nfpa-smoke-alarms}}

NFPA also says to learn the sounds of your smoke and carbon monoxide alarms from the user guide, since they differ by brand and model.{{cite:shelter-nfpa-smoke-alarms}} A CO alarm is a different device with a different job; the CPSC says that if one sounds you should get outside to fresh air immediately and then call 911.{{cite:shelter-cpsc-co}} For where CO comes from during an outage, see {{link:outage-heat-cold-generators|heat, cold and generator safety in an outage}}.

Uncertain NFPA's page gives two different figures for how much working smoke alarms reduce the risk of dying in a home fire: "60 percent" in one place and "cut in half" in another. We have not tried to resolve that, and we do not quote either number. The instruction does not depend on it.

Step 2: Map the house and find two ways out of every room

USFA's instructions are short: draw a map of your home that includes all doors and windows, and find two ways out of every room.{{cite:usfa-fire-escape}} NFPA adds that you should mark where each smoke alarm is, and that you should walk through the home and inspect every possible exit and route first. Households with children should consider drawing the floor plan with them.{{cite:shelter-nfpa-escape}} Both agencies offer free escape-grid handouts for this; we have not reproduced them and have not verified their contents beyond the page descriptions.{{cite:usfa-fire-escape}}{{cite:shelter-nfpa-escape}}

"Two ways out" means two ways that are independent of each other. Ready.gov's explanation is that the primary way may be blocked by fire or smoke.{{cite:shelter-ready-home-fires}} For a bedroom, the usual pair is the door and a window. That is where most plans quietly fail, so check each window:

  • Is it stuck? Can it be opened by everyone who might need to use it?{{cite:shelter-ready-home-fires}}
  • Can the screen be taken out quickly?{{cite:shelter-ready-home-fires}}
  • If there are security bars, do they have an emergency release on the inside? NFPA says the release devices will not compromise your security but will improve your chances of getting out.{{cite:shelter-nfpa-escape}}
  • Is anything in front of it, such as furniture, boxes or stored items? NFPA says items that block doors and windows can mean the difference between life and death.{{cite:shelter-nfpa-escape}}

Editor's note Look at the plan from the point of view of the slowest, smallest or sleepiest person in the house. A window that an adult can climb through may be too high for a child, and a door that opens easily in daylight may be blocked by a bed at night. Check both ways out from the bed, not from the middle of the room.

Upstairs bedrooms and escape ladders

NFPA says that if the home has two floors, every family member, including children, must be able to escape from second-floor rooms. An escape ladder placed in or near a window can be an additional route. Read the manufacturer's instructions carefully, practice setting it up from a first-floor window, store it near the window where you can reach it, and let children practice only with a grown-up and only from a first-story window.{{cite:shelter-nfpa-escape}}

Uncertain None of the pages we read evaluates any ladder product, gives a weight limit or says how many floors a ladder suits. We therefore do not recommend a type, and we do not say what height is too high. Follow the manufacturer's instructions and your local fire department's advice.

Step 3: The meeting place, and the number to call

USFA says to choose an outside meeting place in front of your home. NFPA's examples are a neighbor's house, a light post, a mailbox or a stop sign, and it says the place should be a safe distance in front of the home, where everyone can meet after escaping, and should be marked on the plan.{{cite:usfa-fire-escape}}{{cite:shelter-nfpa-escape}} The point is that anyone counting heads can tell, within seconds, who is out and who is not.

NFPA adds two practical details. First, go outside and check that your street number is clearly visible from the road, so responders can find the home. Second, have everyone memorize the emergency number for the fire department so that any member of the household can call from a neighbor's home or a cell phone once safely outside.{{cite:shelter-nfpa-escape}}

Once you are out, stay out

NFPA says that under no circumstances should you go back into a burning building. If someone is missing, tell the fire department dispatcher when you call. Firefighters have the skills and equipment to perform rescues.{{cite:shelter-nfpa-escape}} Ready.gov gives the same instruction in a slightly different form: if you cannot reach someone who needs help, leave and call 911 or the fire department, and tell the operator where the person is. If pets are trapped, tell firefighters right away.{{cite:shelter-ready-home-fires}}

Ready.gov also tells you to teach children not to hide from firefighters.{{cite:shelter-ready-home-fires}}

A plan that works only for a healthy adult is not a plan. The sources are specific about several situations:

Editor's note The "assign a backup" instruction is the one households most often skip. The person assigned to carry a toddler or help a grandparent is also the person most likely to be away, or asleep on a different floor. Write the primary and the backup names on the map.

Step 5: Practice, and make it realistic

NFPA says to practice at least twice a year and to make the drill as realistic as possible.{{cite:shelter-nfpa-escape}} Ready.gov also says twice a year.{{cite:shelter-ready-home-fires}} What "realistic" means, according to the pages we read:

  • Start with the alarm. USFA's drill begins by pushing the test button, then everyone gets outside to the meeting place.{{cite:usfa-fire-escape}}
  • Do it in the dark, and from bed. Ready.gov suggests practicing feeling your way out of the house in the dark or with your eyes closed. NFPA says to hold a nighttime drill once children have mastered the plan.{{cite:shelter-ready-home-fires}}{{cite:shelter-nfpa-escape}} Editor's note Keep every drill a simulation. Do not use real smoke, flame, heat or fog, do not light anything, and do not switch off power or any safety or medical equipment. Clear trip hazards before a dark or eyes-closed run. None of the sources asks for any of those things.
  • Go low. NFPA says everyone should practice getting low and going under the smoke to the exit. It also says to choose the escape route that is safest, with the least smoke and heat, but to be prepared to escape through toxic smoke if necessary.{{cite:shelter-nfpa-escape}} Ready.gov's explanation is that heavy smoke and poisonous gases collect first along the ceiling.{{cite:shelter-ready-home-fires}}
  • Close doors on the way out. NFPA says closing doors slows the spread of fire and gives you more time to escape.{{cite:shelter-nfpa-escape}} Ready.gov advises sleeping with your bedroom door closed.{{cite:shelter-ready-home-fires}}
  • Watch what actually happens. NFPA says to determine during the drill whether children and others can readily wake to the sound of the alarm.{{cite:shelter-nfpa-escape}}

Editor's note A drill is a test of the plan, not of the people. If someone cannot open a window or does not wake up, change the plan: replace the window lock, move the bed, add a bedside alarm. None of the sources gives a target time for a drill, and we do not invent one.

If there is a real fire: what the sources say to do

This is the part to read once, calmly, and then rehearse. Ready.gov's actions during a fire are:{{cite:shelter-ready-home-fires}}

  1. Drop to the floor and crawl low under any smoke to your exit.
  2. Before opening a door, feel the doorknob and the door. If either is hot, or if smoke is coming around the door, leave the door closed and use your second way out.
  3. If you open a door, open it slowly, and be ready to shut it quickly if there is heavy smoke or fire.
  4. If you cannot get to someone who needs help, leave and call 911 or the fire department, and tell the operator where the person is.
  5. If clothes catch fire: stop, drop and roll. Stop immediately, drop to the ground and cover your face with your hands; roll over and over or back and forth until the fire is out. If you or someone else cannot do that, smother the flames with a blanket or towel.

For a burn, Ready.gov says to use cool water on the burn immediately for three to five minutes, cover it with a clean, dry cloth, and get medical help right away by calling 911 or the fire department.{{cite:shelter-ready-home-fires}}

If you cannot get out

Ready.gov says: close the door and cover vents and cracks around doors with cloth or tape to keep smoke out; call 911 or the fire department and say where you are; signal for help at the window with a light-colored cloth or a flashlight.{{cite:shelter-ready-home-fires}} NFPA's version, which it calls "sealing yourself in for safety," is to close all doors between you and the fire, use duct tape or towels to seal door cracks and cover air vents, open the windows at the top and bottom if possible so fresh air can get in, report your exact location to the fire department, and wave a flashlight or light-colored cloth at the window.{{cite:shelter-nfpa-escape}}

Uncertain The two pages differ in one detail: NFPA suggests opening the window at the top and bottom, and Ready.gov does not mention opening it. We have not found a source explaining when each is advisable. This is a last resort, not a plan, and if there is a way out you can use, use it.

Apartments, condominiums and high-rises

USFA's home fire escape plans page singles out high-rise and condominium buildings as especially dangerous and gives a specific sequence: know where all the exit stairs are; leave your apartment and close the door behind you; pull the fire alarm on your way out to notify the fire department and your neighbors; use the stairs and not the elevator unless the fire department directs otherwise; go to your outside meeting place and stay there; call the fire department and tell them if someone is trapped in the building.{{cite:usfa-fire-escape}}

USFA's separate high-rise page (posted January 2022, last reviewed May 2026) is more detailed and more recent. Its default is to evacuate: shout "fire" or pull the fire alarm on your way to the nearest exit, call 911, and notify any building staff you meet. Go quickly to the nearest stair exit and close any doors behind you. Do not use elevators, because they may become disabled or be secured by first responders. Keep going down to the ground floor and fully out of the building. Close stair and corridor doors: most stair enclosures in modern buildings have fire-resistant walls and doors and pressurization systems that blow outside air into the stairwell, and these work best with doors closed. Never prop stairway or corridor doors open.{{cite:shelter-usfa-high-rise}}

Sheltering in place appears on that page only for people who cannot self-evacuate or use the stairs. They should call 911 and be prepared to shelter in place, which may mean staying in a room with the door closed and sealing the cracks around the door with a wet towel. People with limited mobility who can reach the nearest exit stair may wait on the landing for help if it is clear or nearly clear of smoke. The page also notes that not all tall buildings meet modern standards, and tells residents to learn the building's exits, practice the route, and keep exits unblocked.{{cite:shelter-usfa-high-rise}}

NFPA's wording is different

NFPA's escape-planning page says that residents of high-rise and apartment buildings "may be safer defending in place" and links to a separate high-rise tip sheet that we did not open.{{cite:shelter-nfpa-escape}} USFA's high-rise page makes evacuation by stairs the default and limits sheltering in place to people who cannot self-evacuate.{{cite:shelter-usfa-high-rise}} Neither page explains how the two fit together. Editor's note We read USFA's page as the more specific and more recent statement and NFPA's as general wording, but that is our reading, not either agency's, and the NFPA tip sheet might qualify it. Ready.gov's building guidance says to determine whether to stay put, shelter in place or get away, and to listen for and follow instructions.{{cite:shelter-ready-plan-locations}} Ask your building manager and the fire department now which plan applies to your building, and practice that one. This is an item for the human safety reviewer.

Ready.gov's guidance for planning at a building you live or work in is to ask for the building's alarm and alert systems, evacuation plans including alternate exits, and any building shelter-in-place plan. In a high-rise, know the closest exit and a second way out, do not use elevators, and stay to the right in stairwells so emergency workers can come up.{{cite:shelter-ready-plan-locations}}

Editor's note For renters: you can do most of this without anyone's permission: map your unit, test the alarms, clear the window, agree a meeting place. You usually cannot change a building's hardwired alarms or fit security-bar releases yourself, so ask the landlord in writing. We did not research tenancy law, so we make no claim about what yours requires.

What the sources say about preventing the fire

An escape plan is for when prevention fails. Ready.gov's prevention list, shortened to the items most relevant to outage and cold-weather households, includes:{{cite:shelter-ready-home-fires}}

  • Keep combustible objects at least three feet away from portable heaters, and only buy heaters evaluated by a nationally recognized laboratory such as Underwriters Laboratories.
  • Check that a portable heater has a thermostat and will switch off automatically if it falls over.
  • Never use a stove range or oven to heat your home; it can increase the risk of fires, burns and damage the appliance, and gas stoves can also build up dangerous CO.
  • Stay in the kitchen when frying, grilling or broiling, and turn the stove off if you leave even briefly.
  • Keep candles at least 12 inches from flammable materials, or use flameless candles. Never leave children near operating stoves or burning candles.
  • Keep a fire extinguisher in the kitchen, and ask the local fire department about proper use and maintenance.

Power outages raise several of these risks at once, because households bring in heaters and candles. The CDC prefers battery flashlights and lanterns to candles, and says that if you use candles you must keep them away from anything that can catch fire and stay near them.{{cite:shelter-cdc-power-outage}} For the full outage picture see {{link:outage-heat-cold-generators|heat, cold and generator safety in an outage}}.

After a fire

Ready.gov's post-fire list: contact a local disaster relief service such as the Red Cross if you need temporary housing, food and medicines; check with the fire department that the residence is safe to enter; do not try to reconnect utilities yourself; make an inventory of damaged property and keep receipts before throwing anything away.{{cite:shelter-ready-home-fires}} This guide does not go further into recovery.

Connecting the plan to the rest of your household planning

The fire escape plan is one part of a household plan. The Ready.gov family planning page lists shelter, evacuation and communication questions alongside it, and suggests practicing the whole plan with your household.{{cite:shelter-ready-plan}} For what to do when you cannot go home, see {{link:shelter-or-evacuate|shelter in place or evacuate}}, and for how a family stays in touch when separated, see {{link:family-communication-plan|the family communication plan}}. Editor's note Your outside meeting place for a fire is a good first entry on that plan, but a spot just in front of your home is unlikely to suit a wildfire or a flood, which is why a household plan usually names more than one place.
