Read this first

This is a case study of one overland journey that failed, written from the records without embellishment. It contains no survival method and should not be read as one. It describes real deaths. We have tried to treat them plainly and to say where the record is thin or disputed.

  • If you travel in winter, tell someone your timetable and your main and alternate routes, avoid traveling alone, and check road and weather conditions before you leave.{{cite:hist-field-nws-winter-before}}
  • If your vehicle gets stuck in a storm, the National Weather Service says to stay in it, because leaving means becoming disoriented quickly in wind-driven snow and cold.{{cite:hist-field-nws-winter-during}}
  • Call your emergency number (911 in the United States) early. In 1846 there was no number to call.

The vehicle advice above is the start of an official page, not the whole of it. Read the National Weather Service pages for the rest, including how to avoid carbon monoxide poisoning, before you rely on any of it.{{cite:hist-field-nws-winter-during}}

  • Documented history Recorded by an agency, museum, archive or scholar, as reported by the source cited.
  • Official guidance A current statement from a government safety body.
  • Uncertain A figure or claim the sources disagree about or cannot settle.
  • Editor's note Our reasoning. It is not a statement from the source.

Why this case

"Supply chain" sounds like a modern phrase for trucks and warehouses. It describes an older problem too: whether the things a household or a group needs arrive where and when they are needed. For a wagon party crossing North America in the 1840s there was no warehouse at the other end. Everything had to be carried, pulled or walked, and what a party could not carry it could not count on.

We chose the Donner-Reed party of 1846-47 because the record can carry it. Federal and state agencies describe it, a state history agency has published an account of the route it took, a medical journal has published an analysis of who died, and a major reference work summarizes the rest. The Library of Congress holds an Army handbook of the period. It is also widely retold, and retelling produces myth. The aim here is to separate what the records support from what the legend adds.

Editor's note We do not claim the Donner party was typical. The National Park Service says emigrants who started early in 1846 were reluctant to believe Hastings, and Britannica says the other migrants of 1846 had finished their journey by the time this party reached the Humboldt River.{{cite:hist-field-nps-donner}}{{cite:hist-field-britannica-donner}} The case is useful because it shows, in unusual detail, how several parts of a journey's "supply chain" depended on each other. It is not a case of what usually happened.

What a journey like this had to supply

Reading the sources together, four things had to hold at once.

  • Food. Provisions loaded at the start, plus whatever could be obtained along the way.
  • Draft animals. Oxen pulled the wagons. Britannica also calls the oxen the party's chief reserve of food.{{cite:hist-field-britannica-donner}}
  • Time. A route over the Sierra Nevada had to be finished before winter snow. The National Park Service notes a late start, with 2,500 miles to go when the party passed Independence, Missouri.{{cite:hist-field-nps-donner}}
  • Information. Where the route went, how far it was, and where water and grass lay.

Editor's note Read together, the sources suggest a chain: wrong information about the route cost time, and lost time and lost animals meant less food. The chain is our reading of the sources; none of them states it in these terms.

The plan, and the advice they were given

The National Park Service says the party of 87 men, women and children, led by George Donner and James Reed, left Illinois on April 12 and passed Independence on May 12, with 2,500 miles to go. Late starters, the Park Service says, found Lansford W. Hastings's claim of a faster route enticing, and the party agreed to join Hastings's group to save 150 to 500 miles, a range the Park Service notes Hastings himself was "a little vague" about.Documented history{{cite:hist-field-nps-donner}}

Britannica's account differs in a few details: it has the families leaving Springfield on April 14 and says Hastings claimed to shave more than 300 miles.Uncertain{{cite:hist-field-britannica-donner}} It also says that in reality the cutoff was 125 miles longer than the established trail.{{cite:hist-field-britannica-donner}} The sources disagree on the departure date and on the promised saving, and we have not found a record that resolves either.

The Utah Division of State History is careful about Hastings's intent. His 1845 Emigrants Guide to Oregon and California promoted California, but it is unclear, the state history says, whether he meant to promote the cutoff from Fort Bridger or merely to say that a more direct route than the usual one through Fort Hall was possible.Uncertain{{cite:hist-field-utah-hastings}} That essay also records that the route had supporters, among them John Fremont, James Hudspeth and James Bridger, and critics: James Clyman tried to dissuade the Donner-Reed party and Joseph R. Walker thought it an unproven risk.{{cite:hist-field-utah-hastings}} Britannica adds that James Reed was warned at Fort Laramie by a friend who had just come east that way.{{cite:hist-field-britannica-donner}}

The guide itself is digitized. In the Internet Archive's scan of the 1845 Cincinnati edition, as we read its machine-made (OCR) text, Hastings gives the entire distance "by this route," from Independence to either Oregon or California, as about twenty-one hundred miles and says the usual time to make the journey is about 120 days, exclusive of delays, while admitting that parties had often taken much longer. In the same passage he says emigrants had so far traveled together as far as Fort Hall, because it was the only settlement in that vicinity where they could get horses and provisions, and he names the Soda Springs, not Fort Hall, as the point where the Oregon and California routes would most advantageously diverge.Documented history{{cite:hist-field-ia-hastings-guide}} The same passage also describes a California route leaving the Oregon route and heading southwest toward the Salt Lake, but the OCR of that stretch is too garbled for us to quote its details.{{cite:hist-field-ia-hastings-guide}} Editor's note Our arithmetic, not the guide's: 120 days counted from May 12, the date the National Park Service gives for passing Independence, ends in early September, and the National Park Service puts the party at the Humboldt River on September 26.{{cite:hist-field-nps-donner}} The guide's own estimate, in other words, had little margin for the late start. We do not know that this party relied on that figure.

The state history gives the final turn. The party left Fort Bridger on July 31 and found a note near present-day Henefer in which Hastings warned them away from the Weber Canyon route and promised a better road. Reed hurried out to catch him, and Hastings showed him a route from a summit, which the party then followed. Following that route, the essay says, the party lost more time and met extreme hardship, both there and in the Salt Lake Desert.Documented history{{cite:hist-field-utah-hastings}}

Editor's note Nothing in these sources says the party was foolish. They were given conflicting advice by men with reputations, and Britannica says Hastings, who had promised to lead migrants along the trail, left Fort Bridger with a different company so that Reed had to act as guide.{{cite:hist-field-britannica-donner}} On the evidence above, the sources locate the first failure in route information. We cannot weigh that against the late start, the weather or other causes, and none of the sources tries to.

Where the time and animals went

Britannica supplies the specifics the other sources omit. Breaking a new trail through the Wasatch mountains cost the party about two weeks. Hastings left a note saying the Great Salt Lake Desert could be crossed in two days; the crossing took five. The party lost dozens of cattle and abandoned several wagons, and spent more days in a fruitless search for missing oxen.Documented history{{cite:hist-field-britannica-donner}} The National Park Service puts it more generally: the route took more time than the popular routes would have, "depleting supplies and livestock," and the party reached the Humboldt River on September 26.{{cite:hist-field-nps-donner}} By then, Britannica says, the other emigrants of 1846 had completed their journey and the Donner party was racing the weather to clear the Sierra passes.{{cite:hist-field-britannica-donner}}

Britannica says Paiute warriors killed several of the remaining oxen, and that by this point the party had cached nearly everything except food, clothing and essentials to lighten the load on exhausted animals. The National Park Service quotes the Paiute Sarah Winnemucca that her people would have helped the Donner-Reed party, "only my people were afraid of them."Disputed{{cite:hist-field-britannica-donner}}{{cite:hist-field-nps-donner}} These two accounts do not sit easily together. We could not find a source that reconciles them or an account written at the time by the Paiute, and we make no claim about who is right.

The record gets less certain as it nears the snow. Britannica says the pass was blocked by deepening snow on October 31 and that many oxen, "the chief reserve of food," wandered off and were lost in the first storms.{{cite:hist-field-britannica-donner}} The Park Service says the party reached Truckee Lake in October, exhausted and low on provisions, and was then trapped for four months.{{cite:hist-field-nps-donner}}

The winter

The National Park Service says that when all the animals had been killed by mid-December, the party was forced to eat rawhide, and that some members left on a self-rescue mission and reached help, but not before resorting to cannibalism; those in the camps also ate the dead to stay alive. The survivors were not rescued until the spring of 1847.Documented history{{cite:hist-field-nps-donner}} Britannica says seven men reached the lake camp on February 18 and took 23 of the starving emigrants, including 17 children, back to the settlements, with several deaths on the way, and that other relief parties followed but could not remove everyone.{{cite:hist-field-britannica-donner}}

The evidence for the cannibalism is the written accounts of survivors and rescuers. Britannica notes that researchers who examined the Alder Creek campsite in 2010 announced they had found no human bones or other physical evidence of it, and that they themselves said this did not rule it out given the extensive contemporary accounts.Uncertain{{cite:hist-field-britannica-donner}} We note the point once and go no further. The record does not need the details, and the people in it should not become a spectacle.

Britannica calls Breen's account of the winter the only contemporary written record of the ordeal, and says that Virginia Reed, a survivor, wrote to a cousin not to take "no cutofs."{{cite:hist-field-britannica-donner}} The diary survives. The Bancroft Library's finding aid describes a 32-page booklet kept from November 20, 1846 to March 1, 1847, with entries filling the first 29 pages, written tersely and in a matter-of-fact tone, and ending on the day a relief party arrived.Documented history{{cite:hist-field-oac-breen-diary}} The finding aid also warns that early printed versions, beginning with a copy sent to a newspaper in May 1847, are "considered to be corrupt" because of omissions, editorial changes or outright falsification, and names Teggart's 1910 edition as the first complete scholarly one.{{cite:hist-field-oac-breen-diary}} The finding aid does not itself say the diary is the only contemporary record, so that claim stays with Britannica. We read the finding aid, not the diary's entries, and we have not found Reed's letter in a collection record we could read. Quotations of Breen that circulate online may come from the altered early versions.

The difference is small, but it is real. Possible reasons include who counts as a member of the party, such as hired drivers and people who joined or left along the way. None of the pages we read explains the discrepancy, so we do not choose a number. When this guide gives a figure it names the source.

The McCurdy study, which we read only as an abstract, analysed the party's mortality by age and sex. It found that age was the most important risk factor. Mortality was lowest, at about 10%, among those aged 6 to 14, and highest for children under 6 and adults 35 and older. People traveling without other family members had about twice the relative risk, and men and boys also had about twice the risk. The author suggests that keeping family structures intact may improve survival in nutritionally stressed populations.Documented history{{cite:hist-field-mccurdy-1994}} Editor's note That is a statistical finding about a group, not a verdict on any individual or family, and we have not read the paper's methods or caveats.

What the Army handbook says it was written to prevent

The Library of Congress has a digitized copy of Captain Randolph B. Marcy's The Prairie Traveler, a handbook "published by authority of the War Department." The LOC copy is a later printing, 1861, and the work postdates the events above, so it was not a response to them. Its contents list is still instructive about what the Army thought a traveler needed to know: routes and their merits, wagons and teams, "stores and provisions," anti-scorbutics (foods against scurvy), the amount of supplies, camp equipment, and guides.Documented history{{cite:loc-prairie-traveler}} The preface says the book draws on the author's frontier experience and on the reports of army topographical engineers who had surveyed the routes.{{cite:loc-prairie-traveler}}

We have read only the contents, preface and title page, so we do not report what the book recommends. A reader who wants ordinary overland provisioning in the travelers' own words could start with the Library of Congress collection of 49 diaries and seven published guides for emigrants, Trails to Utah and the Pacific, which we have also not read beyond its catalogue record.{{cite:loc-pioneer-diaries}} For navigation, see {{link:navigation-before-gps|finding your way before GPS}}.

How the site is remembered

California State Parks runs Donner Memorial State Park near Truckee, at an elevation of about 6,000 feet. Its Pioneer Monument, completed in 1918 by the Native Sons of the Golden West, stands on a 22-foot pedestal where the Schallenberger and later the Breen cabin once stood, and was restored in 2022. The park's visitor center has exhibits on the emigrant experience and the Donner Party, and on the Land of the Washoe, Chinese construction of the railroad, and early motoring over Donner Pass.Documented history{{cite:hist-field-ca-parks-donner}} The park's page is a visitor guide, not a history, so we use it for the memorial and not for the account above.

Current safety verdict

Not validated for modern household use

Do not use this journey as a model. It is a record of what went wrong, not a plan to adapt. The practical takeaways below come from current official guidance, not from the history.

  • Starting late, on an unverified route: not recommended. Official winter-travel guidance asks you to check conditions and restrictions before you leave, avoid traveling alone, and give someone your timetable and your main and alternate routes.{{cite:hist-field-nws-winter-before}}
  • Carrying the pantry on the thing that is moving you: Editor's note a pattern worth avoiding. The party's food and its transport were the same animals. Modern guidance has you carry a vehicle kit with blankets, high-calorie non-perishable food, extra clothing, a compass and road maps.{{cite:hist-field-nws-winter-before}}
  • Leaving your shelter to find help: the National Weather Service says that if a vehicle is stuck in a storm you should stay in it, because you will become disoriented quickly outside in wind-driven snow and cold.{{cite:hist-field-nws-winter-during}} Whether that applies to a situation like the 1846 camps is a question the history cannot answer, and we do not claim it does.
  • Relying on one guide's word: Editor's note not recommended. Check a second, current source.

Then: everything carried, no way to call

Documented history A party pulled its food, its animals and its information along with it. The route taken cost the party weeks, per Britannica, and the NPS says it depleted supplies and livestock.{{cite:hist-field-nps-donner}}{{cite:hist-field-britannica-donner}}

Now: conditions checked, plan shared

Official guidance Check road and weather conditions first, tell someone your route and timetable, carry a vehicle kit and a paper map and compass, and call for help early rather than late.{{cite:hist-field-nws-winter-before}}{{cite:hist-field-nws-winter-during}}

What we have not done

This account relies on a federal trail page, a state-agency history essay, a reference work, and the abstract of one journal article. We read the Bancroft Library's finding aid for Breen's diary but not the diary's entries, and only passages of the OCR text of Hastings's guide (the rest of that long text was not read). We have not read Virginia Reed's letter, the original relief-party accounts, or the full text of McCurdy's paper, which is a scanned page image we could not read. Britannica is a tertiary source; we use it for specific figures that the others omit, name it each time, and flag the single-source claims in the ledger.

Several things are missing. We have no account of the Paiute or Washoe experience of these events from a source we are willing to cite. We have no inventory of the party's actual provisions at any date. We make no claim about the supplies other 1846 parties carried or about how most emigrants fared. We have no image of the route or the monument that we have the rights to publish, so this page has none. A human reviewer should read the counts and the cannibalism paragraph with particular care.
