What to do now

  1. Write your medicines down. Name and strength of each one, what it is for, how much you take and when, plus your conditions, medical supplies and allergies. Keep a copy of your prescriptions with your emergency supplies.{{cite:health-fda-safe-drug-use}}
  2. Ask your pharmacist or prescriber about an emergency supply and about your state's emergency refill laws. If a storm or other disruption is coming and the pharmacy may be out of reach, ask about an early refill.{{cite:health-fda-safe-drug-use}}
  3. Find out what needs power or cold. List every device or medicine that depends on electricity or refrigeration, then ask your medical provider and the device maker what the backup is. Do it before an outage, not during one.{{cite:health-ready-power-outages}}
  4. Tell the people who can help. Let your electric company and fire department know you have a device that needs power, and ask your power provider about priority restoration.{{cite:health-fda-devices}}{{cite:health-ready-disabilities}}
  5. Do not decide on your own to stop, throw out or ration a prescribed medicine. If one got warm, hot or wet, check its label, then call a pharmacist, your prescriber or the manufacturer, and arrange a replacement. Every agency page below says to replace it quickly, and none says to go without.{{cite:health-fda-safe-drug-use}}{{cite:health-fema-power-outage-sheet}}

If you depend on a device to stay alive and it has lost power, call your emergency number now. The FDA says to seek emergency services immediately in that case.{{cite:health-fda-devices}} This page gives no medical instructions of its own.

What this guide is, and what it is not

Medicine and medical equipment are the part of emergency planning where a confident-sounding page can do the most harm. A drug that has lost strength looks the same as one that has not. A device that has reset itself to a default setting looks like it is working. For that reason this guide does something unusual for us: it gives you almost no numbers of its own.

Editor's note Everything here is a pointer to what the FDA, CDC, FEMA and HHS publish, together with the questions worth asking the people who actually know your situation: your prescriber, your pharmacist, and the company that made your device. We are not clinicians, and nothing here replaces them. We do not tell anyone to change a dose, stretch a supply, substitute one medicine for another or treat a symptom. None of the sources we read recommends rationing doses without a clinician's guidance, and we will not be the first to suggest it.

The one place the FDA does publish dose-related conversions, its insulin page, carries its own warning that switching should happen under a doctor's care with close monitoring of blood sugar. We link to it and do not copy it. Anyone who uses insulin should read the FDA's page directly, with their clinician.{{cite:health-fda-insulin}}

Start with a list and a short conversation

The FDA's advice before a disaster is practical and unglamorous. Keep an up-to-date list of your medicines with the name and strength of each, what it is for, how much you take and when. Include your medical conditions, medical supply needs and allergies. Keep a copy of your prescriptions with your emergency supplies. Know how much medicine you have on hand.{{cite:health-fda-safe-drug-use}}

The same FDA page then describes the conversation that turns the list into a plan. Talk with your pharmacist or health care provider about maintaining an emergency supply, and learn about your state's emergency refill laws. If you expect your access to a pharmacy to be disrupted, obtain early refills.{{cite:health-fda-safe-drug-use}} FEMA's disability page makes the same point from a different angle: about half of all Americans take a prescription medicine every day, and an emergency can make it hard to refill one or to find an open pharmacy.{{cite:health-ready-disabilities}}

Uncertain We do not know what your state's emergency refill law allows, how your insurer treats an early refill, or what an emergency supply of your particular medicine would cost. The sources we read say to ask; they do not answer. That is a real gap for anyone on a tight budget, and the honest advice is to ask the pharmacist what options exist before you need them.

Medicines that need to stay cold

Some medicines need refrigeration to keep their strength, and the FDA names insulin and certain liquid antibiotics as examples.{{cite:health-fda-safe-drug-use}} The first part of the plan is simple: the FDA says to have a plan for keeping them cold during an outage, with a cooler and ice or frozen gel packs available if needed, following the medicine's labeled storage instructions.{{cite:health-fda-safe-drug-use}} FEMA's disability page also lists a cooler and chemical ice packs for medicines that need refrigeration.{{cite:health-ready-disabilities}}

The harder part is what to do once the cold chain has already broken. Here the agencies speak in different registers, and it is better to see them side by side than to blend them.

Same direction, different emphasis

Uncertain The CDC and FEMA's Ready.gov page lead with a rule (discard after a day or more, unless the label says otherwise). FEMA's newer fact sheet softens the verb to "replace" and adds "call your doctor or pharmacist" for anyone who depends on the medicine. The FDA leads with a question (check the label or ask someone who knows). All of them share the exception for a life-sustaining medicine with no replacement, and all say to replace the medicine quickly. None gives a general number of hours that applies to every refrigerated drug, and neither do we.

Editor's note Read "discard" and "replace" the same way: they describe the medicine you will end up using, not permission to go without in the meantime. Whether to keep using a particular drug that has been warm is a decision for your pharmacist or prescriber, and for a life-sustaining drug every source gives the same answer: call, get a replacement, and use what you have only until the new supply arrives. Where FEMA's page and fact sheet differ in wording, we show both and follow the more cautious reading, which is to call a professional.

Editor's note The phrase "unless the drug's label says otherwise" carries a lot of weight in the CDC and FEMA wording. The label, and then the manufacturer, are where a drug-specific answer lives. That is why the practical step is to ask the pharmacist now, before an outage, how long each of your medicines tolerates being warm, and to write the answer on your sheet. FEMA recommends exactly that conversation.{{cite:health-ready-power-outages}}

Insulin: the one figure set the FDA publishes

The FDA's insulin page is specific because insulin labels are. It says that, according to the labels of the manufacturers it lists, insulin is stored in a refrigerator at roughly 36 degrees F to 46 degrees F, and that unopened insulin stored that way keeps its effectiveness until the expiration date. Insulin in vials or cartridges supplied by the manufacturers, opened or unopened, may be left unrefrigerated between 59 degrees F and 86 degrees F for up to 28 days and continue to work. Insulin that has been diluted or removed from the manufacturer's original vial should be thrown away within two weeks.{{cite:health-fda-insulin}}

The page adds that extreme temperatures make insulin less effective, that the longer it is exposed to extreme heat or cold the less effective it becomes, and that in emergency conditions you might still need to use insulin stored above 86 degrees F. That is the FDA's acknowledgment of a last-resort situation, not a recommendation; over time the heat can make blood sugar harder to control, so tell your clinician and ask for replacement insulin. The page tells you to keep it as cool as possible, to avoid freezing it when you use ice, and not to use insulin that has been frozen. Keep it away from direct heat and sunlight. Once you have properly stored insulin again, the FDA says to throw away vials that were exposed to extreme temperatures and replace them.{{cite:health-fda-insulin}} For insulin pumps, the page says insulin in the infusion set (reservoir, tubing, catheters) should be thrown away after 48 hours unless your pump's instructions differ, and that insulin exposed to temperatures above 98.6 degrees F should be thrown away.{{cite:health-fda-insulin}}

Editor's note These are the FDA's figures for the products on its page, quoted with their conditions. A different insulin product, a pen, or a newer product may have different label limits; the FDA page itself notes one weekly product with a longer window. Your product's label and your clinician's guidance come first. We add one observation: the 28-day room-temperature figure sits uneasily beside a blanket "discard after a day" rule. The FDA's insulin page is the more specific source for insulin, and the CDC and FEMA rule both begin with "unless the label says otherwise."{{cite:health-fda-insulin}}

When medicine meets heat, flood or unsafe water

The FDA draws a clear line. High temperatures from a fire can reduce or destroy a medicine's effectiveness, so consider replacing medicines that may have been exposed to excessive heat. Medicines that have been in contact with floodwater, or any water that may be contaminated, can become unsafe and should be discarded even if they are in their original containers with screw-top caps, snap lids or droppers.{{cite:health-fda-safe-drug-use}}

There is a narrow exception. If a medicine exposed to fire or unsafe water is needed to treat a life-threatening condition and a replacement is not readily available, the FDA says it may be used if it appears unchanged, and should be replaced as soon as possible. Medicine that is wet or otherwise altered should not be used; it is contaminated and should be discarded.{{cite:health-fda-safe-drug-use}} Medicines that must be mixed into a liquid should be reconstituted only with purified or bottled water, never another liquid.{{cite:health-fda-safe-drug-use}} How to make water safer, and why bottled water comes first, is covered in {{link:making-water-safer|making water safer in an emergency}}.

Editor's note Discarding a ruined medicine is not the same as going without it. The FDA's exception and its repeated "replace as soon as possible" are the reason to call your pharmacist or prescriber about a replacement before, not after, you throw out a medicine you depend on. "Appears unchanged" is the FDA's wording and it is a judgment call that a layperson cannot fully make. If you are in that situation, call a pharmacist, your prescriber or the poison center before relying on it; the agencies describe the exception, they do not describe how to verify it.

Equipment that runs on power

FEMA is blunt that a power outage may prevent the use of medical devices.{{cite:health-ready-power-outages}} The planning steps in the sources fall into five groups.

  1. Take an inventory. List the items you need that rely on electricity, then plan for batteries and other power sources, such as a portable charger or power bank, to meet those needs. FEMA also says to check whether your home phone will work in an outage and how long its battery backup will last.{{cite:health-ready-power-outages}}
  2. Ask the maker. Read your user instructions or call the distributor or manufacturer to find out whether the device can run on batteries or a generator.{{cite:health-fda-devices}}
  3. Ask your clinician. Talk to your doctor or health care provider about what you may be able to do to keep equipment running during an outage.{{cite:health-ready-disabilities}}
  4. Tell the utility and fire department. The FDA says to notify your electric company and fire department that you have a device that needs power. FEMA adds that you can ask your power provider to put you on a list for priority power restoration.{{cite:health-fda-devices}}{{cite:health-ready-disabilities}}
  5. Plan for the equipment itself. FEMA suggests a lightweight manual chair as a backup for a power wheelchair, extra batteries kept charged, a backup supply of oxygen, and extra hearing-aid batteries.{{cite:health-ready-disabilities}}

Generators and medical equipment: the carbon monoxide rule

Many home medical devices need electricity, so people turn to generators. The FDA's warning is direct: never run a generator, pressure washer or any gasoline-powered engine inside a basement, garage or other enclosed structure, even if the doors or windows are open, unless the equipment is professionally installed and vented.{{cite:health-fda-devices}} The CDC says never run one inside your home, basement or garage or less than 20 feet from any window, door or vent, and to use a battery-powered or battery-backup carbon monoxide detector.{{cite:health-cdc-power-outage}} If someone shows signs of carbon monoxide poisoning, call 911 or Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222.{{cite:health-cdc-power-outage}} The full generator and carbon monoxide picture belongs in its own guide; this is only the part a medical-device plan cannot skip.

Two more equipment cautions from the FDA are worth remembering. Do not plug in a power cord if the cord or the device is wet, and check cords and batteries for water damage.{{cite:health-fda-devices}} And if oxygen is in use, use battery-powered flashlights or lanterns rather than gas lights or torches, to minimize fire risk.{{cite:health-fda-devices}}

When power returns, the FDA says to check that your device's settings have not changed, because medical devices often reset to a default mode when power is interrupted.{{cite:health-fda-devices}} Heat and humidity can also affect home test kits, including glucose meters and test strips, so results may not be accurate; the FDA says to read the meter and strip package instructions for use in unusual heat and humidity, and to do the quality-control checks that confirm your testing is reliable.{{cite:health-fda-devices}}

Who else to tell, and what the programs actually are

Several programs sit in the background of this topic, and it is easy to assume they do more than they do.

Your utility's priority list. FEMA says you can ask your power provider to put you on a list for priority power restoration.{{cite:health-ready-disabilities}} Editor's note Treat that as a place in a queue, not a promise of power. The sources do not describe how quickly priority customers are restored, and a list cannot restore a line that is down.

Local registries. Many city and county emergency management agencies keep voluntary registries for people with disabilities to identify themselves for targeted help during emergencies; FEMA says to contact your local emergency management office to find out more.{{cite:health-ready-disabilities}} The FCC's communication tips similarly suggest registering with your local police department and reminding them to keep a record of the help you may need.{{cite:health-fcc-comms-tips}} Uncertain Whether your locality has such a registry, who runs it and what it can actually do is not something any federal page can tell you.

HHS emPOWER. The HHS emPOWER Program, run by ASPR with the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services, uses Medicare claims data to give public health authorities and responders de-identified counts of people who rely on electricity-dependent equipment and certain services, such as dialysis and home oxygen, so communities can plan for them. Its own materials say prolonged power outages can put these individuals in life-threatening situations within hours or days.{{cite:health-empower-about}} The program's pages we read address public health partners, not households, and we found no instruction there telling an individual how to enrol or what to do. Editor's note Its existence is a reason to take your own backup plan seriously. It is not something to rely on instead of one.

Your support network. FEMA suggests a network of people who can help in a disaster, with a contact list in a watertight container in your emergency kit or on your devices, and telling them where your supplies are kept.{{cite:health-ready-disabilities}} If you rely on a medical alert system, the FCC notes that most need a working phone line, so keep a backup such as a cell phone if landlines are disrupted.{{cite:health-fcc-comms-tips}}

Heat, cold and medication

FEMA's disability page notes that people with disabilities and older adults may be at increased risk in extreme heat, and that some medications can interfere with the body's ability to cool itself. Its advice is to read the side effects of your medications and talk with your doctor about how heat exposure will interact with them, and to ask your local energy utility for priority access if power restoration is life-sustaining for someone in the home.{{cite:health-ready-disabilities}} The CDC's outage page recommends seeking an air-conditioned shelter or other cool place if conditions are too hot, and notes that fans alone may not prevent heat-related illness when temperatures are really high.{{cite:health-cdc-power-outage}}

Options for different households

  • Tight budget. The list, the doctor and pharmacist conversation and a basic cooler cost little or nothing in themselves. Editor's note The costly items are a spare battery for a power wheelchair or a backup device. FEMA's guidance is to find out what agencies, organizations or local charitable groups can help you buy one if you cannot afford it.{{cite:health-ready-disabilities}}
  • Renters and apartment dwellers. Editor's note A generator is rarely an option in an apartment, and the sources' 20-foot rule is hard to meet in a building. A battery backup, a power bank, a plan to go to a relative or a shelter, and a conversation with your clinician matter more. FEMA suggests checking with local officials about heating and cooling locations and community locations with power.{{cite:health-ready-power-outages}}
  • People who use mobility, hearing, vision or communication devices. FEMA's page has specific, practical lists for each group, from a weather radio with text display and flashing alert to Braille labels on supplies and laminated communication cards. We point to the page rather than copy it.{{cite:health-ready-disabilities}}
  • People who care for someone else. Keep their list too, and keep it somewhere you can reach if you are separated. FEMA says not to leave a person with Alzheimer's or a related dementia alone, even someone who is not prone to wandering, because unfamiliar environments can change that.{{cite:health-ready-disabilities}}

When to stop reading and call

These steps cover planning and ordinary disruption. They do not cover an emergency in progress. Call your emergency number if someone who depends on a device to stay alive has lost power and cannot restore it, if someone shows signs of carbon monoxide poisoning, or if you are seriously ill or injured. For first aid and medical emergencies, rely on a clinician, emergency services and current training from organizations such as the American Red Cross or the American Heart Association; we have not reproduced their guidance here.

What this guide has and hasn't done

Every figure and instruction here comes from the FDA, CDC, FEMA and HHS pages cited, each read in full on the date shown. We have not tested a medicine, a device or a cooler, and we make no claim about how long any particular drug tolerates heat. We did not reproduce the FDA's insulin-switching tables, its list of manufacturer telephone numbers (the FDA says the numbers change), or its tips for specific devices beyond the general ones above.

The FDA's medical-device page also gives its own instructions for treating water for device use, using a different bleach dose than the one in our water guide. We reproduce neither, and we tell readers to take device-specific water advice from the manufacturer and general water advice from {{link:making-water-safer|our water guide}}; this discrepancy is flagged for the safety reviewer. Among the things we could not verify are state emergency-refill laws, whether your locality has a registry, how quickly priority customers are restored, and whether emPOWER offers any individual service.
