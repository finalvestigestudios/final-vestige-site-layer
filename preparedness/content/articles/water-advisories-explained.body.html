What to do now

  1. Read the exact words of your utility's notice. Your advisory names the danger and tells you what to do. The CDC's central instruction is to follow the recommendations in the advisory.{{cite:cdc-water-advisories}}
  2. Boil-water advisory: use commercially bottled water or boil your tap water at a full rolling boil for 1 minute (3 minutes above 6,500 feet), even if the water has been through a home filter.{{cite:cdc-water-advisories}}
  3. Do-not-drink advisory: boiling will not help. Use commercially bottled water for drinking, cooking, brushing teeth, formula, ice and pets.{{cite:cdc-water-advisories}}
  4. Do-not-use advisory: use bottled water for everything. Do not drink or use tap water for any purpose until it is lifted.{{cite:cdc-water-advisories}}
  5. Under any advisory, do not use water or ice from the dispensers in your refrigerator or freezer.{{cite:cdc-water-advisories}}

If you are unsure which advisory applies to you, assume the stricter one until your utility or health department tells you otherwise. If someone is ill after drinking the water, contact a health professional.

Why advisories exist

A water utility or government agency issues a drinking water advisory when tap water is, or could be, contaminated with something harmful. The CDC lists four kinds of contaminant: germs (such as norovirus or Shigella), chemicals (such as arsenic), toxins (such as the toxins from harmful algal blooms) and radioactive materials (such as uranium).{{cite:cdc-water-advisories}} The advisory then tells you how to avoid getting sick, and the advice depends on the type of germ or chemical involved.{{cite:cdc-water-advisories}}

The key phrase is "is, or could be." An advisory can be a precaution before anyone has been confirmed ill. The CDC's instruction is to follow the advisory either way.{{cite:cdc-water-advisories}} The EPA's emergency page gives examples of interruptions in regular service, such as a hurricane, flood or water pipe breakage, and says local authorities may then recommend using only bottled, boiled or disinfected water until service is restored.{{cite:epa-emergency-disinfection}}

The CDC identifies three kinds of drinking water advisory.{{cite:cdc-water-advisories}} They differ in what the danger is and in how much of your daily routine they touch.

Under a boil water advisory, room by room

A boil water advisory is the least restrictive of the three, and the CDC's advice is detailed. It breaks the routine down this way.{{cite:cdc-water-advisories}}

Drinking and cooking

Use commercially bottled water or boiled water to drink and to prepare and cook food. If you have no bottled water, boil your tap water to a full rolling boil for 1 minute, or 3 minutes above 6,500 feet, and let it cool before using it.{{cite:cdc-water-advisories}} Boil it even if it has been through a home filter or a pitcher that filters water.{{cite:cdc-water-advisories}}

Editor's note The CDC and the EPA set the high-altitude threshold differently (6,500 feet and 5,000 feet). We discuss the difference, and a cautious way to handle it, in the guide on {{link:making-water-safer|making water safer}}.

Infants

The CDC says breastfeeding is the best option for feeding an infant. If you feed your child formula, provide ready-to-use formula if possible.{{cite:cdc-water-advisories}} For preparing powdered formula during an advisory, the CDC's list of water uses to avoid includes making baby formula with water you know or suspect is unsafe.{{cite:cdc-water-safe}} Ask your pediatrician in advance about what to do if the water is not safe.

Hands, bathing and teeth

In many cases you can use tap water and soap to wash your hands during a boil water advisory, but follow your local officials' advice. Be careful not to swallow any water when bathing or showering, and consider a sponge bath for babies and young children to reduce the chance of swallowing water. Brush your teeth with boiled or commercially bottled water, not unboiled tap water.{{cite:cdc-water-advisories}}

Dishes

If possible, use disposable plates, cups and utensils, and sanitize all baby bottles. A dishwasher is generally safe if it has a sanitizing cycle or reaches a final rinse temperature of at least 150°F (66°C), and the CDC says to check the manual or contact the manufacturer to find out what yours reaches. To wash by hand:{{cite:cdc-water-advisories}}

  1. Wash and rinse the dishes as you normally would using hot water.
  2. In a separate basin, add 1 teaspoon of unscented household liquid bleach for each gallon of warm water.
  3. Soak the rinsed dishes in that water for at least one minute.
  4. Let the dishes air dry completely before using them again.

Laundry, cleaning, pets and plants

It is safe to wash clothes as usual. Clean washable toys and surfaces with commercially bottled water, boiled water, or water disinfected with bleach. Give pets commercially bottled water or boiled water that has cooled, because they can get sick from some of the same germs as people or spread germs to people. You can use tap water on houseplants and gardens, including plants you eat.{{cite:cdc-water-advisories}}

Under a do-not-drink advisory

Officials typically issue a do-not-drink advisory when tap water is, or could be, contaminated with harmful chemicals or toxins. The central warning is that boiling will not make the water safe.{{cite:cdc-water-advisories}} During one, the CDC says to use commercially bottled water for:{{cite:cdc-water-advisories}}

  • drinking and cooking;
  • brushing teeth;
  • washing fruits and vegetables;
  • preparing food;
  • mixing baby formula;
  • making ice;
  • giving water to pets.

The CDC adds that these advisories may include information about bathing, flushing toilets, washing dishes, cleaning and other activities, and that you should carefully follow the advice in your advisory.{{cite:cdc-water-advisories}} That is a real limit of this guide. The details of what is and is not safe for bathing depend on the contaminant, and only the notice for your supply can tell you.

Boiling is the wrong tool for chemicals

It is tempting to boil everything when in doubt. For chemicals and toxins, that does not help. The CDC says you cannot make water containing fuel or toxic chemicals safe by boiling or disinfecting it, and that you should use bottled water or another source instead.{{cite:cdc-find-clean-water}}{{cite:cdc-water-safe}}

Under a do-not-use advisory

This is the strictest and the rarest. Officials issue a do-not-use advisory when any contact with tap water could be dangerous, including contact with your skin, lungs or eyes, because the water is or could be contaminated with harmful germs, chemicals, toxins or radioactive materials. Use commercially bottled water, and do not drink or use tap water for any purpose as long as the advisory is in effect.{{cite:cdc-water-advisories}}

The CDC also notes that local officials may advise you to shut off the main water valve to your home so contaminants do not enter your household pipes.{{cite:cdc-find-clean-water}} That is something to do on instruction, not on a guess, and it is worth finding where your main shutoff is, and how it turns, before you ever need it.

What you can do before one is issued

The three advisories change how much bottled water you need and for what. Editor's note A do-not-use advisory has the highest demand, because every use of tap water has to be replaced, and a boil water advisory has the lowest, because you can boil what you have. The practical implication is that the water you store in advance, covered in {{link:water-math|water math}} and {{link:two-week-water-storage|two-week water storage}}, is the thing that turns an advisory from an emergency into an inconvenience.

A few other preparations follow directly from the CDC's text:

  • Keep a supply of unscented household bleach and a dropper, for the dish-soak step and for treating water, following the guidance in {{link:making-water-safer|making water safer}}.
  • Know which of your appliances connect to the water line, including the refrigerator dispenser and ice maker, so you can stop using them quickly.{{cite:cdc-water-advisories}}
  • Keep disposable plates, cups and utensils on hand, since the CDC prefers them during a boil water advisory.{{cite:cdc-water-advisories}}
  • If your household includes an infant, speak to your pediatrician beforehand about formula during an advisory, and keep ready-to-use formula in mind.{{cite:cdc-water-advisories}}

What an advisory will not tell you, and what we don't know

Three things are worth being honest about.

How long it will last. The CDC page does not say how long an advisory typically lasts, and neither do we. Your utility lifts the advisory, and that announcement is the signal, not the water looking or tasting normal.

What to do when it ends. The sources we read do not give general steps for flushing household pipes or appliances afterward. Your utility's notice may. We would rather say so than offer a procedure we have not verified.

What a given advisory is really about. A notice that says "boil" is about germs, and one that says "do not drink" is about chemicals or toxins. The notice for your supply is authoritative and this guide is not.
