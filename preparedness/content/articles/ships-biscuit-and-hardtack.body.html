Read this first

This is a history, not a method. Nothing on this page is a recipe, a bake time, or a storage instruction, and it should not be used as one.

  • Moldy bread and baked goods are not salvageable. USDA food-safety guidance is to discard them, because porous foods can be contaminated below the surface and some molds make toxins.{{cite:hist-food-fsis-molds}}
  • Do not trust a bread-like food just because it looks and feels dry. The historical record below is full of dry biscuit that went moldy, wormy or both once it got damp.{{cite:hist-food-nps-hardtack-mana}}
  • For emergency food you actually plan to rely on, use commercially made, labelled, shelf-stable products and rotate them. The Food section of this archive will grow to cover that; until then, FEMA's Ready.gov and your local Extension office are the places to start.

If you feel unwell after eating food you suspect was spoiled, contact a doctor or poison control. Call your emergency number for severe symptoms such as blurred vision, difficulty swallowing or breathing, or weakness.

The problem biscuit solved

A warship in 1813 could not count on a market, a bakery or a cold room. When USS Constitution was provisioned for a projected six-month cruise, the load-out recorded in the court of inquiry that later examined her captain included 84,456 pounds of bread, set against 57,700 pounds of beef and 50,600 pounds of pork.Documented history{{cite:usscm-brenckle-2019}} Bread was the heaviest single item in the hold. It was also the one that had to survive the longest, in the dampest place, in the care of men who could not replace it.

The answer the navies settled on was not "bread" in the sense a baker would use the word. The Constitution Museum's study describes ship's bread as an unleavened biscuit of flour and water, baked until thoroughly dry. Stored in a dry, ventilated bread room, it could last for years.{{cite:usscm-brenckle-2019}} The idea is simple and old: take the water out of a starch, keep water from getting back in, and the food stays edible far longer than a loaf would. This piece is about how well that idea held up in practice, and what its limits were.

It pairs with our companion piece on {{link:salt-beef-salt-pork-age-of-sail|salt beef and salt pork at sea}}, which covers the other half of the same ration, and with {{link:history-of-food-preservation|the wider history of food preservation}}.

What the record says it was

The word "hardtack" is what most readers will reach for, and it is not the period term for ship's bread. The museum study dates "hardtack" to the 1840s and says it spread during the American Civil War.{{cite:usscm-brenckle-2019}} We use "ship's biscuit" for the age of sail and "hardtack" for the soldier's version afterward, because the sources do.

The study quotes a post-war contract for Navy bread requiring wheat flour only, with no rye, and that the bread be thoroughly kiln dried before shipment. Surviving British biscuits are about half an inch thick, 4¾ to 5¾ inches wide and weigh about four ounces, which puts the 14-ounce daily ration at three to four pieces.{{cite:usscm-brenckle-2019}} A surviving example is in the collection of the National Maritime Museum at Greenwich: a biscuit made to an 1846 recipe, marked with a broad arrow and the letter C, measuring 15 by 135 millimetres.{{cite:rmg-biscuit-aab0766}}

That is nearly everything the sources tell us about the thing itself. They tell us what it was made of, how hard it was baked, how big it was and how much a sailor was issued. They do not give a household-scale method, and we do not try to reconstruct one. The surviving object and the contract describe a product made by a naval bakery to a specification; they are not instructions.

A caution about home "recipes"

Editor's note Some park-service and museum pages offer a hardtack recipe for schools and re-enactors. We have read one, and we have chosen not to reproduce or link it. A recipe that produces a cracker for a classroom demonstration is a very different thing from a method validated for long storage, and none of the food-safety sources we checked says anything about home-baked hardtack as an emergency food.

What a sailor and a soldier were issued

The 1813 weekly ration for an American sailor, as tabulated in the museum's study, included 98 ounces of bread, which is 14 ounces a day, alongside beef, pork, flour, suet, cheese, butter, peas, rice, molasses and vinegar.{{cite:usscm-brenckle-2019}} The same study warns that the table describes the ideal diet and "did not always reflect actual practice".

On land the figures were similar. An 1840s U.S. Army ration, as described by the National Park Service at Fort Scott, included 18 ounces of flour made into bread, baked on site, with hardtack issued when fresh bread was not available. On campaign about a pound of hardtack, nine or ten crackers, was issued to a soldier whether on the march or in camp.{{cite:hist-food-nps-fosc-rations}} The park's page credits its hardtack details to John D. Billings's memoir Hardtack and Coffee rather than to archival ration returns, which is worth remembering when weighing how precise the figures are.

Where it failed: damp, mold and insects

Every source that describes biscuit in use also describes its failure, and the failure is always the same one: water. The National Park Service's Manassas page says hardtack "could last a long time", noting that some Civil War hardtack survives in the park's museum. In the same breath it says the crackers were known for getting wet, which let mold grow in the boxes supplied to the army, and that insects sometimes laid eggs in them, earning the nickname "worm castles".{{cite:hist-food-nps-hardtack-mana}} The other nicknames the page records, "sheet iron crackers" and "tooth duller", are about hardness.

At Fort Scott the park lists the "common problems" with hardtack as being too hard, wet, moldy or infested with maggots and weevils, and records the soldiers' remedy: boil the hardtack in coffee and skim the weevils and maggots off the foam.{{cite:hist-food-nps-fosc-rations}} That remedy tells us how common the problem was. It is not evidence that the cracker was safe afterward.

The naval record is slightly more cautious. The museum study identifies real pests that could ruin a bag of biscuit (the cadelle beetle, the bread beetle and weevils) and records rats getting into the bread room of USS Essex on her passage to the Pacific. It also argues that the lurid versions of biscuit "crawling with weevils" were mostly the work of civilian writers, and notes that one sailor's recollection of moldy, grub-ridden biscuit on a round-the-world cruise in 1838 came from a ship that had been at sea for many months with probably infested stores.{{cite:usscm-brenckle-2019}} Royal Museums Greenwich lists poor storage, poor ventilation and vermin among the reasons ship's food deteriorated.{{cite:rmg-life-at-sea}}

Editor's note Putting those together gives a fair reading. Biscuit baked hard and kept dry lasted remarkably long; biscuit that was handled carelessly, shipped in damp boxes or stored in poor ventilation did not. The stories of the worst biscuit are real but are probably not typical, and the museum's historian says as much. The point for a modern reader is that the product had no margin: its safety depended on staying dry for its whole life, and a ship or a wagon is a place where that is hard to guarantee.

From the ship to the camp

The same product turns up on land with a new name. The Manassas page describes hardtack as made from flour, water and salt and as one of the most common meals for Civil War soldiers, one that gave them energy but that they disliked.{{cite:hist-food-nps-hardtack-mana}} Soldiers softened it in coffee, broke it up into stews such as the one the page calls "lobscouse", or soaked and fried it in bacon fat into what they called "skillygalee".{{cite:hist-food-nps-hardtack-mana}} Fort Scott records the same cooking uses, and the parody of a popular song that soldiers sang about hard crackers.{{cite:hist-food-nps-fosc-rations}}

The Manassas page adds a detail about supply: Confederate forces did not always have hardtack because it was made from wheat flour, and wheat was not widely grown in most Confederate states beyond Virginia and Georgia, so they leaned on corn-based breads instead.{{cite:hist-food-nps-hardtack-mana}} It is a useful reminder that "preserved starch" was always tied to what a region grew and could mill, and that a ration built around one grain fails when that grain is scarce.

On the overland trail

Captain Randolph Marcy's 1859 handbook for overland emigrants, digitized by the Library of Congress, recommends for each grown person on the journey from the Missouri River to California 150 pounds of flour "or its equivalent in hard bread", with bacon or pork, coffee and sugar, and fresh beef driven on the hoof.{{cite:hist-food-marcy-prairie-traveler-text}}{{cite:loc-prairie-traveler}} Note the phrase: hard bread is offered as one of two ways to carry the same flour. Marcy treats biscuit as a convenience of packing, not as a food with magic keeping qualities. The wider story of that guidebook and of what emigrants actually carried is in {{link:pioneer-travel-rations|our piece on pioneer travel rations}}.

A note on the sources: the Library of Congress digitization we link is the 1861 Harper printing; the book was first published in 1859, and the 1861 copy is the one we read. Marcy's text was checked in an Internet Archive text of the same scan, because the Library of Congress page-level text view would not load for us.

The logic, and where it stops

The sources do not explain why dry biscuit lasts, and we are not going to supply a food-science lecture they do not contain. What they do show is a clear pattern, which we label as our reading:

  • Editor's note Dryness was the whole preservation method. Where the record describes successful long storage, the bread is dry and the room is dry and ventilated. Where it describes failure, the bread has been wetted.
  • Editor's note Dryness protects against the failures that need water, such as mold, but does nothing against insects and rodents, which the record shows getting into it.
  • Editor's note Dryness is a property of the biscuit and of its surroundings, and the surroundings are the part nobody on a ship or a wagon controlled.

USDA's own guidance on mold makes a related point in modern terms: molds tolerate salt and sugar better than most other food invaders, and mold roots can run deep inside food where they are invisible.{{cite:hist-food-fsis-molds}} That page does not mention biscuit or hardtack, and we are not claiming it does. But it is a reminder that salt in the dough, as the Manassas page describes, is not a shield.

What biscuit did not provide

A dry cracker is calories, not a balanced diet. Royal Museums Greenwich is direct that poor diet at sea caused malnutrition and specific diseases such as scurvy, which results from a lack of vitamin C.{{cite:rmg-life-at-sea}} A chaplain aboard USS Congress described the sailor's bread as so hard the hammer could scarcely break it, with a diet of meat "as juiceless and dry as the bones" and none of the fresh garden produce left behind.{{cite:usscm-brenckle-2019}} The scurvy story, and why salt provisions and biscuit together could keep a crew fed and still make it ill, is told in our {{link:salt-beef-salt-pork-age-of-sail|salt-provisions piece}}. Marcy himself recommended that overland travellers carry antiscorbutics, including dried or canned vegetables, because they could not get fresh ones.{{cite:hist-food-marcy-prairie-traveler-text}}

Current safety verdict

Not validated for modern household use

Do not rely on home-baked hardtack or ship's biscuit as a stored emergency food. The historical record shows it lasting when kept dry and failing when it was not, and we found no modern food-safety source that tests or endorses a home method.

  • USDA FSIS says to discard moldy bread and baked goods, because porous foods can be contaminated below the surface, and warns that some molds produce mycotoxins that can make people sick.{{cite:hist-food-fsis-molds}}
  • FSIS also says you should not sniff moldy food, since that can cause respiratory trouble.{{cite:hist-food-fsis-molds}}
  • The soldiers' habit of boiling hardtack in coffee and skimming off the insects shows how common infestation was. It is not a treatment.{{cite:hist-food-nps-fosc-rations}}

We read no source that studies how long a home-baked cracker stays safe, so this page gives no storage time and no bake instructions. Our FSIS source is also an older page (last updated 2013); check the agency's current guidance before relying on it.

Then: the biscuit

Documented history Flour-and-water biscuit baked very dry, packed, shipped and issued by the pound. It lasted when it stayed dry and failed to damp, mold and insects. It supplied calories and nothing else, and a crew living on it needed other food to stay well.{{cite:usscm-brenckle-2019}}{{cite:hist-food-nps-fosc-rations}}

Now: the labelled, sealed product

Official guidance Choose commercially packaged, shelf-stable foods you can inspect and rotate, and discard bread products that show mold.{{cite:hist-food-fsis-molds}} Treat any dry food as perishable if it gets damp.

What we have not done

This piece leans heavily on one long secondary study, the USS Constitution Museum's, and on two National Park Service interpretive pages. The study's footnotes point to National Archives contracts and to naval regulations we have not pulled individually, so claims attributed to them are reported as the museum study reports them. The two park pages are interpretive, one of them aimed at young visitors, and they lean on Billings's 1887 memoir; we have not read the memoir. The Royal Navy's biscuit, made in its own bakeries to its own specification, is only touched here through a single museum object.

We did not find a modern regulator source that addresses hardtack or home-baked biscuit directly. The FSIS page we cite is a general mold guide and we have said so. We have no image of a biscuit that we have the rights to publish, so this page has none. The page remains a draft until a human reviewer has read the verdict and the editor has signed off.
