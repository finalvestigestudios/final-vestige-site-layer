What to do now

  1. Keep the refrigerator and freezer doors shut. An unopened refrigerator keeps food cold for about 4 hours. A full freezer holds its temperature for about 48 hours, a half-full one for about 24.{{cite:fsis-power-failure}}{{cite:food-cdc-after-emergency}}
  2. Watch the clock from the moment the power goes out, and write the time down. If it has been 4 hours and you have a cooler and ice, move the perishable food into the cooler and keep it at 40°F or below with ice or frozen gel packs.{{cite:food-cdc-after-emergency}}
  3. Do not taste food to decide whether it is safe. After the power returns, throw out perishable food (meat, fish, eggs, milk, cut fruits and vegetables, leftovers) that spent more than 4 hours without power or a cold source, and anything with an unusual smell, colour or texture.{{cite:food-cdc-after-emergency}}
  4. Frozen food that still has ice crystals, or is at 40°F or below, can be refrozen or cooked. Quality may suffer. Safety depends on the food, so check the chart further down.{{cite:food-cdc-after-emergency}}{{cite:food-foodsafety-outage-chart}}
  5. When in doubt, throw it out.{{cite:food-fsis-emergencies}}

If someone is seriously ill after eating, or cannot keep fluids down, call your emergency number or a clinician rather than relying on this page. The USDA Meat and Poultry Hotline (1-888-674-6854, Monday to Friday, 10 a.m. to 6 p.m. Eastern) answers food-safety questions.{{cite:food-fsis-emergencies}} Refrigerated medicines such as insulin are a medical question, not a food one. FEMA's December 2023 power-outage fact sheet says to make a plan with your doctor for refrigerating medicines and powering medical devices. It also says that if the power is out for a day or more, refrigerated medications should be replaced unless the label says otherwise, that you should call your doctor or pharmacist if you depend on refrigerated medication that has been at room temperature, and that you should use the medicine only until you have a new supply.{{cite:food-fema-outage-sheet}} Editor's note Do not stop or skip a prescribed medicine on your own, and do not wait for an outage to ask: call your pharmacist or prescriber ahead of time, and again if your medicine has warmed.

Two clocks, and why people merge them

Every page we read gives the same headline figure: a closed refrigerator keeps food safe for about 4 hours.{{cite:fsis-power-failure}}{{cite:food-cdc-after-emergency}}{{cite:food-fda-outage-flood}}{{cite:food-ready-food}}{{cite:food-fema-outage-sheet}} Most of those pages also contain a second figure, 2 hours, and the two get tangled in memory. They measure different things.

  • The 4-hour figure is how long a refrigerator that was cold, full and kept shut is expected to stay cold after it stops running. It is a property of the appliance. FDA says "about 4 hours".{{cite:food-fda-outage-flood}}
  • The 2-hour figure is how long a perishable food may spend at unsafe temperatures. FoodSafety.gov's refrigerated-food chart is built on it: its question is whether the food was exposed to 40°F or above for more than 2 hours.{{cite:food-foodsafety-outage-chart}} FDA says to discard perishable food held at room temperature for 2 hours or more, or 1 hour if it is above 90°F outdoors.{{cite:food-fda-outage-flood}}

Editor's note Put together, the numbers describe an expectation, not a guarantee. A full, cold, unopened refrigerator may reach the end of its 4 hours with food still at a safe temperature; after that the clock for time above 40°F is running. A half-empty refrigerator that was opened several times has probably used up some of its allowance already. The sources say "about 4 hours" and do not tell you how to adjust for either case.

Where the sources differ

FDA alone allows a measured 45°F as a pass, and says to cook and eat such food quickly. FSIS and CDC state a flat 4-hour cut-off and do not mention 45°F. FEMA's own two documents differ: its Ready.gov Food page says to discard perishables above 40°F for two hours or more, while its newer December 2023 fact sheet says more than 4 hours above 40°F. The table lists both. The newer document is the December 2023 fact sheet, and the 2-hour figure is the more cautious of the two.

Editor's note We take the cautious reading: treat 4 hours without power as the outer limit for a closed, cold, well-stocked refrigerator, and count any time above 40°F separately and strictly. If you have no thermometer reading and you are unsure how long the power was out, discard. That synthesis is ours, not any agency's, and it is one of the items the human safety reviewer is asked to confirm.

Before an outage: the cheap preparations

Every item below appears on at least two of the official pages. None requires buying much.

  • Put an appliance thermometer in the refrigerator and another in the freezer. Aim for 40°F or below in the refrigerator and 0°F or below in the freezer. {{cite:food-fsis-emergencies}}{{cite:food-cdc-after-emergency}}{{cite:food-fda-outage-flood}}
  • Fill empty freezer space with containers of water (frozen plastic jugs work) and freeze gel packs. They keep the freezer colder longer and can fill a cooler later.{{cite:food-fsis-emergencies}}{{cite:food-fda-outage-flood}} FDA adds that melting ice can also supply drinking water if the normal supply is unavailable.{{cite:food-fda-outage-flood}}
  • Group food together in the refrigerator and freezer, so the cold is shared.{{cite:food-fsis-emergencies}}
  • Freeze what you will not need soon: leftovers, milk, fresh meat and poultry. Frozen food stays at a safe temperature longer than refrigerated food.{{cite:food-fda-outage-flood}}
  • Have a large insulated cooler and know where to buy dry ice or block ice.{{cite:food-fsis-emergencies}}{{cite:food-cdc-after-emergency}}

Editor's note If money is tight, start with the two preparations that need little or no spending: an appliance thermometer for each compartment, and water frozen in containers you already own. You do not need dry ice to follow any of the advice on this page. For renters and apartment dwellers with a small freezer, the same logic applies at a smaller scale: a freezer packed with frozen water bottles holds cold better than one with empty air in it. For anyone who cannot lift heavy coolers or 50-pound blocks of ice, smaller soft-sided coolers and gel packs split the load.

During an outage

  1. Keep doors closed.{{cite:food-fsis-emergencies}}
  2. Plan to eat from the refrigerator first, then the freezer, then the pantry. That is the order Oregon State Extension recommends, with its own condition that you keep watching temperatures and discard food as the numbers require.{{cite:food-osu-survival-food}}
  3. At the 4-hour mark, transfer the perishables to a cooler with ice or frozen gel packs, and add more ice as it melts. The aim is 40°F or below, checked with a thermometer.{{cite:food-fsis-emergencies}}{{cite:food-cdc-after-emergency}}
  4. In a freezer, small items thaw first. Food at the front, in the door, or in small thin packages defrosts faster than large thick items or food at the back or bottom. If the freezer is not full, quickly group packages together.{{cite:food-fsis-emergencies}}{{cite:fsis-power-failure}}
  5. Do not put perishable food out in the snow. FSIS says outdoor temperatures vary and the food can be exposed to unsanitary conditions and animals. Instead, fill buckets or empty containers with water, leave them outside to freeze, and use the ice in your coolers.{{cite:food-fsis-emergencies}}
  6. If you plan to eat refrigerated or frozen meat, poultry, fish or eggs that are still at a safe temperature, FDA says to cook them thoroughly to a safe minimum internal temperature. It also says that perishable food held at room temperature for 2 hours or more (1 hour if it is above 90°F outdoors) should be discarded, however well it is cooked.{{cite:food-fda-outage-flood}}

Dry ice and block ice

FSIS and FDA both say 50 pounds of dry ice should keep an 18-cubic-foot, fully stocked freezer cold for two days.{{cite:food-fsis-emergencies}}{{cite:food-fda-outage-flood}} Ready.gov gives a different example: 25 pounds of dry ice for a 10-cubic-foot freezer, for three to four days.{{cite:food-ready-food}} Those are not the same freezer or the same claim, so they are not a contradiction, but neither is a formula you can scale to your own appliance. Treat them as order-of-magnitude examples.

Handling: FSIS says not to touch dry ice with bare hands or let it touch food directly.{{cite:food-fsis-emergencies}} Ready.gov says to wear dry, heavy gloves.{{cite:food-ready-food}} Editor's note None of the pages we read says anything about ventilation. Dry ice is frozen carbon dioxide, and we have not read a source that tells you how much air you need around it, so ask the supplier for its handling instructions when you buy.

A word about generators

Running a refrigerator from a generator brings fuel and exhaust hazards that have nothing to do with food safety. FEMA's fact sheet says to use generators outdoors and at least 20 feet from building openings.{{cite:food-fema-outage-sheet}} This guide does not cover the rest of generator safety; see {{link:outage-heat-cold-generators|our guide to heat, cold and generator safety}}. Editor's note We would not improvise a power setup to save the contents of a refrigerator.

After the power returns

These steps combine the CDC and FDA pages, which agree with each other here.{{cite:food-cdc-after-emergency}}{{cite:food-fda-outage-flood}}

  1. Read the thermometers. If the freezer thermometer reads 40°F or below, FDA says the food is safe and may be refrozen. For the refrigerator, CDC says to check whether the thermometer is still at 40°F or below, but its discard rule for perishables is written in hours without power or a cold source, not in the reading. Editor's note That is why we would not treat a refrigerator reading of, say, 38°F after a long outage as rescuing the milk by itself. It is our reading of how the two statements fit, and the reviewer is asked to confirm it.
  2. If the freezer had no thermometer, check each package. Food that still contains ice crystals, or is 40°F or below, can be refrozen or cooked. You cannot judge by appearance or smell.
  3. Check coolers the same way. Throw out food that has thawed or is above 40°F.
  4. Discard the perishable food the charts below call unsafe.

Where the sources differ: opened juice

The FSIS list of foods to discard includes "fruit and vegetable juices (opened)".{{cite:food-fsis-emergencies}} The FoodSafety.gov chart says opened fruit juice can be kept and opened vegetable juice should be discarded.{{cite:food-foodsafety-outage-chart}} Both discard vegetable juice. For opened fruit juice the two pages disagree.

Editor's note We would discard it, since the FSIS page is the more cautious. This is flagged for the reviewer.

One more thing you may run into elsewhere. An Oregon State Extension publication notes that eggs kept at room temperature can last up to six days unless they were washed or cooked, and in the same passage says most commercially purchased eggs have been washed and should be discarded.{{cite:food-osu-survival-food}} So for shop-bought eggs no source we read says to keep them after an outage: FSIS and the FoodSafety.gov chart both say discard.{{cite:food-fsis-emergencies}}{{cite:food-foodsafety-outage-chart}}

Reading your body, too

Foodborne illness usually appears within 1 to 3 days of eating the contaminated food, but FDA says it can appear within 20 minutes or as late as 6 weeks afterwards. Symptoms can include vomiting, diarrhea and abdominal pain, or flu-like fever, headache and body ache. If you think you or a family member has a foodborne illness, FDA says to contact a healthcare provider promptly.{{cite:food-fda-outage-flood}} The long window is why "it didn't make me sick last time" is poor evidence that a particular practice is safe.

The CDC says that people 65 and older, children under 5, people with weakened immune systems (for example from diabetes, liver or kidney disease, HIV, or chemotherapy) and pregnant women are at higher risk of severe illness from food poisoning, and that it is especially important for them to take steps to prevent it.{{cite:food-cdc-risk-factors}} Editor's note The outage pages we read give one set of time limits for everyone and no separate, stricter rule for these groups, and we did not read CDC's separate "Safer Food Choices" pages for them. If anyone in your household is in one of those groups, we would treat the charts as a minimum, discard in doubtful cases, and ask a clinician beforehand how they want you to handle it.

Floods, fires and cans

If the outage came with a flood, a different set of rules applies. The official pages say not to eat food that touched floodwater, to discard food in containers that are not waterproof (screw caps, snap lids, pull tops, crimped caps), and to discard home-canned foods and cardboard cartons that were in contact with floodwater because they cannot be cleaned and sanitized. Undamaged commercially made all-metal cans and retort pouches can be saved if washed and sanitized.{{cite:food-fsis-emergencies}}{{cite:food-fda-outage-flood}}{{cite:food-cdc-after-emergency}} After a fire, FSIS says to discard food that was near it, including canned goods, because heat and fumes can damage the containers.{{cite:food-fsis-emergencies}}

We deliberately do not give a bleach recipe here

The four pages that give sanitizing amounts do not agree with each other. FSIS gives 1 tablespoon of unscented bleach per gallon of water for utensils, counters and cans.{{cite:food-fsis-emergencies}} FDA gives the same 1 tablespoon per gallon for utensils and counters, but 1 cup of bleach in 5 gallons of water for cans.{{cite:food-fda-outage-flood}} CDC gives 1 cup in 5 gallons for dishes, surfaces and cans.{{cite:food-cdc-after-emergency}} Ready.gov gives 1 part bleach to 10 parts water for cans.{{cite:food-ready-food}} Those are different strengths, with different soak times. Read the page for your situation and follow one of them exactly; do not blend them.

Editor's note We have flagged this disagreement for the reviewer. It is another reason we link out instead of reprinting figures that do not match.

When your household is not the textbook case

  • Infants. Ready.gov recommends ready-to-feed formula, and says that if you must mix formula you should use bottled water, or boiled water as a last resort.{{cite:food-ready-food}} Opened formula is a "discard" on the chart.{{cite:food-foodsafety-outage-chart}}
  • Pets. None of the pages we read covers pet food in an outage. Follow the manufacturer's label and the same time-and-temperature common sense. We have no official figure to give you.
  • Medically dependent households. If a freezer or refrigerator holds medication or breast milk, talk to the prescriber, pharmacist or provider in advance; see the FEMA medication wording in the box at the top. We have no official figure for breast milk.
  • Repeated short outages. The sources read treat an outage as a single event and give no rule for adding up several short ones. Editor's note If your power cuts in and out, the time without power accumulates and you may not know how much. An appliance thermometer shows how cold the refrigerator is now; it cannot show how long it was warm.

What this guide has and hasn't done

Every figure above comes from the FSIS, CDC, FDA, FoodSafety.gov, Ready.gov and Extension pages cited, each read in full on the date shown. Where they differ, the page says so. We did not test any refrigerator or freezer. We cannot tell you how long your appliance will hold its temperature; the 4-hour and 48-hour numbers are rules of thumb ("about" and "approximately" in the sources) for a full, closed unit that started out cold. They do not cover a crowded or empty unit, a refrigerator in a hot kitchen, a chest freezer versus an upright, or an older appliance with a failing seal, and none of the pages we read addresses those.

We read FEMA's December 2023 fact sheet only as text extracted from a local copy of its PDF (one page, read in full) and did not read the older 2020 FEMA wording on medicines. We also did not read the CDC's printable "Eat Safe Food After a Power Outage" infographic, the FSIS Hotline's own scripts, or any individual food entry in the FoodKeeper database. The safety review for this guide is recorded in the box at the bottom of the page. It does not replace an editor's read-through or the launch checks, and the page stays a draft until those are done.
