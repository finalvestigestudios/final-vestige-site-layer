What to do now

  1. Check that your phone can receive Wireless Emergency Alerts. Look in your phone settings and user manual. Older phones may not be capable, and some models require you to turn WEAs on. Some carriers label them "Government Alerts" or "Emergency Alert Messages."{{cite:health-ready-alerts}}
  2. Get a NOAA Weather Radio. Look for tone alert, SAME programming for your county, and battery backup.{{cite:nws-nwr}} FEMA's basic kit lists one with tone alert, plus a separate battery-powered or hand-crank radio.{{cite:health-ready-kit}}
  3. Use more than one channel. A phone alert, a radio, a TV or radio broadcast and a word from neighbors fail in different ways. FEMA's own alerting system sends through several at once for exactly that reason.{{cite:health-fema-ipaws}}
  4. Put local paper maps in your kit, and make sure your phone has a charger and a backup battery.{{cite:health-ready-kit}}
  5. Agree on how you will reach each other if calls will not go through: text first, calls short, and one contact outside your area.{{cite:health-fcc-comms-tips}}

An alert tells you to act. It is not a substitute for your local officials' instructions. If a life is at risk, call your emergency number.

The problem: you cannot act on a warning you did not get

Warnings save lives only if they reach people in time, and the channels that carry them have different weak points. A phone can be switched off, out of battery, out of range or too old to receive the alert. A radio can be unplugged or out of batteries. A transmitter can be damaged. A broadcaster can be off the air. No single channel is dependable enough to carry the whole load, and the national system is designed on that assumption.

FEMA describes its Integrated Public Alert and Warning System (IPAWS) as a way for authorized officials to write one message, have it authenticated, and deliver it through multiple pathways at once, because using multiple pathways increases the likelihood that the message will reach the public.{{cite:health-fema-ipaws}} This guide takes the same view from the household's side: build a small set of redundant ways to be warned, and a small set of ways to reach each other once the usual ones are strained.

Editor's note We also want to be plain about what this guide cannot tell you. It cannot say which radio station serves your county, whether your local government runs its own sign-up alert system, or how good your cell coverage is. Those are local facts. We point you to where the national sources say to look.

Wireless Emergency Alerts

WEAs are the alerts that make your phone buzz and chirp at once. They are sent by authorized public safety officials, including state, local, tribal and territorial officials, the National Weather Service, the National Center for Missing and Exploited Children and the President, through FEMA's IPAWS to wireless providers, which push them from cell towers to devices in the affected area.{{cite:health-ready-alerts}}{{cite:health-fcc-wea}} FEMA says WEAs look like text messages but have a unique sound and vibration repeated twice, are not affected by network congestion, will not disrupt texts, calls or data sessions in progress, cost nothing to receive, and need no subscription.{{cite:health-ready-alerts}} FEMA's IPAWS page goes further, saying phones receive WEAs by location even if cellular networks are overloaded and can no longer support calls, texts and emails.{{cite:health-fema-ipaws}}

Two practical cautions. First, check your phone: Ready.gov says older phones may not be WEA capable, some models require you to enable WEAs, and all major phone providers and some smaller ones participate; the FCC keeps a registry of participating providers.{{cite:health-ready-alerts}} The FCC adds that provider participation is voluntary, though those that offer WEA must meet its requirements.{{cite:health-fcc-wea}} Second, talk to children. FEMA notes that children receive WEAs on their own phones and suggests parents and guardians explain them in advance.{{cite:health-ready-alerts}}

A phone alert depends on a working phone and a working tower

Editor's note WEA is designed to bypass the congestion that clogs calls and texts. That does not mean it works when the tower itself is down, out of power or damaged. It cannot reach a phone that is off, flat or out of range, either. The FCC's own WEA page lists a 2023 item seeking partners to test delivering wireless alerts during cell tower outages; we did not read that item, but the existence of the project suggests the gap is real and not yet solved.{{cite:health-fcc-wea}} That is the reason to keep a radio that does not depend on a cell tower.

NOAA Weather Radio, in plain terms

NOAA Weather Radio All Hazards is a nationwide network of radio stations that broadcasts official warnings, watches, forecasts and other hazard information 24 hours a day, every day, from the nearest National Weather Service office. It works with the FCC's Emergency Alert System, and its broadcasts cover more than weather: the NWS page lists earthquakes and avalanches, chemical releases and oil spills, and public safety alerts such as AMBER alerts and 911 telephone outages.{{cite:health-nws-nwr-national}} The network includes more than 1,000 transmitters covering all 50 states, adjacent coastal waters, Puerto Rico, the U.S. Virgin Islands and the U.S. Pacific territories.{{cite:health-nws-nwr-national}}

Two facts that surprise people. First, it needs a special radio, or a scanner able to pick up its signal; an ordinary AM/FM radio will not receive it.{{cite:health-nws-nwr-national}} Second, it broadcasts on seven frequencies in the VHF public service band: 162.400, 162.425, 162.450, 162.475, 162.500, 162.525 and 162.550 MHz.{{cite:health-nws-nwr-national}} The NWS also publishes station listings, county listings and coverage maps so you can find the transmitter that serves your county.{{cite:health-nws-nwr-national}}

What to look for in a receiver

The National Weather Service lists three features that make a weather radio more useful for emergencies.{{cite:nws-nwr}}

  • Tone alert. The radio sounds an alarm when a warning is issued, and can sit in standby mode so it wakes you overnight.
  • SAME (Specific Area Message Encoding). You program the radio to alert only for your county. Transmitters cover several counties, and you may not want alarms for the next one over.
  • Battery backup. It keeps the radio working when the power is out.

For people who are deaf or hard of hearing, FEMA's disability page lists a weather radio with a text display and a flashing alert, along with extra hearing-aid batteries, pen and paper, and a battery-operated lantern so that signing or lip reading remains possible in the dark.{{cite:health-ready-disabilities}}

Transmitters fail too

Official guidance When we read the NWS national page, it listed transmitters that were degraded or out of service, with reasons that included antenna damage, an inoperable transmitter and a telecommunications issue, and said for each that listeners should check the county coverage listings for alternate transmitters. The legend notes that an out-of-service transmitter is temporarily non-operational due to problems such as a power outage or antenna damage.{{cite:health-nws-nwr-national}}

Editor's note A weather radio is a strong layer. It is not an infallible one. The NWS publishes an outage list because outages happen. Check that your county has an alternate transmitter, and treat a silent radio with some suspicion. Pair it with at least one other layer.

Uncertain The pages we read do not compare receiver models, state prices, or say how often a tone alert sounds for a warning that turns out not to affect you. We make no recommendation of a brand.

The Emergency Alert System and local alerting

The Emergency Alert System is the older channel. Ready.gov describes it as a national public warning system that allows the president to address the nation within 10 minutes during a national emergency; other authorized federal, state, local, tribal and territorial authorities can use it for weather information, imminent threats, AMBER alerts and local incident information targeted to specific areas.{{cite:health-ready-alerts}} It is delivered through broadcast TV and radio, satellite digital audio services, direct broadcast satellite providers, cable systems and wireless cable systems, and it is also used when all other means of alerting the public are unavailable.{{cite:health-ready-alerts}}

IPAWS is the plumbing behind these channels. Local alerting authorities, of which FEMA counts more than 2,000, write their own messages; FEMA does not review, edit or approve them.{{cite:health-fema-ipaws}}{{cite:health-ready-alerts}} The same system can feed sirens, digital road signs and emergency telephone networks in communities that use them.{{cite:health-fema-ipaws}}

Uncertain Whether your county or city runs a sign-up alert system of its own is a local question the national pages cannot answer. Ready.gov says to contact local officials directly about alerts in your area.{{cite:health-ready-alerts}} Ask your county emergency management office what it uses, and how to sign up.

The FEMA App

The FEMA App provides real-time weather and emergency alerts from the National Weather Service for up to five locations anywhere in the nation, helps you find a nearby shelter and sends notifications to loved ones.{{cite:health-ready-alerts}}{{cite:health-ready-disabilities}} Editor's note The "up to five locations" feature is worth a moment's thought. You can follow your home, your workplace, your child's school, and a parent or sibling in another state, so that you hear about their weather before they have to call you. It still depends on a charged phone and a data connection, so it adds a layer but does not replace a radio.

When calls will not go through

During an emergency, many more people try to use their phones at the same moment than in normal times, and the extra volume can create network congestion. The FCC's tips for communicating in an emergency, written to help your call get through, are short and worth knowing by heart.{{cite:health-fcc-comms-tips}}

  1. Limit non-emergency calls. That frees up capacity for emergency communications and conserves battery.
  2. Keep calls brief, and use them to pass on vital information.
  3. Try text messages. The FCC says text messages will often go through when a call will not.
  4. If one service fails, try another: text, email, a landline.
  5. Wait 10 seconds before redialing. The FCC explains that redialing too quickly sends the same data before the handset-to-tower data has cleared, contributing to a clogged network.
  6. Have charged batteries and car-charger adapters ready for backup power, and keep a list of emergency numbers in your phone.
  7. If you are in a vehicle, place calls while it is stationary.
  8. Have a family communications plan with someone out of the area as the central contact, and make sure every family member knows who to contact if separated.
  9. If your home number has Call Forwarding, forward it to your wireless number before an evacuation.
  10. Keep at least one corded telephone that does not depend on electricity; cordless phones usually do not work in a power outage.

FEMA's Make a Plan page frames the same thing as questions to settle with your household: how will we receive alerts, what is our shelter plan, what is our evacuation route, and what is our communication plan. It adds that you should know how you will contact one another and reconnect if separated, pick a familiar and easy-to-find meeting place, and practice the plan.{{cite:health-ready-plan}} For a full treatment, see the family communication plan guide in this section.

Uncertain The FCC page is undated and some of its wording (pagers, PDAs, "text radio") reflects older technology. Its core advice on congestion is general and still holds in the sources we read, but we cannot say how well each tip applies to every modern network or phone.{{cite:health-fcc-comms-tips}}

Maps and finding your way

FEMA's basic kit lists local maps, and, separately, a cell phone with chargers and a backup battery.{{cite:health-ready-kit}} FEMA's planning questions include "What is my evacuation route?"{{cite:health-ready-plan}} Those are the only map-related statements we found in the sources we read.

Uncertain None of the federal pages we read discusses downloadable offline map apps, how to prepare them, or how reliable they are in an outage. We therefore cannot cite an authority for them, and nothing in this section about phone maps is official guidance.

Editor's note Our reasoning, for what it is worth. A paper map needs no battery, no signal and no data, which is why we are comfortable repeating FEMA's line. A phone map is more convenient but depends on a charged phone and either a connection or something saved in advance. Whichever you rely on, practice using it with your phone's network switched off, before you need it, using a spare or nonessential phone or tablet if you can (never disable a phone you rely on for a medical alert, medical-device app or to call for help), and mark two routes out of your area, one of which avoids the main road, on paper. Write down the address and phone number of your meeting place and your out-of-area contact; a flat phone takes the address book with it.

Make it routine

  • Once a season: check that your phone receives alerts, test the weather radio's battery backup, and replace batteries. FEMA's kit page says to re-think your needs every year and replace expired items.{{cite:health-ready-kit}}
  • After you move or change phones: reprogram the SAME county code, and check WEA settings on the new phone.{{cite:nws-nwr}}{{cite:health-ready-alerts}}
  • With your household: practice the plan.{{cite:health-ready-plan}} Editor's note A warning is only useful if everyone knows what it means they should do. Decide in advance where the household goes when a tornado warning sounds, and where it meets afterward.

The NWS national page also carries a notice about one receiver model that beeps if it has gone ten days without receiving its expected weekly test from the National Weather Service. We mention it because it shows that NWR radios are tested on a schedule, and that a radio that has lost its signal can quietly stop being useful.{{cite:health-nws-nwr-national}}

Options for different households

  • Tight budget. Checking WEA settings and learning the FCC's calling tips cost nothing. FEMA says WEA is free to receive and needs no subscription.{{cite:health-ready-alerts}} A weather radio is the one item to budget for; the sources we read give no prices.
  • Renters. A battery-powered weather radio needs no installation or contract. Editor's note Check on the NWS county listing that a transmitter serves your address, then confirm the radio actually receives it where you plan to keep it. The sources we read do not discuss reception problems in buildings.
  • Households with deaf, hard-of-hearing, blind or low-vision members. FEMA lists text-display weather radios with flashing alerts, extra hearing-aid batteries, Braille or large-print labels, and communication devices as part of a kit.{{cite:health-ready-disabilities}}
  • Rural and low-coverage areas. Weather Radio does not depend on a cell tower. Whether your county is within range of a transmitter is something you can check using the NWS station and county listings.{{cite:health-nws-nwr-national}}
  • Families with children. Children receive WEAs on their own devices. FEMA has a children's fact sheet on WEAs and suggests parents talk with children about them.{{cite:health-ready-alerts}}

What this guide has and hasn't done

Every statement here comes from the FEMA, FCC and National Weather Service pages cited, read in full on the date shown. We have not tested any radio, app or phone. We did not read the FCC's WEA-capable devices guide, FEMA's alerting-people-with-disabilities page, or the FCC's text-to-911 information, and we do not summarize them; ask your local 911 center whether text to 911 is available where you live.

We have not defined the different kinds of weather watch and warning. Your local National Weather Service office is the source for that, and its terms matter more than ours. We have no sourced guidance on offline map apps, on radio brand quality or on amateur (ham) radio, and we say nothing about them. The NWS page we cited changes constantly, since it lists live transmitter outages, so its details were true on the day we read it and may not be true today.
