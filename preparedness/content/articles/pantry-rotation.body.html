What to do now

  1. Start from what your household already eats. Ready.gov's advice is to store at least a several-day supply of non-perishable food, to choose foods your family will eat, and to remember special dietary needs.{{cite:food-ready-food}} Oregon State Extension adds the reason: food you actually like is food you can cycle through.{{cite:food-osu-survival-food}}
  2. Add a little each time you shop instead of buying a stockpile at once. Buy small cans so there are no leftovers that need refrigeration, and keep a manual can opener with the food.{{cite:fsis-emergency-foods}}{{cite:food-osu-survival-food}}
  3. Label what you buy with the month and year, shelve it behind what is already there, and use the oldest first.{{cite:food-usu-canned-goods}}{{cite:food-illinois-room-temp}}
  4. Keep it cool and dry, away from the stove, pipes and furnace, and keep metal cans off the floor. FSIS says temperatures below 85°F are best and that you should check your pantry every few weeks.{{cite:food-fsis-shelf-stable}}{{cite:food-usu-canned-goods}}
  5. Throw out cans that are bulging, leaking, or deeply dented (a dent you can lay a finger into), and heavily rusted cans. Do not taste food from a can that spurts when you open it.{{cite:food-fsis-shelf-stable}}

This guide is about the shelf-stable part of an emergency food supply. For the refrigerator and freezer during an outage, see {{link:food-safety-power-outage|food safety during a power outage}}. For water, see {{link:water-math|water math}} and {{link:two-week-water-storage|two-week water storage}}. If someone is ill after eating, call a clinician or your emergency number.

The idea: a pantry you turn over, not a pantry you bury

The usual picture of an emergency food supply is a shelf of unfamiliar cans in the back of a closet, bought in one panicked weekend and discovered, years later, to be expired, dented, or something nobody wants to eat. The better picture is dull and works: a pantry that you shop from and refill. You eat the oldest item, you replace it, and the stock turns over.

This is the "first in, first out" method that Utah State and Illinois Extension both recommend for canned and packaged food.{{cite:food-usu-canned-goods}}{{cite:food-illinois-room-temp}} Oregon State Extension gives the logic: store foods your family will eat, so that you can regularly cycle through the supply and keep it fresh.{{cite:food-osu-survival-food}} A supply that is part of ordinary meals costs less in waste, takes less thought, and is more likely to be there when you need it.

Editor's note That is also the argument for buying ordinary groceries rather than specialty emergency products. Nothing in the official pages we read says a household needs special "survival" food. FSIS and Ready.gov both list ordinary canned, dried and packaged groceries.{{cite:fsis-emergency-foods}}{{cite:food-ready-food}}

What the official lists contain

FSIS's list of foods to keep for emergencies is short: dried fruit; canned fruit or vegetables; shelf-stable cans of meat, poultry and fish; jars of peanut butter and jelly; small packages of cereal, granola bars and crackers; nonfat dry milk; and small boxes of juice drinks. It adds that you should include infant formula, pet food and foods for family members with special dietary needs, have a manual can opener, and periodically use and refresh your supply.{{cite:fsis-emergency-foods}}

Ready.gov's list is similar: ready-to-eat canned meats, fruits and vegetables with a can opener; protein or fruit bars; dry cereal or granola; peanut butter; dried fruit; canned juices; non-perishable pasteurized milk; high-energy foods; food for infants; and comfort or stress foods.{{cite:food-ready-food}} Oregon State's publication organizes similar ideas by food group, with a menu-planning approach built on the USDA's MyPlate, and suggests packing your first three days of meals into labeled bags so no one raids them.{{cite:food-osu-survival-food}}

The official lists share three features. The foods need no refrigeration. They need little or no cooking and little or no water. And they are foods people will eat when they are stressed. Oregon State warns that dehydrated and freeze-dried foods may need extra water and fuel, and that freeze-dried food, though it may advertise a 20- to 30-year storage life, does not ensure quality.{{cite:food-osu-survival-food}}

How much

The official sources are more cautious about the amount than most marketing is. Ready.gov says "at least a several-day supply".{{cite:food-ready-food}} FSIS names a 3-day supply of commercially bottled water per person, a minimum of 3 gallons, alongside the food.{{cite:fsis-emergency-foods}} Oregon State's publication, written for people at risk of a major Pacific Northwest earthquake, says the state's minimum goal is two weeks of meals and that many local emergency managers recommend four weeks, "to ensure that you have enough food and water until community needs are assessed".{{cite:food-osu-survival-food}} Those two-week and four-week figures are for that region and that hazard. They are not a national standard, and we have not found an official national figure beyond "several days".

Editor's note A practical way to start is arithmetic you can do on the back of a receipt. Decide how many days (start with three), count the people, and write out three meals and a snack per person per day from foods you already like. Then count what you already have. Most households find they already hold part of a three-day supply. We do not give a calorie target because none of the sources we read supplies one in a form we can quote without inventing it; the Oregon State publication points to MyPlate planning sheets for that.

Food that needs water or fuel

Count the water and cooking fuel a food needs in the same plan as the food. Pasta, rice, dried beans and freeze-dried meals all need water to prepare, and most need heat. FSIS and Ready.gov steer you to foods that do not.{{cite:fsis-emergency-foods}}{{cite:food-ready-food}} The water you store for drinking is separate arithmetic: see {{link:water-math|water math}}.

What the dates on the package mean

Most of the waste in a home pantry comes from misreading dates, so it is worth being exact. FSIS says that, apart from infant formula, product dating is not required by federal law and that dates indicate quality, not safety.{{cite:food-fsis-dating}}

Common date labels as FSIS and Illinois Extension describe them

"Best if Used By / Before"When the product will be at its best flavor or quality.{{cite:food-fsis-dating}}No
"Sell-By"Tells the store how long to display the product.{{cite:food-fsis-dating}}No
"Use-By"The last date recommended for use at peak quality.{{cite:food-fsis-dating}}No, except on infant formula
"Freeze-By"When to freeze to maintain peak quality.{{cite:food-fsis-dating}}No

Two things in that table deserve emphasis. First, infant formula is the exception: federal regulations require a "Use-By" date on it, and FSIS says not to buy or use formula after that date.{{cite:food-fsis-dating}} Illinois Extension lists infant formula and baby food among products whose expiration dates should be respected.{{cite:food-illinois-room-temp}} We give no advice on the dates on medicines; ask your pharmacist. Second, a can can carry both a closed code and an open calendar date. FSIS says the open date is usually a "Best if Used By" date for peak quality, and that high-acid canned foods such as tomatoes and fruits keep their best quality for 12 to 18 months, while low-acid canned foods such as meats and vegetables keep for two to five years.{{cite:food-fsis-dating}}

That is a statement about quality. Utah State Extension makes a slightly different one, about safety: for emergency storage, commercially canned foods in metal or glass remain safe to consume as long as the seal has not been broken, though that is "not to say the quality will be retained for that long".{{cite:food-usu-canned-goods}} The two statements are compatible, but they are not the same, and it is easy to quote one as if it were the other.

One FSIS row we do not repeat

The same chart says home-canned foods keep 12 months and should be boiled "10 minutes for high-acid foods; 20 minutes for low-acid foods" before use. The text of the same FSIS page says something different: low-acid and tomato foods should be boiled 10 minutes, plus 1 minute for each 1,000 feet of elevation, and "properly home-canned fruits have never needed the 10-minute boil".{{cite:food-fsis-shelf-stable}} The CDC page agrees with the text, not the chart.{{cite:food-cdc-home-canned}} We therefore do not quote the chart's home-canned row. Home-canned food is covered in {{link:pressure-canning-low-acid|our pressure-canning guide}}, with this disagreement stated there.

Where to keep it

Every storage source says some version of "cool, dry, dark". The details differ.

  • FSIS: store canned and other shelf-stable foods in a cool, dry place, never above or beside the stove, under the sink, in a damp garage or basement, or anywhere with temperature extremes. Temperatures below 85°F are best, and high temperatures (over 100°F) are harmful to canned goods.{{cite:food-fsis-shelf-stable}}
  • Utah State Extension: cool, dark, dry space away from furnaces, pipes and places where temperatures swing, such as uninsulated attics; do not let sealed cans or jars freeze; keep metal cans off the floor, especially bare concrete, because moisture can wick up and cause rust.{{cite:food-usu-canned-goods}}
  • Illinois Extension: keep the storage area dry; keep food off the ground; keep food and cleaning supplies apart; cover opened food or put it in a lidded container to keep pests out.{{cite:food-illinois-room-temp}}
  • Oregon State Extension: storage in a cool, dry, dark location is best, but in an earthquake-prone area the best place may be one you can reach if the house is damaged; in a small home, closets, under beds or behind furniture can work. It says canned foods can become dangerous when stored above 95°F.{{cite:food-osu-survival-food}}

The temperature advice differs in what it is trying to protect. FSIS's 85°F is a quality and shelf-life target. CDC's and Oregon State's 95°F is a safety line for home-canned food, with CDC adding that 50°F to 70°F is best for quality.{{cite:food-cdc-home-canned}}{{cite:food-osu-survival-food}} Editor's note For an apartment with no cool basement, the practical reading is the same in all of them: choose the coolest interior spot you have, away from the oven, the dishwasher exhaust, a sunny window and the water heater. A closet on an interior wall often beats a kitchen cabinet over the stove.

Cans: what to keep and what to discard

FSIS is specific. Discard heavily rusted cans, which may have tiny holes. Surface rust you can rub off with a finger or paper towel is not serious. If you open a can and there is rust inside, do not eat the food. A small dent in an otherwise sound can is acceptable, but discard any can with a deep dent, one you can lay your finger into, and any can with a dent on a seam. Do not use swollen cans.{{cite:food-fsis-shelf-stable}} Ready.gov and Illinois Extension say the same about swollen, dented or corroded cans.{{cite:food-ready-food}}{{cite:food-illinois-room-temp}}

The reason for caution is botulism. FSIS describes a rare but serious danger in incorrectly processed canned foods: the toxin-producing bacterium grows where there is no oxygen, symptoms appear within 12 to 48 hours and include double vision, droopy eyelids, trouble swallowing and difficulty breathing, and without treatment a patient can die of suffocation. FSIS's advice is not to taste food from containers that are leaking, bulging, badly dented, that have cracked jars or loose lids, foul odors, milky liquids, or that spurt when opened.{{cite:food-fsis-shelf-stable}} Botulism is a medical emergency: call your emergency number.

If a can freezes

FSIS: do not use a swollen can, and discard frozen cans that are not swollen if they have been allowed to thaw at 40°F or higher. A frozen can that has not thawed can be defrosted in the refrigerator, and used if the contents look and smell normal.{{cite:food-fsis-shelf-stable}} Utah State Extension is more blunt about prevention: do not allow sealed cans or jars to freeze, because freezing can lead to rust, bursting cans and broken seals that let in harmful bacteria.{{cite:food-usu-canned-goods}} Editor's note If your only storage space is an unheated garage in a cold climate, that is a good reason to move the food indoors, even to a closet.

Without waste

Rotating is how you avoid waste, and the sources give a few specifics.

  • Eat before it matters. If a quality date is near, plan a meal around it. FSIS says foods not showing signs of spoilage are wholesome past a "Best if Used By" date if handled properly.{{cite:food-fsis-dating}}
  • Donate food you will not use. FSIS says food can be donated after the date passes, as long as it is evaluated for quality and shows no noticeable change in wholesomeness before distribution.{{cite:food-fsis-dating}}
  • Small cans. Choosing small cans means no leftovers that need refrigeration, in an outage or otherwise.{{cite:fsis-emergency-foods}}
  • Opened cans. FSIS says the unused portions can be kept refrigerated in the can, but to preserve quality, move them to a glass or plastic container.{{cite:food-fsis-shelf-stable}}

Your household is not a textbook

  • Tight budget. Add one or two items per shop and watch for sales; Oregon State advises spreading the cost over several weeks or months rather than buying everything at once, and notes that seasonally available foods are often of better quality and cost less to preserve at home.{{cite:food-osu-survival-food}} Utah State advises against budget resellers (scratch-and-dent sales, dollar stores) for storage cans because of the risk of damaged cans, while noting that commercially canned foods are superior to home-canned food for storage.{{cite:food-usu-canned-goods}} Editor's note That last advice is one place where the "cheapest" option may not be the best, so inspect cans from any discount source with extra care.
  • Renters and small spaces. Oregon State suggests bedroom or coat closets, under beds and behind furniture.{{cite:food-osu-survival-food}} Keep to the cool, dry rules above.
  • Limited strength, hands or mobility. Pop-top cans, small cans and soft packages are easier to open and lift than large cans. Oregon State warns the seams of pop-top cans are fragile and may not survive heavy shaking.{{cite:food-osu-survival-food}} Editor's note Which compromise suits you depends on your hands and your hazard. We have no official source that ranks packaging for accessibility.
  • Special diets, allergies and infants. Include infant formula, pet food and special-diet foods in the plan, as FSIS says.{{cite:fsis-emergency-foods}} Oregon State notes many canned, boxed and dried foods contain high amounts of sodium and suggests accounting for that for anyone who needs to limit it.{{cite:food-osu-survival-food}} Medical dietary needs are for your clinician to decide.
  • Pets. FSIS lists pet food. The sources we read do not give a quantity for pets.{{cite:fsis-emergency-foods}}

Home-canned and home-dried food on the shelf

If part of your stock is food you preserved yourself, handle it separately. Utah State Extension's general rule is that unopened home-canned food has a one-year shelf life and should be used before two years.{{cite:food-usu-canned-goods}} CDC advises canning only the amount of food you will use in one year, and not storing it above 95°F.{{cite:food-cdc-home-canned}} Beyond that, the safety of home-canned low-acid food depends on how it was processed, and no inspection of the jar can fully tell you that. See {{link:pressure-canning-low-acid|pressure canning low-acid foods}}. Home-dried jerky has a much shorter life than the commercial kind; see {{link:safe-jerky-dehydration|safe jerky and dehydration}}.

What this guide has and hasn't done

Every instruction and figure here comes from the FSIS, Ready.gov and FoodSafety.gov pages and the Extension publications cited, each read in full on the date shown. Where they differ we say so. We have not tested any food, and we do not claim to know how long a particular product will last on your shelf; the figures are the agencies' ranges.

We did not read individual entries of the FoodKeeper database, we did not read the Illinois Extension emergency-food-supply PDF or the Utah State food-storage booklet, and we did not find an official national figure for how many days or weeks of food a household should keep beyond Ready.gov's "several days". We give no calorie, protein or portion targets because we could not source them without inventing numbers. The safety review for this guide is recorded in the box at the bottom of the page. It does not replace an editor's read-through or the launch checks, and the page stays a draft until those are done.
