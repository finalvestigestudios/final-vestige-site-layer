Read this first

This is a history, not a packing list. The quantities below are what guidebook authors recommended to emigrants in the 1840s and 1850s. They are not guidance for any journey today, and they were not enough for many of the people who followed them.

  • The lesson that carries over is about margin and resupply, not about bacon. The records show people running short of food far from help, and the sources say so plainly.
  • Home-cured, smoked, preserved and fermented foods have been linked to botulism cases. You cannot see, smell or taste the toxin.{{cite:fsis-home-cure-qa}}{{cite:cdc-botulism-prevention}}
  • For food you will carry, use commercially packaged, labelled products. If you want to dry or cure meat yourself, use a procedure published by USDA or the National Center for Home Food Preservation and follow it exactly.{{cite:nchfp-cure-smoke}}

If you are traveling in remote country, tell someone your route and when to expect you, and carry enough food and water for the delay you cannot predict. In an emergency, call your emergency number. Symptoms of botulism can include blurred vision, trouble swallowing or speaking, and weakness; that is a medical emergency.

What we read, and what we did not

The best evidence on what overland emigrants ate comes from two kinds of document: the diaries they wrote on the trail, and the guidebooks written for people about to go. The Library of Congress holds a collection of 49 diaries, 43 maps and seven published guides for emigrants, covering Mormon and other pioneers moving to Utah, Montana and the Pacific between 1847 and 1869.{{cite:loc-pioneer-diaries}}

We have to be straightforward about the limit of this piece. We read that collection's catalogue description, not the individual diaries. The diary quotations on this page are the ones the National Park Service reproduces in its own interpretive writing, and we say so each time. The guidebook we did read in full for its provisions chapter is Randolph B. Marcy's The Prairie Traveler, which the Library of Congress has digitized.{{cite:loc-prairie-traveler}} The park service's page on trail supplies relies on a different guide, Joseph Ware's Emigrant's Guide of 1849.{{cite:hist-food-nps-emigrant-trails}}

What the guidebooks said to carry

The National Park Service reports that Ware's 1849 guide, published in St. Louis, suggested the following food for three people headed west on the Oregon Trail: 1,080 pounds of flour, 600 of bacon, 100 of coffee, 5 of tea, 150 of sugar, 75 of rice, 50 of dried fruit, 50 of salt and pepper, 10 of saleratus (baking soda) and 50 of lard, along with corn meal, hard-tack, dried beef, molasses, vinegar, eggs and beans.{{cite:hist-food-nps-emigrant-trails}} The same page notes that in 1850 it cost roughly $800 to $1,200 to obtain a proper outfit and get by for a whole year without harvesting a crop.{{cite:hist-food-nps-emigrant-trails}}

Marcy's allowance is different in kind. Writing for travelers crossing from the Missouri River to California, he recommends, for each grown person, enough to last 110 days: 150 pounds of flour "or its equivalent in hard bread", 25 pounds of bacon or pork, enough fresh beef driven on the hoof to make up the meat portion, 15 pounds of coffee and 25 pounds of sugar, plus saleratus or yeast powder, salt and pepper.{{cite:hist-food-marcy-prairie-traveler-text}}

Dividing Ware's flour by three gives 360 pounds each, more than twice Marcy's figure, and his bacon works out to 200 pounds each against Marcy's 25.Editor's note We would not conclude from this that one author was wrong. The park page sets Ware's list inside the cost of an outfit to get by for a whole year, while Marcy's is for the roughly 110-day crossing, and Marcy relies on cattle driven alongside. The sources we read do not say how the two lists were meant to relate, so we do not reconcile them, and we would be cautious about treating either as a measure of what people actually carried or ate.

What people ate on the road

According to the Park Service, emigrants supplied themselves with flour, beans, bacon, potatoes, biscuits, dried fruit, jerky and coffee, and ate venison, buffalo, beef and wild birds when hunting succeeded. Leftover meat made stew the next day; fat drippings mixed with flour made gravy. A family with a milk cow could churn butter in the swaying wagon, and where there was no wood on the plains, fires were fed with "buffalo chips". The page adds that "in desperate times" pioneers cooked snakes or prairie dogs.{{cite:hist-food-nps-emigrant-trails}}

Marcy's handbook shows what an experienced army officer thought a traveler should do about the parts of the diet that fresh food would otherwise supply. He recommends desiccated vegetables pressed into a hard cake, which he calls "the best preparation for prairie traveling that has yet been discovered", and says he believes their antiscorbutic properties are not impaired by drying. For travelers who cannot carry vegetables, he suggests citric acid, wild onions, wild grapes and greens.{{cite:hist-food-marcy-prairie-traveler-text}} The reason was the same one that shaped the ship's diets described in our {{link:salt-beef-salt-pork-age-of-sail|piece on salt provisions at sea}}: a diet of flour, bacon and coffee lacks what fresh produce provides, and scurvy was a recognized danger.

He also describes "cold flour", made by parching corn and pounding it, as the most portable and simple preparation of subsistence he knew and as one used extensively "by the Mexicans and Indians".{{cite:hist-food-marcy-prairie-traveler-text}} He does not name any people or nation. We repeat that vagueness rather than invent a specific attribution; a more precise account would need a source that names the peoples and speaks for them.

For meat that had to keep, Marcy writes that fresh meat can be "jerked" on the plains by hanging thin strips to dry in the sun. He says no salt is used.{{cite:hist-food-marcy-prairie-traveler-text}} We do not reproduce his dimensions or drying times, and the safety verdict below explains why modern guidance differs. Marcy also describes pemmican as the near-exclusive diet of the Fur Company's men in the Northwest; the fuller story of that food, with the peoples who made it named, is in {{link:pemmican-and-dried-meats|our piece on pemmican and dried meats}}.

When the food ran short

The same guides that tell people what to bring also record what happened when it was not enough. Marcy himself describes a winter crossing of the Rocky Mountains in 1857-58 in which his party's supplies were entirely consumed eighteen days before the first settlements in New Mexico, and the men lived on starved mules and horses at "five to six pounds of this meat per man daily", growing weak and thin while craving fat meat.{{cite:hist-food-marcy-prairie-traveler-text}} He adds that "heretofore many of the California emigrants have improvidently exhausted their stocks of provisions before reaching their journey's end" and had to pay exorbitant prices to make up the shortfall.{{cite:hist-food-marcy-prairie-traveler-text}}

The Park Service's interpretive guide for the trails across Nevada tells several such stories. The Bidwell-Bartleson party, the first covered-wagon emigrants to head overland for California, were in mid-September 1841 lost in the desert near today's Utah-Nevada border with their provisions gone; diarist Dawson noted grimly that "there was one thing we had no trouble to pack—our provisions", because they had none. They had begun butchering their weakest oxen, and the guide notes that the ox-drovers owned the food and the oxen were the food.{{cite:hist-food-nps-nht-nevada-guide}} By the time emigrants reached the Humboldt River in the main years of travel, the guide says, they had often thrown away tools, stoves and even bedding to spare their animals. But "nobody threw away bacon, flour, or beans", because many parties were running dangerously low on provisions.{{cite:hist-food-nps-nht-nevada-guide}} The guide says the Donner-Reed party of 1846 realized, before reaching the Humboldt, that it did not have food enough to last to its destination.{{cite:hist-food-nps-nht-nevada-guide}} After 1847 the Hastings Cutoff at least passed through Salt Lake City, where travelers could buy provisions.{{cite:hist-food-nps-nht-nevada-guide}}

Editor's note What these records share is that a plan built around carrying a fixed stock, with no reliable resupply and no margin for slow animals or bad weather, fails slowly and then quickly. The failures are not about any one food. They are about arithmetic, distance and chance.

Whose hunger?

The same NPS guide is candid that emigrant hunger and Indigenous hunger met on the Humboldt. It reports that emigrants routinely encountered Western Shoshone people, whom it calls Newe, and, in the passage as we extracted it, that they gave advice, answered questions about the country ahead and sometimes offered their own food to starving pioneers. It also says the emigrants' livestock ate Newe seed grasses, trampled their wild crops and fouled their water, that emigrants cut Newe pine nut groves and competed for scarce game, and that from 1849 on native resources were quickly depleted.{{cite:hist-food-nps-nht-nevada-guide}}

The guide adds that Newe and Numa (Paiute) people drove off or killed emigrants' oxen and mules, and says "the ultimate cause of trouble between native people and emigrants crossing the Great Basin, though, was hunger, not greed", with one full belly meaning another might go empty. It records an 1855 report from a Utah territorial Indian agent that local people said emigrants had eaten up their grass, deprived them of seed, and scared off game with their long guns. And it describes retaliation by outraged emigrants that fed a cycle of violence.{{cite:hist-food-nps-nht-nevada-guide}} It also relays a Shoshone account, passed down across generations, of how the Newe tried traditional rituals against the arrival of the emigrants.{{cite:hist-food-nps-nht-nevada-guide}}

These are the Park Service's words describing an oral tradition and a government agent's letter, not a Newe or Numa author's own account. We have not found one that we could read for this piece, and we mark that as a gap. What the passage does establish is that a "ration" is only half the story: the food an overland party consumed on the way was sometimes food that the people already living there were counting on.

Current safety verdict

The planning lesson holds; the home-preserved foods are not validated

Plan for delay and resupply, and use modern, inspected food for the stock you carry. We do not endorse home-cured bacon, home-dried jerky made the 1859 way, or any other traditional trail food as a safe way to store food.

  • USDA's current recommendation for home jerky is to heat meat to 160°F and poultry to 165°F before dehydrating, because dried meat's bacteria become much more heat resistant, and a dehydrator may not reach those temperatures.{{cite:hist-food-fsis-jerky}} Marcy describes jerking in the sun with no heat step and no salt.{{cite:hist-food-marcy-prairie-traveler-text}}
  • The two federal and extension sources we read disagree in detail on timing: the National Center for Home Food Preservation lists heating in marinade before drying or in an oven after drying, while USDA recommends the heat before dehydrating. Both give 160°F for meat.{{cite:hist-food-nchfp-jerky}}{{cite:hist-food-fsis-jerky}} For a gated safety question we link to both rather than choosing, and flag it for the human reviewer.
  • The CDC lists improperly preserved foods among the most common sources of botulism and says to throw out food you cannot vouch for.{{cite:cdc-botulism-prevention}}

Nothing on this page gives a method. Our sources for the modern guidance are the agencies' own pages; check them for the current version.

Then: the outfit

Documented history Sacks of flour and bacon, coffee, sugar, saleratus and hard bread bought before departure, supplemented by hunting, herd animals, and a few antiscorbutics, with no reliable resupply for much of the route. When supplies ran out, people ate their draft animals.{{cite:hist-food-marcy-prairie-traveler-text}}{{cite:hist-food-nps-nht-nevada-guide}}

Now: margin and inspection

Official guidance Choose commercially packaged foods, carry more than you expect to need, tell someone your plan, and follow USDA or NCHFP methods for anything you preserve yourself.{{cite:nchfp-cure-smoke}}{{cite:hist-food-fsis-jerky}} See also our pieces on {{link:ships-biscuit-and-hardtack|ship's biscuit and hardtack}} and {{link:history-of-food-preservation|the history of food preservation}}.

What we have not done

We did not read individual emigrant diaries; the Library of Congress collection is cited at catalogue level, and diary quotations come from the Park Service's reproduction of them. We could not trace Ware's guide to a copy, so his figures are as the Park Service reports them. Marcy's text was read in an Internet Archive transcription of the Library of Congress's scan, and the Library's own page-level text view would not load. The LOC copy is the 1861 printing of a book first published in 1859.

We found no source in the voices of the Western Shoshone (Newe) or Paiute (Numa) peoples. The account of their experience here is the National Park Service's. We have no image of an outfit, a wagon or a ration that we have the rights to publish, so this page has none. The page remains a draft until a human reviewer has read the verdict panel.
