Read this first

This is a history, not a recipe. Nothing on this page is a brine, a ratio, a time or a storage instruction, and it should not be used as one. Old recipes are part of the record of what people ate. They are not tested food-safety procedures.

  • If you want to pickle or ferment at home, use a recipe that has been tested, and follow it exactly. The National Center for Home Food Preservation (NCHFP), a University of Georgia program supported by USDA, publishes them.{{cite:nchfp-home}}
  • Improperly canned, preserved or fermented homemade foods are the common sources of foodborne botulism. You cannot see, smell or taste the toxin.{{cite:hist-home-cdc-botulism-about}}{{cite:cdc-botulism-prevention}}
  • If you do not know whether a home-preserved food was made safely, do not eat it and do not taste it to check. The CDC's advice is: if in doubt, throw it out.{{cite:hist-home-cdc-botulism-home-canned}}

Botulism is a medical emergency. The CDC lists difficulty breathing and muscle paralysis among its effects, and says to see a doctor or go to an emergency room immediately if you or your child have signs of it.{{cite:hist-home-cdc-botulism-about}} Call your emergency number.

  • Official guidance A current statement from a government health body or a USDA-supported extension program.

Two words that hide a lot

"Pickling" and "fermenting" are used loosely. The NCHFP separates them by ingredients and method. Regular dill pickles and sauerkraut are fermented: the vegetable sits in a salted brine while bacteria change it and acidity rises. Fresh-pack or quick-process pickles are not fermented; they are covered with vinegar and seasonings. Fruit pickles are made by heating fruit in a seasoned syrup acidified with lemon juice or vinegar, and relishes are chopped fruits and vegetables cooked with seasonings and vinegar.Official guidance{{cite:hist-home-nchfp-pickling}}

That distinction matters for safety, because the two families protect food in different ways. In a fermented product, salt favors the growth of desirable bacteria while inhibiting the growth of others. In a vinegar pickle, it is the acidity of the finished product that does the work. The NCHFP puts the second in the plainest terms available: the level of acidity in a pickled product is as important to its safety as it is to taste and texture.{{cite:hist-home-nchfp-pickling}}

Editor's note For a reader of old cookbooks, the lesson is that "pickles" is not one technology. A recipe has to be read for which kind it is before anyone can ask what is keeping it safe. That is already more than most old recipes say.

What the printed record shows

The Library of Congress holds a digitized copy of the first cook book written by an American and published in the United States, Amelia Simmons's American Cookery, printed in Hartford by Hudson and Goodwin in 1796. Its title page promises directions for "vegetables," "preserves" and the "best modes" of making many dishes, adapted to this country.Documented history{{cite:hist-home-loc-simmons-1796}} The Library says it is not aware of any U.S. copyright or other restrictions on the digitized documents in the collection.{{cite:hist-home-loc-simmons-1796}}

We read the catalogue record, not the pages inside. We therefore make no claim about what the book says on pickles, and we do not quote any of its directions. We cite it for something narrower and more useful: it is a primary document, readable by anyone, from a time when a printed recipe was the only guidance a household had, and it was written before any of the tested methods discussed below existed.Editor's note If you open it, read it as you would read a letter from an ancestor. It tells you what a cook of 1796 thought was worth writing down, not what a food scientist today would recommend.

A much later and more thoroughly documented moment is the home-front canning drive of World War II, which the National Park Service describes. Preservation took several forms, including drying, freezing, pickling and canning, and extensive resources were available from the government, local agricultural extension services and businesses. Some advice was cautionary: sources discouraged canning vegetable mixes as too dangerous. The same account explains the stakes: if bacteria were not properly eliminated in airtight containers, botulism and other food poisoning was possible, and in general only acidic foods like tomatoes and fruits could be canned in a hot-water bath, while other vegetables and meats needed pressure canners.Documented history{{cite:hist-home-nps-ww2-canning}}

The scale was large. The same page says that in 1943, 75% of American homemakers put up 4.1 billion containers of food, and that by the end of the war there were more than 3,800 community canneries, where knowledgeable staff or volunteers were present to help ensure safety.{{cite:hist-home-nps-ww2-canning}}

Editor's note The wartime picture is instructive for what it shows about the system, not any single recipe. The way mass home preservation was made reasonably safe was by pairing recipes with instruction, equipment and people who knew what they were checking. A recipe by itself is the weakest part of that system. A lone recipe from a 1796 book has none of the rest.

Why an old recipe is not a procedure

The NCHFP's pickling guidance supplies three rules. Do not alter the proportions of vinegar, food or water in a recipe, or use a vinegar of unknown acidity. Use only recipes with tested proportions of ingredients. And there must be a minimum, uniform level of acid throughout the mixed product to prevent the growth of botulinum bacteria.Official guidance{{cite:hist-home-nchfp-pickling}} Look at what each rule says about the older printed record.

"Tested proportions." An early recipe is a record of what one cook did. Whether any given quantity had been tested for safety is exactly what the old text cannot tell you. The NCHFP page we read is adapted from a USDA guide to home canning revised in 2015 and was reviewed in February 2018; the older book we cite predates any comparable testing program that we know of, but we did not research when such testing began, so we do not claim a date for it.{{cite:hist-home-nchfp-pickling}}Uncertain

"Uniform" acidity. The NCHFP says the minimum acid level must be uniform throughout the mixed product. A recipe that gave a flavor but not a measurable acid level, or that depended on the strength of a vinegar nobody could check, cannot satisfy that rule. We cannot say what any particular old recipe did, since we did not read one, but the test is the right one to apply.Editor's note

Salt is not just seasoning. In fermented sauerkraut and brined pickles, the NCHFP says, salt provides characteristic flavor and is "vital to safety and texture," and warns not to attempt fermented pickles or sauerkraut by cutting back on the salt required.{{cite:hist-home-nchfp-pickling}} The same page notes that flake salt varies in density, which is one reason it is not recommended for making pickled and fermented foods, and that pickling or canning salt is preferred because anti-caking additives can cloud brine.{{cite:hist-home-nchfp-pickling}}

Editor's note Put those two statements side by side and a hidden problem for historians appears. Any old directions that measure salt by the spoon or the handful are measuring something whose weight depends on which salt was used. That does not make the old cook careless; it makes the instruction impossible to reproduce reliably. This is our inference from the NCHFP's remark about density, not a claim about a specific book.

The reason this is gated

The hazard is botulism. The CDC calls it a rare but serious illness, caused by a toxin that attacks the body's nerves and causes difficulty breathing, muscle paralysis and even death, and says that common sources of foodborne botulism are homemade foods that have been improperly canned, preserved or fermented. It adds that store-bought foods can also be contaminated, but that is uncommon.Official guidance{{cite:hist-home-cdc-botulism-about}}

The USDA's food-safety service describes the same pattern from the meat side: many botulism cases have followed home-canned, preserved, cured, smoked or fermented foods contaminated with toxin.{{cite:fsis-home-cure-qa}} Its botulism page also lists traditionally prepared salted or fermented fish among foods linked to recent illness.{{cite:fsis-botulism}} The source does not name which communities prepare those foods or how, and we do not guess. We make no generalization about any people's traditional foods, and we say plainly that this is the USDA's statement, not ours.

What makes it dangerous is that you cannot check. The CDC says you cannot see, smell or taste the toxin.{{cite:cdc-botulism-prevention}} Its page on home-canned foods lists signs that a container may be contaminated: leaking, bulging or swollen containers, damage or cracks, spurting liquid or foam when opened, and food that is discolored, moldy or smells bad. It adds that a food can be unsafe without any of those signs, so a person who does not know whether safe canning guidelines were followed should discard it. Never taste it to see.{{cite:hist-home-cdc-botulism-home-canned}}

The same CDC page lists certain home-preserved foods, including low-acid home-canned foods and home-fermented foods, that it says to boil before eating, and gives times for doing so. We do not reproduce them. They are that agency's to keep current, and they belong to a safety procedure that should be read in full on the page itself rather than quoted from a history article.{{cite:hist-home-cdc-botulism-home-canned}}

Fermenting is not forbidden

It would be easy to leave this page with the idea that all home pickling and fermenting is dangerous, and that would be a mistake. The NCHFP, the program the USDA's food-safety service points consumers toward,{{cite:fsis-home-cure-qa}} publishes tested guidance for fermented and pickled foods, and the page we read covers sauerkraut, brined pickles, fresh-pack pickles, fruit pickles and relishes.{{cite:hist-home-nchfp-pickling}} It describes boiling-water canner processing as preventing spoilage by microorganisms, including yeasts and molds, and by enzymes, and says that processing times and procedures vary with the acidity of the food and the size of the pieces.{{cite:hist-home-nchfp-pickling}}

The NCHFP is also candid about the limits of its own advice on this page: it says the salt reduction in fresh-pack pickles can be done safely with tested recipes, while the same cannot be said of fermented ones.{{cite:hist-home-nchfp-pickling}} That is a narrow, sourced rule, and it is the opposite of a blanket warning. The safety line runs between tested and untested, not between old and new.

Editor's note A fair reading of the evidence is that tradition and safety are not enemies. Many traditional foods are made in ways that work. The difficulty is that a household cannot tell, from the food or the old recipe, which tradition was working and which was lucky. A tested recipe replaces luck with a published check.

Current safety verdict

Not validated for modern household use

Do not use an old recipe as your method. This verdict applies to historical recipes and to reproducing them by feel. It does not apply to tested modern procedures.

  • Use only recipes with tested proportions; do not alter vinegar, food or water proportions; and do not cut back the salt in a fermented pickle.{{cite:hist-home-nchfp-pickling}}
  • Home-preserved foods are a common source of botulism, and you cannot detect the toxin by sight, smell or taste.{{cite:hist-home-cdc-botulism-about}}{{cite:cdc-botulism-prevention}}
  • If you do not know whether a home-preserved food was made safely, throw it out.{{cite:hist-home-cdc-botulism-home-canned}}
  • For tested methods, start at the NCHFP or your local Cooperative Extension office.{{cite:nchfp-home}}

We found no source that evaluates any specific historical pickling or fermenting recipe for modern safety. That is a gap in what we read, not evidence that old recipes are safe.

Then: the printed recipe and the household

Documented history A cook relied on a printed directions book, local custom and experience. By the 1940s, governments and extension services were publishing preservation instruction, discouraging risky combinations, and running community canneries with trained staff.{{cite:hist-home-loc-simmons-1796}}{{cite:hist-home-nps-ww2-canning}}

Now: tested, published and updated

Official guidance A USDA-supported program publishes tested directions for pickled and fermented foods, based on a USDA guide revised in 2015, and says to use only those with tested proportions. The CDC says to discard home-preserved food of unknown provenance.{{cite:hist-home-nchfp-pickling}}{{cite:hist-home-cdc-botulism-home-canned}}

What we have not done

We did not read the text of American Cookery, only its library catalogue entry, so this page makes no claim about any recipe in it. We read one NCHFP page on pickling, whose text is shared with its fermenting page, and not the complete USDA guide it is adapted from. We did not read NCHFP guidance on specific foods, or on kombucha, infused oils or other fermented products, and this page says nothing about them.

We did not research when household preservation testing began in the United States, or how any particular acid level was first measured. We do not have a primary source on Indigenous fermentation or preservation traditions and make no claims about them; the USDA's reference to traditionally prepared fish is quoted as the agency's statement only. The wartime canning page is a Park Service interpretive article and does not cite a single document for each claim. The human safety review for this article is recorded in the box at the bottom of the page. It does not replace an editor's read-through or the launch checks, and the page stays a draft until those are done.
