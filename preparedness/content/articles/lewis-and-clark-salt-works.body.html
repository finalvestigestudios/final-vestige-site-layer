Read this first

This is a history, not a method. It describes what a small party did in the winter of 1805-1806. It is not instructions for making salt, and it is not a safe way to produce food-grade salt or to preserve meat.

  • Boiling water does not remove salts or most chemicals. It leaves them behind, concentrated.{{cite:epa-emergency-disinfection}} Boiling seawater to make salt therefore keeps whatever else was dissolved or suspended in it.
  • Salt alone does not make meat safe from botulism. Home-cured, smoked, preserved and fermented foods have been linked to botulism cases, and you cannot see, smell or taste the toxin.{{cite:fsis-home-cure-qa}}{{cite:cdc-botulism-prevention}}
  • If you want to preserve meat at home, use a procedure published and tested by USDA or the National Center for Home Food Preservation, and follow it exactly.{{cite:nchfp-cure-smoke}}

If you or someone you are with has blurred vision, trouble swallowing or speaking, or growing weakness after eating a preserved food, treat it as a medical emergency and call your emergency number.

An expedition without salt

By the time the Corps of Discovery reached the Pacific coast in late 1805, it had run out of salt. The National Park Service describes the problem plainly: salt was crucial for preserving meat for the lean winter months, and it made food taste better. The party decided to winter near the ocean so that they could make salt by boiling seawater.Documented history{{cite:nps-salt-works}}

The journals show how the loss was felt. On 5 January 1806 Meriwether Lewis wrote that the party had had none since 20 December, and that he "felt a considerable inconvenience from the want of it". William Clark, by contrast, declared it "a mear matter of indifference" to him whether he had salt or not.{{cite:hist-food-lc-journal-1806-01-05}} The Park Service makes the same point, that Clark did not mind going without while Lewis found unsalted food bland.{{cite:nps-salt-works}} The captains' two voices are a good reminder that "necessity" was partly a matter of taste and habit, and partly a matter of meat.

This is a story with several parties in it: the expedition's members, the salt makers by name, and the Indigenous peoples on whose homeland the work was done. We try to name each carefully, and we say where the sources disagree.

The order, from the captains' own journals

On 28 December 1805, Clark recorded the detail. Joseph Field, William Bratton and George Gibson were to go to the ocean, form a camp and begin "makeing Salt" using five of the largest kettles, while Alexander Willard and Peter Weiser went along to help carry the kettles to the coast. The rest of the men stayed on picketing and gates for the fort.{{cite:hist-food-lc-journal-1805-12-28}}

The University of Nebraska-Lincoln edition of the journals adds an editors' note that the camp was at present Seaside, Oregon, ran until 21 February 1806, and produced about three or four bushels of salt, with usually about three men present although the personnel varied.{{cite:hist-food-lc-journal-1805-12-28}} The editors, rather than the captains, are the source of that total; Lewis and Clark kept no tally of the whole production that we found.

The five-day search for a site was not quick. When Willard and Weiser returned on 5 January, Lewis and Clark both wrote that it was not until the fifth day after leaving the fort that they found a convenient place, and that they had settled on the coast about 15 miles from the fort.{{cite:hist-food-lc-journal-1806-01-05}}

What the salt makers did

The Park Service's interpretive article lists what the work required: five kettles, wood, rocks, iron kegs and seawater. In its account the men piled rocks with room for firewood beneath and set the kettles of seawater on top, and when the water boiled away the salt remained. They worked around the clock and could make almost a gallon a day, which Lewis thought slow, calling it "a very tedious opperation" with only five kettles.{{cite:nps-lewis-clark-salt}}

That description is an interpretation as much as a record. The captains' entries do not describe the oven. The Lewis and Clark Trail Alliance's educational site says the "salt cairn" at the site is a reassembly done in the 1950s by local historians, a guess at the appearance of the original after it had collapsed into a pile of rocks by 1900.{{cite:hist-food-lc-salt-works-trail-alliance}} The replica the Park Service photographs at the site shows five metal kettles set on a rock structure with a hollowed area beneath.{{cite:nps-salt-works}} So the picture of how it looked is a reconstruction, even though the fact of the salt works is well documented.

The journals do record what the men did while they worked. Willard and Weiser reported on 5 January that the salt makers had built a "Comfortable Camp", killed an elk and several deer, and secured a good stock of meat, and that they could make "from 3 quarts to a gallon a day".{{cite:hist-food-lc-journal-1806-01-05}}

The people who lived there

The salt works stood near the homes of Indigenous people, and the sources identify them in slightly different ways. We set them out rather than flatten them.

  • The Park Service's article and place page say the camp was near a Clatsop-Nehalem village in present-day Seaside, Oregon.{{cite:nps-lewis-clark-salt}}{{cite:nps-salt-works}}
  • Lewis wrote that the camp was near "the lodge of some Killamuck families", using the captains' spelling for the Tillamook. Clark wrote that it was near the houses of "Clat Sop & Kil a mox" families.{{cite:hist-food-lc-journal-1806-01-05}}
  • The Park Service's page on the region's tribes says the Clatsop lived on the south side of the Columbia and down the coast to Tillamook Head, and that the Nehalem were the northernmost band of the Tillamook, living from Tillamook Head south to Kilchis Point.{{cite:hist-food-nps-lewi-tribes}}
  • The journal editors note that Clatsop people had winter villages at Seaside, moving there from summer villages around Point Adams.{{cite:hist-food-lc-journal-1805-12-28}}

These are consistent with each other: the neighbours were Clatsop and Nehalem Tillamook families, whom the sources name sometimes jointly and sometimes separately. The Park Service lists the Clatsop-Nehalem Confederated Tribes among the homeland tribes of this region today.{{cite:hist-food-nps-lewi-tribes}} They are a living community, not only a name in the journals; this page cannot speak for them.

The Park Service says the three salt makers befriended people in the village, who "had been verry kind and attentive to them", that some may have let them stay in wooden houses that gave better shelter than canvas, and that the villagers helped feed the salt makers by selling them whale blubber.{{cite:nps-salt-works}} The journals tell the same story from the fort's side. Lewis and Clark both wrote on 5 January that the Indians had given the salt makers a considerable quantity of blubber from a whale that had perished on the coast; Ordway wrote that the men bought some of the meat.{{cite:hist-food-lc-journal-1806-01-05}} The sources do not agree on whether it was a gift, a sale or both, and it may well have been both on different occasions.Uncertain

One more thing the record preserves comes from outside the expedition. The Trail Alliance's page quotes the 1955 plaque at the site, which says the location was fixed by a committee of the Oregon Historical Society in 1900 on the testimony of Jenny Michel of Seaside, whose Clatsop father remembered seeing the white men boiling water and pointed out the place to her when she was a young girl.{{cite:hist-food-lc-salt-works-trail-alliance}} The captains' journals say where the camp was only in general terms. The specific location of the site as we know it rests on Clatsop family memory.

What the salt was like

Willard and Weiser brought back a specimen of about a gallon on 5 January. Lewis found it "excellent, fine, strong, & white" and a "great treat". Clark found it "excellent white & fine" but "not So Strong" as rock salt or the salt made in Kentucky.{{cite:hist-food-lc-journal-1806-01-05}} Two other diarists recorded the amount differently: Sergeant Ordway and Private Whitehouse both wrote that the men came with about 2 gallons.{{cite:hist-food-lc-journal-1806-01-05}} The captains and the other diarists describe the same delivery in different numbers, and the source does not reconcile them.

The totals are similarly loose. Lewis's entry for 21 February, when the camp was evacuated, puts the stock of salt at about 20 gallons, 12 of them secured in "2 small iron bound kegs" and set aside for the voyage.{{cite:hist-food-lc-journal-1806-02-21}} The journal editors' footnote says the whole camp produced about three or four bushels, and the plaque quoted by the Trail Alliance says approximately four bushels.{{cite:hist-food-lc-journal-1805-12-28}}{{cite:hist-food-lc-salt-works-trail-alliance}} Gallons of stock on hand and bushels produced are different measures at different dates, and the sources do not explain how they relate. Some of the salt will have been used in the meantime, but none of the documents we read says how much.

What the sources do not tell us

Editor's note We found no source that records how much seawater the men boiled, what the water looked like, how much firewood they burned, or how the salt was stored and used to preserve meat. Another site gives a figure of 28 gallons of salt and about 1,400 gallons of seawater, but it does not say where those numbers come from, so we do not use them. We do not try to reconstruct a process from the numbers that exist.

The date discrepancy and why it matters

The 1955 plaque quoted by the Trail Alliance says the salt works was established on 2 January 1806 and that the men stayed until 20 February.{{cite:hist-food-lc-salt-works-trail-alliance}} The journals show that the five men left the fort on 28 December, that the first salt came back on 5 January, and that the sergeant brought the party home on 21 February.{{cite:hist-food-lc-journal-1805-12-28}}{{cite:hist-food-lc-journal-1806-02-21}} The plaque's dates are plausible for the time the camp was actually running, since the men had spent several days finding the site, but it is a monument's summary and the journals are the primary record.

The Park Service gives "mid-February" for the end of the work.{{cite:nps-lewis-clark-salt}} For a history this small, the discrepancy is trivial. We report it because the Archive's rule is that where sources differ, the page says so.

Current safety verdict

Not validated for modern household use

Do not make salt from seawater at home for food, and do not treat home salting as a preservation guarantee. We found no regulator or Extension source that endorses either, and the historical record is silent on contamination or illness.

  • EPA states that boiling does not remove salts, heavy metals and most chemicals.{{cite:epa-emergency-disinfection}} Editor's note Evaporating a pot of water leaves everything dissolved in it behind, which is why we would not assume that salt made from untested water is clean. The sources we read do not test any seawater.
  • The CDC lists improperly preserved foods among the most common sources of botulism and says the toxin cannot be seen, smelled or tasted.{{cite:cdc-botulism-prevention}}
  • USDA's food-safety service records botulism cases following home-canned, preserved, cured, smoked or fermented foods and points consumers to the National Center for Home Food Preservation.{{cite:fsis-home-cure-qa}}

The Corps came through the winter, but the journals were written to account for the expedition, not to count illnesses. Surviving is not the same as demonstrating that a method is safe.

Now: the inspected supply

Official guidance Salt is bought, not boiled. Households who want to preserve meat should use a method published and tested by the NCHFP or a Cooperative Extension service.{{cite:nchfp-cure-smoke}} See also our {{link:salt-beef-salt-pork-age-of-sail|piece on salt provisions at sea}} and our {{link:history-of-food-preservation|overview of food preservation}}, and for making water safer, the {{link:making-water-safer|water guide}}.

What we have not done

We read three journal entries from the University of Nebraska-Lincoln edition (28 December, 5 January and 21 February) and the editors' notes attached to them. We did not read the whole run of entries between those dates, including Clark's trip to the salt camp in early January, so we say little about it.

Our secondary sources are interpretive. The Park Service pages are short stories in a public-history series, and the Trail Alliance page is a public-service educational site; both give some figures, such as the five-day search or the Trail Alliance's 28-gallon total, that we could not trace to a journal entry. We found no account in the voices of the Clatsop or Nehalem Tillamook peoples themselves about the winter of 1805-1806; we read the Clatsop-Nehalem Confederated Tribes homepage, which holds no history of the event, and do not cite it. We have no image of the site that we have the rights to publish, so this page has none.
