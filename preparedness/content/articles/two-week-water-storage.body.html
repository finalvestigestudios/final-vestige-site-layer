What to do now

  1. Start with sealed, commercially bottled water. The CDC calls it the safest and most reliable source in an emergency. Keep it in its sealed original container, in a cool, dark place, and observe the expiration date on the label.{{cite:cdc-water-store}}{{cite:ready-water}}
  2. If you fill your own containers, use food-grade water-storage containers that have been washed with soap, rinsed, and sanitized with 1 teaspoon of unscented household bleach (5%-9% sodium hypochlorite) per quart of water. Cover, shake, wait at least 30 seconds, pour out, air-dry, then fill.{{cite:cdc-water-store}}
  3. Label each container "drinking water" with the date. Replace water you stored yourself every six months.{{cite:cdc-water-store}}
  4. Keep it cool, shaded and away from chemicals. The CDC's range is 50°F to 70°F, out of direct sunlight, away from gasoline and pesticides.{{cite:cdc-water-store}}

How many gallons you need is in {{link:water-math|water math}}. What to do when the stored water runs out, or you doubt it, is in {{link:making-water-safer|making water safer in an emergency}}.

Bottled or self-filled: pick the lazy option first

The simplest way to store water safely is to buy it already sealed. The CDC calls unopened, commercially bottled water the safest and most reliable source in an emergency, and says to use it for drinking, cooking and brushing teeth.{{cite:cdc-water-store}} Ready.gov's advice is the same: buy commercially bottled water and keep it in the sealed original container in a cool, dark place.{{cite:ready-water}} Check the expiration date on store-bought water and rotate accordingly.{{cite:cdc-water-store}}

Filling your own containers is cheaper per gallon and lets you store larger volumes, but it puts every step of the hygiene on you. The CDC's instruction in that case is to fill clean containers, and the rest of this guide is the detail of what "clean" means.{{cite:cdc-water-store}}

Editor's note A sensible split for many households is a base of sealed bottled water for the first three days, plus self-filled larger containers for the rest of a two-week target. That keeps the highest-trust water for the earliest hours and spreads the labor. It is a suggestion, not an official recommendation.

Choosing a container

The CDC's first preference is an FDA-approved, food-grade water-storage container. These are sold at surplus and camping supply stores, and if you are not sure a container is food-grade, you should contact its manufacturer. The reason given is that such containers will not transfer toxic substances into the water.{{cite:cdc-water-store}} Ready.gov likewise says to purchase food-grade containers if you prepare your own.{{cite:ready-water}}

If you cannot use a food-grade container, the CDC says to choose one that:{{cite:cdc-water-store}}

  • has a top that closes tightly;
  • is made of durable, unbreakable materials, and not glass;
  • has a narrow neck or opening if possible, so you can pour from it.

Never reuse a container that held chemicals

The CDC says not to use containers that previously held liquid or solid toxic chemicals, such as bleach or pesticides.{{cite:cdc-water-store}} Washing does not make that safe, and the guidance does not say that it does.

Cleaning and sanitizing, step by step

Before filling any container, the CDC gives these steps:{{cite:cdc-water-store}}

  1. Wash the container with soap and rinse completely with water.
  2. Sanitize it with a solution of 1 teaspoon of unscented liquid household chlorine bleach in 1 quart (4 cups) of water. Use bleach that contains 5%-9% sodium hypochlorite.
  3. Cover and shake well so the solution touches all the inside surfaces.
  4. Wait at least 30 seconds, then pour the solution out.
  5. Dry. Let the empty container air-dry before use.
  6. Fill with clean water and cover it with a tight lid.

Ready.gov's version is consistent: clean thoroughly with dishwashing soap and sanitize with one teaspoon of non-scented household chlorine bleach to a quart of water before filling with chlorinated water.{{cite:ready-water}}

Editor's note Two small notes from reading the sources side by side. First, both name unscented or non-scented bleach; use that and not a fragranced or specialty formula. Second, the CDC's strength range is 5%-9% while Ready.gov, in its own water-treatment section, uses a narrower 5.25% to 6.0%. For sanitizing a container the two pages give the same quantity, so this guide follows the CDC. For treating drinking water the numbers matter more, and that is covered separately, with the differences stated, in the guide on making water safer.

Filling, labeling and dating

The CDC's instruction for water stored from your home is to pour clean water into the sanitized container and cover it with a tight lid.{{cite:cdc-water-store}} Ready.gov says to fill with chlorinated water.{{cite:ready-water}} Neither page gives instructions for filling from an untreated source, and this guide does not either.

Label each container "drinking water" and include the storage date.{{cite:cdc-water-store}} The date is the single piece of information that makes rotation possible. A container with no date is a container you cannot trust and will eventually have to pour out.

Where to keep it

The CDC's storage conditions, for water you store from your home:{{cite:cdc-water-store}}

  • A cool place, in the range of 50°F to 70°F.
  • Away from direct sunlight.
  • Away from toxic substances such as gasoline or pesticides.

That last line is the one that gets broken in practice. A garage is the obvious large, out-of-the-way place, and it is also where fuel, solvents and pesticides tend to live and where summer temperatures can leave the 50-70°F range. The guidance does not say a garage is unsafe, but a household that keeps water there should check both the temperature and what the containers are sitting next to.

Editor's note Water is heavy, a little over 8 pounds a gallon, so the best storage place is often a closet floor or the bottom of a pantry, not a high shelf or an attic. Stack so the oldest containers are in front. A small notebook or a sticky label with the date of the next swap saves you from relying on memory.

Rotation: the six-month rule

Both sources agree on the interval. The CDC says to replace water in containers you filled yourself every six months, and Ready.gov says the same for water that has not been commercially bottled.{{cite:cdc-water-store}}{{cite:ready-water}} Commercially bottled water has its own expiration date.

Editor's note A rotation habit that works for many households is to tie the swap to two dates already on the calendar, for example the two dates each year when the clocks change in the United States, which fall about six months apart. Whatever anchor you pick, make rotation do double duty: use the old water for watering plants or cleaning, refill the container using the same cleaning steps, and write the new date on it. The point is not the exact day. It is that no container is ever older than six months.

Taking water out without spoiling the rest

If you use a scoop or other device to take water from a container, the CDC says to use a clean one each time. Do not touch the water or the inside of the container with your hands, and do not scoop out water with your hands.{{cite:cdc-water-store}} Pouring from a container with a narrow neck, which the CDC prefers for this reason, avoids the issue.{{cite:cdc-water-store}}

Keep a bottle of unscented liquid household chlorine bleach with 5%-9% sodium hypochlorite in your supplies. The CDC lists it for disinfecting water if you need to, and for general cleaning and sanitizing.{{cite:cdc-water-store}} It does not do the job of stored water, but it extends your options when the stored water runs out.

If you doubt a container

The CDC and Ready.gov do not publish a test for stored water that has gone off, and this guide will not invent one. Editor's note Our practical rule is to treat water you cannot vouch for, such as a container with no date or a lid that was not tight, the way you would treat any questionable water: use the methods in the guide on making water safer, or use bottled water instead. Do not rely on smell or taste.

Then: casks aboard ship

Documented history Captain David Porter of USS Essex wrote that the only precaution needed to have good water at sea was casks made of well-seasoned staves, cleaned and filled with pure water. If casks had to be filled with seawater for the ship's trim, they were to be filled, soaked and cleaned with fresh water before use for drinking. As proof the water had not spoiled, he cited a small live fish, bred from spawn in a cask filled in the Delaware River, that was pumped out of it.{{cite:usscm-brenckle-2019}}

Now: your containers

Official guidance Food-grade container, washed, sanitized and dried; dated label; cool, dark and away from chemicals; replaced every six months.{{cite:cdc-water-store}}

Porter's whole method was a container rule, and so is the modern one. His fish was a sailor's proof of freshness, not a safety test, and this guide does not recommend any such check.

A one-page storage checklist

Everything above, compressed into the form you can print and tape inside a closet door. Each line maps to the CDC or Ready.gov guidance cited in this guide.{{cite:cdc-water-store}}{{cite:ready-water}}

  • Sealed bottled water is stored in its original container, cool and dark, with the expiration date visible.
  • Self-filled containers are food-grade, with tight lids, and were never used for chemicals.
  • Each was washed with soap, rinsed, sanitized with 1 teaspoon of unscented 5%-9% bleach per quart, shaken, left at least 30 seconds, emptied and air-dried before filling.
  • Each is labeled "drinking water" with the date it was filled.
  • They sit between 50°F and 70°F, out of direct sunlight, away from gasoline, pesticides and other toxic substances.
  • Self-filled water is swapped at least every six months, with the old water reused for cleaning or plants.
  • A clean scoop, or a narrow-necked container, is used for withdrawal. Hands never touch the water.
  • A bottle of unscented 5%-9% bleach is on hand, in the supplies and not in the water store.

If space is tight

Small homes and apartments are where the six-month rule is hardest to keep. Editor's note Prefer many small sealed bottles to a few large drums if you cannot lift or move large containers, since the weight of a filled container is a safety issue on its own. Use the dead space under beds and in the bottoms of closets. A supply that is only enough for the first three days, and that you actually rotate, is a better outcome than a fourteen-day plan that exists only in your head. The official guidance does not address small-space strategies, so none of these suggestions is an official recommendation.

What this guide has and has not done

Every storage instruction here comes from the CDC's emergency-water page and Ready.gov's water page, read in full on the date shown. The Ready.gov page was last updated in 2021. We have not tested containers or water.
