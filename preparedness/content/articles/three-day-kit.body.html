What to do now

  1. Start with water. The CDC baseline is at least 1 gallon of water per person per day, for at least 3 days; it suggests aiming for 2 weeks if you can.{{cite:cdc-water-store}} FEMA's guide tells you to store commercially bottled water with your kit, not tap water.{{cite:ready-are-you-ready}} The arithmetic is in {{link:water-math|water math}}.
  2. Add food you already eat. Ready.gov says to store at least a several-day supply of non-perishable food and to choose foods your family will actually eat.{{cite:start-ready-food}}
  3. Gather what you already own. Flashlight, batteries, blankets, a first aid kit, a can opener, a radio, a phone charger and sturdy shoes are all on the official list.{{cite:start-ready-kit}} Put them in one place before you buy anything.
  4. Cover medicines and papers. Ask your pharmacist or doctor how to make an emergency supply of any prescriptions, keep a written list of them, and keep copies of key documents.{{cite:start-cdc-pyh-prescriptions}}{{cite:start-ready-kit}}
  5. Put it all in one or two containers that you can carry, and tell everyone where they are.{{cite:start-ready-kit}}

If there is an emergency now, call 911 or your local emergency number and follow local officials. A kit is for the days after help has told you to wait or stay put.

What "three days" means, and what it does not

FEMA's 2020 guide describes being prepared as having your own food, water and other supplies "to last for at least 72 hours," and adds that most of the recommended items are inexpensive and easy to find.{{cite:ready-are-you-ready}} Ready.gov's current kit page speaks of several days rather than a specific number: after an emergency you may need to survive on your own for several days, so you should have a supply to last for several days.{{cite:start-ready-kit}}

Editor's note Treat three days as a starting target and not as a forecast. No source we read says that emergencies last three days. It is a size that most households can reach in a few weeks of ordinary shopping, and it is a sensible base to build on if you later extend to a week or two. The {{link:household-emergency-plan|household emergency plan}} guide places the kit in the larger picture of the first hour, the first 72 hours and the first two weeks.

Water: the one line you should not economize on

Water is the first line of the list and, for most households, the most important one. The CDC says to store at least 1 gallon per person per day for at least 3 days and to try for 2 weeks.{{cite:cdc-water-store}} FEMA's guide gives the same gallon figure and says that, depending on where you live, you may wish to keep 3, 7 or even 14 days of water on hand.{{cite:ready-are-you-ready}} How to scale this to your household, including extras for heat, illness, pregnancy and pets, is in {{link:water-math|the water-math guide}}; how to keep it clean is in {{link:two-week-water-storage|the storage guide}}; and what to do if the water you have is questionable is in {{link:making-water-safer|making water safer}}.

Editor's note If you can only afford one improvement this month, put the money into water. A household can eat from a pantry, eat less, or eat what is already open. It cannot replace water with anything.

Food: what you will actually eat

Ready.gov's food page says the point of an emergency food supply is to have foods that do not need refrigeration, cooking, water or special preparation, along with a manual can opener and utensils. It adds three rules: store at least a several-day supply, choose foods your family will eat, and remember any special dietary needs.{{cite:start-ready-food}} The foods it lists include ready-to-eat canned meats, fruits and vegetables, protein or fruit bars, dry cereal or granola, peanut butter, dried fruit, canned juices, non-perishable pasteurized milk, high-energy foods, food for infants, and comfort or stress foods.{{cite:start-ready-food}}

Editor's note The "foods you will eat" rule is the one that saves money. A pantry stocked with unfamiliar emergency food is likely to be forgotten, to expire and to be rejected by children at the worst moment. A pantry made of two extra cans of things you eat every month can be rotated through normal meals so nothing is wasted. We have not priced anything, and no source we read gives a cost for a kit.

Food safety basics

  • Do not eat from cans that are swollen, dented or corroded, even if the contents look safe.{{cite:start-ready-food}} FEMA's guide adds that you should throw out any canned good that is expired or has become swollen, dented or corroded.{{cite:ready-are-you-ready}}
  • Keep canned food in a cool, dry place and store boxed food in tightly closed plastic or metal containers.{{cite:start-ready-kit}} Put new items at the back and older ones in front.{{cite:ready-are-you-ready}}
  • An unopened refrigerator keeps food cold for about four hours, and Ready.gov's power-outage page says a full freezer holds for about 48 hours. Ready.gov's food and power-outage pages say to discard perishable food that has been above 40°F for two hours or more.{{cite:start-ready-power}}{{cite:start-ready-food}} FEMA's newer December 2023 fact sheet says to throw away refrigerated food exposed to 40°F or higher for more than 4 hours.{{cite:start-fema-power-sheet}} Uncertain The two FEMA sources differ, and we cannot say which is intended to govern. Editor's note When in doubt, throw it out, as both documents say, and use the shorter time if you are unsure how long food was warm.
  • Charcoal grills and camp stoves are for outdoor use only.{{cite:start-ready-food}} Ready.gov's power-outage page places generators, camp stoves and grills outdoors and at least 20 feet from windows, doors and attached garages, and says never to heat a home with a gas stovetop or oven.{{cite:start-ready-power}} FEMA's 2023 fact sheet gives the same 20 feet from building openings such as windows, doors or garages, says never to use a grill or dryer to heat a home, and says not to leave a vehicle running in a garage, even with the door open.{{cite:start-fema-power-sheet}} Editor's note Twenty feet is the agencies' stated distance, not a promise of safety. Follow the appliance maker's instructions, never run fuel-burning equipment indoors, and keep working carbon monoxide alarms on every level. Wind, a nearby window or an open door can change the picture, and this page does not replace the maker's manual or your local fire department's advice.

For babies, Ready.gov's food page recommends ready-to-feed formula and says that if you must mix formula you should use bottled water, or boiled water as a last resort.{{cite:start-ready-food}} The CDC's infant checklist agrees that ready-to-feed formula is the safest formula option in an emergency, and asks you to check an infant's kit monthly because babies grow quickly.{{cite:start-cdc-infant-checklist}} More on this is in {{link:go-bags|the go-bag guide}}.

Light, radio and charging

The official list includes a radio that works on batteries or a hand crank, with NOAA Weather Radio tone alerts, a flashlight, spare batteries and a phone with chargers and a backup battery.{{cite:start-ready-kit}} Ready.gov's power-outage page also tells you to take an inventory of the items you rely on that need electricity, and to plan for batteries or a power bank to cover them.{{cite:start-ready-power}}

Editor's note This is where households quietly fail. A flashlight with dead batteries, or batteries of the wrong size, looks fine in the drawer. Test every light and radio when you assemble the kit, tape spare batteries to the radio, and write the date on them. If anyone in the household uses a medical device that runs on power, that is a separate planning question and not a flashlight question; see {{link:preparedness-real-households|preparedness in real households}}.

Almost every list tells you to include medicine, but they do not agree on how much. This is a real disagreement among official and semi-official sources, and we set it out in full.

Medicines that need refrigeration: ask your prescriber or pharmacist first

Editor's note Do not stop, throw away or ration a medicine you depend on because of anything on this page. Ask your prescriber or pharmacist, before an emergency, whether each of your medicines needs refrigeration, how long it can safely stay warm, and what the plan is for getting a replacement. If you are already in an outage and unsure, call your doctor or pharmacist. If someone is unwell, or may have taken the wrong medicine, call your local emergency number or poison control.

Official guidance FEMA's December 2023 power-outage fact sheet says to make a plan for refrigerating medicines and powering medical devices, and to talk to your doctor about storing your medications. After an outage it says: "Replace refrigerated medications if the power is out for a day or more, unless the drug's label says otherwise. Call your doctor or pharmacist if you depend on refrigerated medications that have been at room temperature. Only use the medicine until you have a new supply."{{cite:start-fema-power-sheet}} Ready.gov's power-outage page says to talk to your medical provider about a power-outage plan and to find out how long medication can be stored at higher temperatures.{{cite:start-ready-power}}

Two FEMA documents, two wordings

FEMA's 2020 "Are You Ready?" guide says, "If the power is out for more than a day, discard any medication that should be refrigerated, unless the drug's label says otherwise,"{{cite:ready-are-you-ready}} and the Ready.gov power-outages web page repeats a discard instruction.{{cite:start-ready-power}} The newer December 2023 sheet says to replace such medication, to call your doctor or pharmacist if you depend on it, and to use it only until you have a new supply.{{cite:start-fema-power-sheet}} Editor's note We follow the newer, more cautious wording. The older "discard" line, read alone, could lead someone to go without a medicine they need before a replacement is in hand. The right answer for each medicine comes from your prescriber or pharmacist, not from a general rule, which is why we ask you to arrange it ahead of time. We could not establish which FEMA document the agency intends to govern, and the Ready.gov web page has not been updated to match the newer sheet.

Editor's note A practical approach is to ask your pharmacist two questions: how large an emergency supply can I get, and how should I rotate it? Then keep a written list of every medicine with diagnosis, dose, frequency and allergies, which is the CDC's recommendation.{{cite:start-cdc-pyh-prescriptions}} Also ask what interim-use and replacement plan they recommend if a refrigerated medicine warms up, and what your state's emergency refill rules allow; the CDC notes that some states have such laws.{{cite:start-cdc-pyh-prescriptions}}

Two items worth a second look

Masks. The Ready.gov kit page lists a "dust mask (to help filter contaminated air)."{{cite:start-ready-kit}} FEMA's 2020 guide is more specific: it recommends several N95 respirator masks, each fit-tested to ensure a tight seal on the face.{{cite:ready-are-you-ready}} Uncertain The pages differ in how specific they are; neither tells you which hazards call for which type, so check your local emergency management office for what suits the hazards where you live.

The wrench. The official list includes a wrench or pliers to turn off utilities.{{cite:start-ready-kit}} FEMA's guide, in its evacuation checklist, says to shut off water, gas and electricity before leaving only if there is damage to your home and you are told to do so, and that you should learn how beforehand from a professional plumber or electrician, your utility provider or Ready.gov.{{cite:ready-are-you-ready}} Editor's note Putting a tool in the kit is not the same as knowing how to use it. If you do not know where your shutoffs are, finding them is a better use of an afternoon than buying anything.

Where it lives, and how it stays ready

FEMA's guide recommends keeping items in airtight plastic bags and putting the whole kit in one or two easy-to-carry containers, such as an unused trash can, camping backpack or duffel bag.{{cite:ready-are-you-ready}} Ready.gov names plastic bins or a duffel bag.{{cite:start-ready-kit}} It adds that the home kit should be in a designated place, ready in case you have to leave quickly, and that everyone should know where it is.{{cite:start-ready-kit}}

The car is the other place for a kit. Ready.gov lists the extras it wants in a vehicle (jumper cables, flares or a reflective triangle, ice scraper, car phone charger, blanket, map, and cat litter or sand for traction) and says to keep the gas tank full in case of evacuation or power outages.{{cite:start-ready-car}}

The maintenance steps are short: keep canned food cool and dry, replace expired items, use food before it spoils and replace it with fresh supplies.{{cite:start-ready-kit}}{{cite:ready-are-you-ready}} The question is how often to check. FEMA's 2020 guide says every six months, as do the CDC's disability and children's checklists; Ready.gov's current kit page says to re-think your needs every year.{{cite:ready-are-you-ready}}{{cite:start-cdc-disability-kit}}{{cite:start-cdc-family-kit}}{{cite:start-ready-kit}} Editor's note Check contents (dates, batteries, clothing sizes) every six months and review the whole kit against your household once a year. If you have a baby, check the infant items monthly, as the CDC advises.{{cite:start-cdc-infant-checklist}}

Building it on a budget

Ready.gov's low-cost material is short but practical: look for deals at garage sales and thrift stores, where camping gear, tools and cookware can be cheaper; have a family evacuation plan; use the free preparedness resources and free communication-plan form; consider adding older clothing that still fits; and sign up for local emergency alerts.{{cite:start-ready-low-cost}}

Editor's note Here is one way to stage it over a few months, in an order that puts the most important things first. This is our suggestion, not an agency schedule.

  1. Costs nothing. Write the plan page. Sign up for local alerts. Find the flashlight, blankets, can opener and first aid supplies already in the house and put them in a single container. Photograph key documents.
  2. Costs a few dollars a week. Add a case of bottled water each time you shop until you reach three days of water for the household. Add two shelf-stable items you actually eat.
  3. Costs a bit more. A battery or hand-crank radio, a set of spare batteries, a power bank, a whistle.
  4. Last. Anything the official list calls optional or that depends on your hazards.

The things we would skip until the basics are done are exactly the things marketing leans on: elaborate kits sold as a package, specialty emergency food, and gadgets. None is on the official list's first tier. We do not recommend or link to any product, and this page contains no affiliate links.

When three days are not enough

A three-day kit is a floor. The Red Cross's guidance for older adults is to plan to stay home for at least two weeks or to evacuate,{{cite:start-redcross-older-adults}} and the CDC recommends aiming for two weeks of water if you can.{{cite:cdc-water-store}} Households with babies, with medical equipment that needs power, with a person who needs particular foods or with a pet will need more, and sooner. For the leaving case, the {{link:go-bags|go-bag guide}} sets out what is different about a bag that must be carried. For the household whose circumstances make the standard advice hard to follow, see {{link:preparedness-real-households|preparedness for renters, apartments, low income, caregiving and disability access}}.
