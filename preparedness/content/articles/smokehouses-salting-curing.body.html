Read this first

This is a history, not a method. Nothing on this page is a cure formula, a brine strength, a smoking time or a temperature for curing, and it should not be used as one. We give none by design.

  • Home-cured, smoked, preserved and fermented foods have been linked to botulism cases. You cannot see, smell or taste the toxin.{{cite:fsis-home-cure-qa}}{{cite:cdc-botulism-prevention}}
  • If you want to cure or smoke meat at home, use a procedure published and tested by USDA or the National Center for Home Food Preservation, and follow it exactly.{{cite:nchfp-cure-smoke}}
  • Smoking in a modern guide means cooking. USDA's page on smoking meat is about slow cooking in a purpose-built smoker, with a thermometer, not about traditional smoke-curing.{{cite:hist-food-fsis-smoking}}

If someone develops blurred vision, difficulty swallowing or speaking, or growing weakness after eating a cured or preserved food, treat it as a medical emergency and call your emergency number.

The craft, as the historic sites describe it

For most of American history, a household that kept pigs and wanted meat in summer cured it in the cold months. Two of the best-documented places where that happened are the plantations of Thomas Jefferson and George Washington, and both are described by their museums in terms that are candid about who did the work.

At Monticello, the Thomas Jefferson Encyclopedia says Ursula Granger and John (surname unknown), like others with their skills, dried and preserved butchered meat during the winter months. John cut the meat into smaller pieces for hams, bacon and salt pork. Granger covered the cuts in salt, which dried and preserved them by drawing moisture out. They hung the salted meat in the smokehouse near a low-burning hardwood fire, whose smoke "continued to dry, cure, and flavor the meat". The preserved pork, beef, mutton and fish was then rationed to enslaved people, hired workers and Jefferson's household.Documented history{{cite:hist-food-monticello-meat}} The same entry notes that prime cuts were intended for Jefferson's household, while enslaved workers and their families received less desirable cuts.{{cite:hist-food-monticello-meat}}

At Mount Vernon, the museum says the smokehouse, built in 1775 according to its virtual tour, smoked meat over a fire pit, mainly bacon and ham, "to feed the family and Mount Vernon's guests". After enslaved workers salted or pickled the meat, they hung it on rails inside the smokehouse above a smoldering fire in a central pit. For long-term storage the meats stayed hanging or were packed in barrels filled with ashes. During the curing process the meat was locked in the smokehouse to prevent theft, a precaution that did not always work: in May 1795, Washington's farm manager William Pearce reported that someone had ripped a plank off the back of the smokehouse and taken several pieces of bacon. Washington could smoke upwards of 300 hams a year, and in 1786 Martha Washington packed a barrel of Virginia hams for the Marquis de Lafayette.{{cite:hist-food-mv-smokehouse}}

Neither page gives a quantity of salt, a length of time, a type of cut beyond "hams, bacon and salt pork", or a temperature. That is not an omission by us. We found no number in either source, and we do not supply one from elsewhere.

Who did the work

Editor's note Both museums name enslaved workers as the people who salted, smoked and rationed the meat, and Monticello records the unequal distribution of cuts. When a "traditional" method is praised, it is worth asking whose knowledge it was and who ate which part of the result. The sources name Ursula Granger and John; we do not know how many others did this work or what they themselves said about it.

Salt and saltpetre in a contract

A third documented example is the barrelled meat of the Navy. The 1812 contract circular quoted in the USS Constitution Museum's study demanded "a sufficient quantity of good Salt and Salt Petre" and gave no ratio, no curing time and no brine strength.{{cite:usscm-brenckle-2019}} Our companion piece on {{link:salt-beef-salt-pork-age-of-sail|salt beef and salt pork at sea}} covers that record in full. The point here is that the sources we could read agree on one thing: the quantities and times lived in the heads and hands of the curers, not on the page.

There is a related contrast in the American West. Captain Randolph Marcy's 1859 handbook for overland travellers says fresh meat can be "jerked" on the prairie by hanging thin strips in the sun, and that "salt is never used in this process". It is not required, he says, as the meat, if kept dry, rarely putrefies.{{cite:hist-food-marcy-prairie-traveler-text}} Salt cures and sun-dried meat are different traditions with different logic, and the next sections explain why neither tells us what is safe for a household today.

What science explained later

The craft came first and the explanation came after. The National Center for Home Food Preservation's historical overview says early curing was "actually dehydration": early cultures used salt to help desiccate foods, and salting was common and even culinary, with cooks choosing raw salts from different sources. In the 1800s, it adds, it was discovered that certain salts gave meat a red colour instead of an unappetizing grey; those salts contained nitrites, or saltpeter. Only as the microbiology of Clostridium botulinum was worked out in the 1920s was it realized that nitrites inhibited that organism.{{cite:nchfp-historical-origins}}

USDA's food-safety service gives the regulatory side. Nitrites in cured meat inhibit the growth of C. botulinum spores, and USDA began authorizing nitrite in meat and poultry products in 1925. Concern about nitrite forming nitrosamines was raised in the early 1970s, and processors may use ascorbic acid or related compounds to counter it.{{cite:fsis-botulism}} The NCHFP describes curing as using salt, and sometimes sugar and seasonings, to draw moisture from meat and create an environment hostile to harmful microorganisms.{{cite:nchfp-cure-smoke}}

Editor's note Put side by side, these sources show a pattern. Salt dehydrated meat, and the people who did it had no way to know that part of the protection came from the nitrate in some of their salt, or that without enough of it, a different hazard could remain. A craft can be effective without being understood, and equally can be partly ineffective without anyone noticing. A lack of recorded illness in a museum page about a smokehouse tells us only that the page does not count illnesses.

The same caution applies to the "traditional" foods that modern regulators still single out. FSIS lists traditionally prepared salted or fermented fish among foods linked to recent botulism illness.{{cite:fsis-botulism}} The sources do not claim that those foods are inherently dangerous, only that tradition has not protected people from the hazard.

What smoke does, and what the sources do not say

Monticello says the smoke from a low-burning hardwood fire "continued to dry, cure, and flavor" the salted meat.{{cite:hist-food-monticello-meat}} Mount Vernon calls the building a place to "smoke meat over a fire pit" and says meats stayed hanging or were packed in ashes for long-term storage.{{cite:hist-food-mv-smokehouse}} Neither page explains how much of the preservation was due to the salt and how much to the smoke or the drying, and neither gives the temperature of the fire.

Modern food-safety guidance takes a different view of the word. USDA defines smoking as slowly cooking food indirectly over a fire, in a smoker made of materials approved for contact with meat, not in "makeshift containers such as galvanized steel cans", with a thermometer to hold the air temperature in a stated range and with cooking to safe minimum internal temperatures. It tells readers to refrigerate smoked meat promptly.{{cite:hist-food-fsis-smoking}} That is guidance for cooking meat you will eat soon, not for a smokehouse where meat hung for weeks. We found no federal page that validates the second use for a household.

Mold is a further wrinkle. USDA's guide to molds notes that molds tolerate salt and sugar better than most other food invaders, and that dry-cured country hams normally have surface mold that must be scrubbed off before cooking, while luncheon meats, bacon and hot dogs with mold should be discarded.{{cite:hist-food-fsis-molds}} That is a detail about commercial dry-cured hams, not an instruction for a home smokehouse. We cite it to show that even in the modern rules, cured meat is not free of hazard and the line between "normal" and "discard" depends on the specific product. The guide is dated 2013; check the agency's current page.

Where the craft failed or fell short

The historic-site pages mention theft and cuts distributed by status, not spoilage, so they say very little about failure. The records we can read name the risks only indirectly. Mount Vernon says the meat was locked in the smokehouse during curing to prevent theft.{{cite:hist-food-mv-smokehouse}} Monticello's entry says the best cuts were kept for the household and the less desirable for the enslaved.{{cite:hist-food-monticello-meat}} The Navy study's historian argues that the Navy's salt meat was generally of good quality but acknowledges the gap in the evidence: nobody found a tally of condemned barrels.{{cite:usscm-brenckle-2019}}

What we can say is narrow: the sources describe a cured, smoked and stored meat that fed households and ships for months, record no method detail, and tell us almost nothing about how often it spoiled or sickened anyone. We should not turn that silence into a claim of safety.

Current safety verdict

Not validated for modern household use

Do not reproduce these methods at home, and do not cure meat from a history page. Salt, smoke and drying were real preservation tools, but nothing on this page or in the museum sources gives the quantities, times and temperatures on which safety depends, and we supply none.

  • The CDC lists improperly preserved foods among the most common sources of botulism, says you cannot see, smell or taste the toxin, and says to throw out food you cannot vouch for.{{cite:cdc-botulism-prevention}}
  • USDA's food-safety service records many botulism cases following home-canned, preserved, cured, smoked or fermented foods and points consumers to the NCHFP for tested methods.{{cite:fsis-home-cure-qa}}
  • FSIS notes that nitrite in cured meat inhibits C. botulinum spores and that traditionally prepared salted or fermented fish have been linked to botulism.{{cite:fsis-botulism}}
  • For smoking as cooking, use USDA's smoking guidance, a purpose-built smoker and a thermometer.{{cite:hist-food-fsis-smoking}}

We have not reproduced any cure quantity. Anything that names a quantity should come directly from NCHFP or an Extension publication, with the whole method.

What we have not done

We read two short museum pages (Monticello's entry on the preservation of meat and Mount Vernon's smokehouse page) and not the underlying farm books, plantation records or archaeology. Neither page gives method detail, and the Mount Vernon page notes that a ham producer sponsored the smokehouse restoration. We did not read the Mount Vernon digital encyclopedia article on hogs; its text was not available to us.

We read the USDA and NCHFP pages that the register already held as verified (the cure-and-smoke overview, the botulism page and the home-curing Q&A) as summarized there, and the NCHFP history overview and USDA smoking and mold pages directly. The mold page is dated 2013 and the NCHFP overview 2002. We found no account in the words of an enslaved worker about smokehouse work, and the page says so rather than imply one.
