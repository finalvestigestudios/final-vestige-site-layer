Read this first

This is a guide to why the rules exist and where the real ones live. It is not a canning recipe and not a substitute for a class. The processes themselves are in the USDA Complete Guide to Home Canning and the National Center for Home Food Preservation (NCHFP), which describes itself as a source of current research-based home-preservation recommendations.{{cite:nchfp-home}} We link to them and do not reprint their times and pressures, because a table copied out of context is how a safe process becomes an unsafe one.

  1. Low-acid foods can only be canned at home in a pressure canner, following a tested USDA/NCHFP process exactly. Low-acid foods are meats, poultry, fish and all fresh vegetables (with some tomatoes and figs). The CDC says pressure canning is the only recommended method for them, and that a boiling-water canner will not protect against botulism.{{cite:food-cdc-home-canned}}
  2. Use only tested recipes. The CDC says to use only recipes and cookbooks that follow the USDA guide, and not to use other recipes "even if you got them from a trusted friend or family member".{{cite:food-cdc-home-canned}}
  3. Do not use an electric multi-cooker for canning, even if it has a "canning" button.{{cite:food-cdc-home-canned}}{{cite:food-nchfp-multicooker}}
  4. You cannot see, smell or taste botulinum toxin. If you do not know whether a jar was canned by a safe method, do not eat it. The CDC's rule is "If in doubt, throw it out".{{cite:cdc-botulism-prevention}}

Botulism is a medical emergency. Symptoms can include double vision, droopy eyelids, trouble swallowing and difficulty breathing, and they typically start 12 to 48 hours after eating contaminated food.{{cite:food-fsis-shelf-stable}} If you or anyone you know has them after eating home-canned food, call your emergency number immediately. If you are new to canning, ask your county Cooperative Extension office about classes before you buy equipment; NCHFP's pages link a directory of local experts and classes.{{cite:food-nchfp-using-pressure-canners}}

  • Uncertain A question where the official pages disagree or are silent.
  • Editor's note Our reasoning. It is not a statement from the source and is marked so you can weigh it differently.

Why low-acid food is different

Most spoilage in a canned jar is a nuisance. One kind is not. Clostridium botulinum forms spores that survive harmlessly in soil and water for years. NCHFP explains that when conditions suit them, the spores produce cells that multiply rapidly and can make a deadly toxin within 3 to 4 days in an environment with three features: a moist, low-acid food; a temperature between 40°F and 120°F; and less than 2 percent oxygen.{{cite:food-nchfp-ensuring-safe}} Editor's note A sealed jar of low-acid food that has not been heated enough can be exactly that environment.

The spores are on most fresh foods, but they are harmless there because they grow only in the absence of air.{{cite:food-nchfp-ensuring-safe}} Canning removes the air. That is what makes canning work and what makes a failed canning dangerous. USDA's food-safety service puts the same point plainly: the canning process forces air out of food, so botulinum bacteria "may find incorrectly or minimally processed canned foods a good place to grow and produce the toxin", and low-acid vegetables such as green beans, corn, beets and peas are the ones at risk.{{cite:food-fsis-shelf-stable}}

What counts as low-acid

"Low-acid" means a pH above 4.6. NCHFP lists red meats, seafood, poultry, milk and all fresh vegetables except most tomatoes. Fruits, pickles, sauerkraut, jams and jellies are acid foods. Mixtures of low-acid and acid foods are usually still above 4.6 unless the recipe adds enough lemon juice, citric acid or vinegar. Tomatoes and figs sit near the line, and NCHFP says that if they are to be canned as acid foods they must be acidified.{{cite:food-nchfp-ensuring-safe}} The CDC adds that low-acid foods are the most common source of botulism linked to home canning, and that home-canned vegetables are the most common cause of botulism outbreaks in the United States.{{cite:food-cdc-home-canned}}

Why a pressure canner, specifically

Botulinum spores are very hard to destroy at boiling-water temperatures. Higher temperatures destroy them more easily, so NCHFP says all low-acid foods should be sterilized at 240°F to 250°F, which pressure canners operated at 10 to 15 PSIG can reach. At those temperatures, it says, the time needed to destroy bacteria in low-acid food ranges from 20 to 100 minutes depending on the food, the pack, and the jar size. For comparison it gives the time to do the same job in a boiling-water canner as 7 to 11 hours.{{cite:food-nchfp-ensuring-safe}}

That last figure is not an alternative method. We quote it only to show why the answer to "can I just boil the jars longer?" is no: no tested home process for low-acid food exists for a boiling-water canner, and the CDC says not to use one.{{cite:food-cdc-home-canned}}

A second point from NCHFP is easy to miss: it is temperature that kills spores, not pressure. "Pressure does not destroy microorganisms, but high temperatures applied for an adequate period of time do kill microorganisms." The pressure matters because the temperature of pure steam depends on it. At sea level a canner at 10.5 pounds of gauge pressure gives an internal temperature of 240°F.{{cite:food-nchfp-recommended-canners}} That is why trapped air is dangerous (see below) and why a gadget that merely reaches a pressure does not prove it has reached a temperature.

What makes a process "validated"

A tested home-canning process is a package. It is a set of conditions under which researchers measured how fast the center of a jar heats and showed that the slowest-heating point reached a lethal temperature for long enough. Change any condition, and the measurement no longer applies. NCHFP makes the point in a post about one such shortcut: the USDA low-acid processes are meant to be used with the full procedures as written, meaning how to select and prepare the food, how to fill the jars, how to run the step-by-step process in a recommended type of pressure canner, and how to make altitude adjustments.{{cite:food-nchfp-dry-canning}}

The outline of a pressure-canner run

NCHFP's step-by-step instructions are the ones to follow. We summarise their shape only to show why the steps exist.{{cite:food-nchfp-using-pressure-canners}}{{cite:food-nchfp-recommended-canners}}

  1. Check the canner before you prepare food: clean the gasket, clear the vent pipe, make sure the burner and range are level. NCHFP says in general not to use a canner on an outdoor LP-gas burner or a range burner over 12,000 BTU, and to check the manufacturer's directions.
  2. Fill and load jars; fasten the lid; leave the weight off the vent.
  3. Vent steam continuously for 10 minutes on high heat before you close the vent.
  4. Come to the pressure the process requires, and only then start the timer.
  5. Hold steady pressure. NCHFP flags this in bold: if the pressure goes below the recommended amount at any time, "bring the canner back to pressure and begin the timing of the process over, from the beginning (using the total original process time)".
  6. Turn off the heat and let the canner cool and depressurize by itself. Forced cooling "may result in food spoilage" or unsafe food.
  7. Open carefully, lift the lid away from your face, and leave the jars undisturbed to cool for 12 to 24 hours before checking seals.

That is not enough to can with. The sections on elevation, the individual food's preparation and the manufacturer's manual are all missing on purpose.

The shortcuts, and what the authorities say

The internet has produced a steady supply of ways to can without a pressure canner, or with one but less carefully. Each sounds plausible. Here is what the official sources say about each, in one place.

Boiling-water ("water-bath") canning of meat or vegetablesThe CDC says not to use a boiling water canner for low-acid foods because it will not protect against botulism.{{cite:food-cdc-home-canned}}
Electric multi-cooker with a "canning" or "steam canning" buttonThe CDC says not to use one. NCHFP does not support using USDA processes in them even when the manufacturer gives instructions, because no thermal-process testing has been done with jars in them, temperature (not pressure) is what counts, and power surges or drops could lower the temperature without the user knowing.{{cite:food-cdc-home-canned}}{{cite:food-nchfp-multicooker}}

NCHFP's post about "dry canning" contains a line worth keeping about why a shortcut feels safe: "Someone can get lucky and not get sick from trying this. Bacteria are not distributed evenly throughout our environment. But the next time, Clostridium botulinum or other harmful bacteria can be in the jar."{{cite:food-nchfp-dry-canning}} Editor's note A shortcut that has worked for years has not been shown safe. It may simply not have met a spore yet.

A note on steam canners

You may see a recommendation for atmospheric steam canners. NCHFP's September 2026 statement considers atmospheric steam canning a safe method for high-acid foods such as fruits, jams, pickles and tomatoes when tested recipes and its extra steps are followed. It says nothing that extends this to low-acid foods.{{cite:food-nchfp-steam-canning}} Separately, NCHFP's older page on equipment still says steam canners are not currently recommended and points to the newer guidance.{{cite:food-nchfp-not-recommended}} Editor's note The stale page is a documentation inconsistency, not a safety one for our topic. For low-acid foods, none of this changes the rule: a pressure canner.

A sealed lid is not proof

A lid that pinged and sealed tells you a vacuum formed. It does not tell you the contents reached a sterilizing temperature. The USDA guide says spoiled low-acid foods "may exhibit different kinds of spoilage evidence or very little evidence", which is why all suspect containers of spoiled low-acid food should be treated as having produced botulinum toxin.{{cite:food-usda-canning-guide-1}} NCHFP says botulism toxin can be present in canned vegetables without any visual changes or odors.{{cite:food-nchfp-dry-canning}}

The CDC lists signs that home-canned (and store-bought) food may be contaminated: a container that is leaking, bulging or swollen; a container that looks damaged, cracked or abnormal; a container that spurts liquid or foam when opened; food that is discolored, moldy or smells bad. If you see any of them, throw it out.{{cite:food-cdc-home-canned}} The absence of those signs is not an all-clear. The USDA guide says to examine each jar's lid and contents when you select it, but its first rule is "Do not taste food from a jar with an unsealed lid or food that shows signs of spoilage."{{cite:food-usda-canning-guide-1}}

If you think a jar is bad

Do not taste it, and do not let a child, pet or anyone else get at it. The USDA guide treats contact with the toxin seriously: "Contact with botulinum toxin can be fatal whether it is ingested or enters through the skin", so it tells you to wear rubber or heavy plastic gloves. Sealed suspect jars or swollen cans go into a heavy garbage bag, closed, in the regular trash. Unsealed, open or leaking containers should be detoxified by boiling before disposal, and surfaces cleaned with a fresh bleach solution.{{cite:food-usda-canning-guide-1}} FSIS adds that you should double-bag suspect cans in tightly closed plastic bags and place them in outdoor non-recyclable trash so animals and others cannot get at them.{{cite:food-fsis-shelf-stable}} We do not reprint the detoxification steps. They are in the "Identifying and handling spoiled canned food" section of Guide 1 of the USDA guide, linked in the sources. Read them before you need them.

The "boil it before eating" advice, and why it is not a rescue

The CDC tells people to boil certain home-canned foods in a saucepan before eating them: home-canned tomatoes, foods containing them, and all low-acid home-canned foods. At altitudes below 1,000 feet, boil for 10 minutes, adding 1 minute for each additional 1,000 feet of elevation. The reason given is that high temperatures can destroy the toxin.{{cite:food-cdc-home-canned}} FSIS gives the same figures but qualifies them hard: it says the boil "is not intended to serve as a recommendation for consuming foods known to be significantly underprocessed", and is "not a guarantee that all possible defects and hazards with non-recommended methods can be overcome by this boiling process".{{cite:food-fsis-shelf-stable}}

The CDC separately says: "Do not eat food if you do not know whether safe canning guidelines were followed. Throw it out!"{{cite:food-cdc-home-canned}}

Editor's note We read these as two different situations. The boil is an extra margin for jars you know were canned by a tested method. It is not a way to redeem a jar whose history you do not know, and it is certainly not a way to redeem one you know was canned by a shortcut.

The agencies do not fully agree on the boil, or on tasting

  • FSIS contradicts itself. Its shelf-stable page gives the 10-minutes-plus-1-minute-per-1,000-feet rule in its text and says properly home-canned fruits never needed the boil, but its storage chart tells you to boil home-canned foods 10 minutes if high-acid and 20 minutes if low-acid.{{cite:food-fsis-shelf-stable}} CDC agrees with the text.{{cite:food-cdc-home-canned}}
  • Utah State Extension says that if older canned food shows no strange appearance or odor, you may taste a sample, and suggests boiling older canned food for 10 minutes before tasting, or heating low-acid food to 165°F or boiling it 5 to 10 minutes.{{cite:food-usu-canned-goods}} The CDC says never to taste food to see if it is safe, and the USDA guide says not to taste food from a jar showing signs of spoilage.{{cite:food-cdc-home-canned}}{{cite:food-usda-canning-guide-1}}

Editor's note We follow the CDC and USDA on both points, and have flagged them for the reviewer. We would not taste-test a jar.

Storing what you can

The CDC says to can only the amount of food you will use in one year (unless directions for a specific food say otherwise), to label and date jars, and to store them in a clean, cool, dark place. For best quality, 50°F to 70°F; for safety, never above 95°F, and not near hot pipes, a range, a furnace, in an uninsulated attic, or in direct sunlight.{{cite:food-cdc-home-canned}} The USDA guide says the same about temperature and adds that dampness may corrode lids, break seals and allow spoilage; accidental freezing will not by itself cause spoilage unless the jars become unsealed.{{cite:food-usda-canning-guide-1}} Utah State Extension takes the stricter line that sealed jars should not be allowed to freeze.{{cite:food-usu-canned-goods}} After a flood, FSIS and the CDC say to discard home-canned foods that touched floodwater, because the jars cannot be effectively cleaned and sanitized.{{cite:food-fsis-emergencies}}{{cite:food-cdc-after-emergency}}

Is this for you? Budget, space, ability

Pressure canning is a real undertaking, and the other food guides in this archive exist partly to say that it is optional.

  • Commercially canned food is the better emergency storage. Utah State Extension says commercially canned foods are superior to home-canned foods for food storage because commercial canners control quality and safety closely.{{cite:food-usu-canned-goods}} For a household whose goal is an emergency supply, buying cans is cheaper in time, equipment and risk. See {{link:pantry-rotation|pantry rotation without waste}}.
  • Cost and equipment. A pressure canner, jars, lids, a gauge check and a level burner are all part of the process; we do not give prices because we did not verify any.
  • Learn before you buy. Many county Extension offices check dial gauges, and NCHFP keeps a directory of local experts and classes.{{cite:food-cdc-home-canned}}{{cite:food-nchfp-recommended-canners}}
  • Strength and safety. NCHFP tells canners to lift, not slide, a heavy canner, to use a jar lifter held below the neck of the jar, and to lift the lid away from the face because of steam.{{cite:food-nchfp-using-pressure-canners}} Editor's note If lifting a loaded canner or handling live steam is difficult for you, say so to your Extension office before you start. We have no official guidance on adaptive equipment.
  • Canning in an emergency. Oregon State Extension notes that some families might can meat, vegetables and fruit rather than waste them in a long power outage, while acknowledging it may be impossible after a major disaster.{{cite:food-osu-survival-food}} Editor's note NCHFP's instructions assume a level burner of suitable output, adequate water and a working gauge; none of the pages we read says how to meet those conditions on an improvised heat source. For fridge and freezer contents, see {{link:food-safety-power-outage|food safety during a power outage}}.

What this guide has and hasn't done

Every instruction and figure here comes from the CDC, USDA and NCHFP pages cited, read in full on the date shown, plus Guide 1 of the USDA Complete Guide to Home Canning. Where the pages disagree we say so. We did not reprint the process tables, the preparation steps for any specific food, the boiling-water-canner instructions, the detoxification steps, or the elevation charts, and we did not read the rest of the 40-page Guide 1, the Complete Guide's food chapters, or NCHFP's video series. A few NCHFP pages differ trivially from one another, for example on how long a canner takes to pressurize (3 to 5 minutes on one page, 3 to 10 on another). Where that matters, the current page for your canner and the manufacturer's manual control.

We have not canned anything for this guide and make no claim to have tested any process. The safety review for this guide is recorded in the box at the bottom of the page. It does not replace an editor's read-through or the launch checks, and the page stays a draft until those are done.
