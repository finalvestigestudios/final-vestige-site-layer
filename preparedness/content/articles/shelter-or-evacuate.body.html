What to do now

  1. If officials tell you to evacuate, go. Ready.gov's hurricane page says that if you live in a mandatory evacuation zone and local officials tell you to evacuate, do so immediately, and its wildfire page says the same.{{cite:shelter-ready-hurricanes}}{{cite:shelter-ready-wildfires}}
  2. If a tornado warning is issued for your area, shelter at once. Go to the safe place you identified in advance and stay away from windows, doors and outside walls. Protect your head and neck with your arms.{{cite:shelter-ready-tornadoes}}
  3. For a chemical or hazardous-materials incident, do what the local authorities say. Ready.gov says instructions differ depending on the threat and the risk of exposure: "sometimes it may be safer to shelter inside your home, other times it may be safer to evacuate."{{cite:shelter-ready-hazmat}}
  4. Get warnings in more than one way. Ready.gov names the FEMA app, community alert sign-ups, and the Emergency Alert System and Wireless Emergency Alerts; the National Weather Service describes NOAA Weather Radio.{{cite:shelter-ready-hurricanes}}{{cite:shelter-nws-nwr}} Editor's note Make sure at least one of your ways works without power.
  5. Decide before the day where you would go, by which route, and who would go with you. The rest of this guide is the detail.{{cite:shelter-ready-evacuation}}

This guide cannot tell you what to do in a particular emergency. Local officials know where the hazard is, and Ready.gov's shelter page says in all cases to stay informed and follow their instructions.{{cite:shelter-ready-shelter}} If you are in immediate danger, or if someone is injured or seriously ill, call 911.

  • Official guidance An instruction or figure from the Ready.gov or National Weather Service page cited beside it.
  • Editor's note Our reasoning or planning suggestion. It is not a statement from a source.
  • Uncertain Something the sources leave open or treat differently.

"Shelter" means three different things

Much of the confusion about "staying or going" comes from one word covering three situations. Ready.gov separates them:{{cite:shelter-ready-shelter}}

  • Stay at home: remain indoors as much as possible and leave home only when necessary.
  • Go to a mass care shelter: a public shelter that provides life-sustaining services to disaster survivors.
  • Shelter in place: stay where you are, at home, at work or elsewhere, and avoid whatever uncertainty is outside.

"Evacuate" is the fourth option, and it usually ends in the second: a mass care shelter, or the home of a friend or relative.{{cite:shelter-ready-evacuation}} Ready.gov notes that the time you are required to take shelter may be short, as in a tornado warning, or extended, in which case you will need to manage water and food supplies.{{cite:shelter-ready-shelter}}

Editor's note The honest answer to "should I shelter or evacuate?" is "it depends on the hazard and on what the people responsible for your area are telling you." The rest of this page is about how the hazard changes the answer, and how to be ready for either.

What the hazard changes

These are the actions each Ready.gov page gives for its own hazard. They are not interchangeable.

The pattern is that fast, local, violent hazards (tornadoes, flash floods) usually mean shelter now, and slower, wider hazards where the danger comes to you (hurricanes, wildfires) usually mean leaving before it arrives. Editor's note That pattern is our summary of the table, not a rule from any source, and the hazmat row shows why it cannot be a rule: the right answer there depends on details no household can know in advance.

Tornadoes: shelter, not escape

Ready.gov's tornado page lists winds that can exceed 200 miles per hour, and says a tornado can happen anytime and anywhere. The signs include a rotating, funnel-shaped cloud, an approaching cloud of debris or a loud roar like a freight train.{{cite:shelter-ready-tornadoes}} You do not evacuate from a tornado; you get to the best protection available fast. The page tells you to identify and practice going to a safe shelter ahead of time, and to cover your head and neck with your arms and put materials such as furniture and blankets around or on top of you.{{cite:shelter-ready-tornadoes}}

The page is blunt about vehicles: "There is no safe option when caught in a tornado in a car, just slightly less dangerous ones." If the tornado is visible, far away, and traffic is light, you may be able to drive out of its path at right angles. If you are caught by extreme winds or flying debris, park as quickly and safely as possible out of the traffic lanes, stay in the car with the seat belt on, and put your head down below the windows. If you can get noticeably lower than the roadway, the page says to leave the car and lie in that area, covering your head. Avoid bridges and overpasses.{{cite:shelter-ready-tornadoes}} Afterward, save phone calls for emergencies and use text messaging or social media to reach family.{{cite:shelter-ready-tornadoes}}

Uncertain The vehicle advice is the one place the page describes more than one option and asks you to judge distance and traffic in the moment. We cannot improve on it, and we do not try to.

Hurricanes: know your zone before the season

Ready.gov says hurricanes can bring storm surge, wind, rip currents and flooding, are not just a coastal problem, and can cause wind, rain, water and even tornadoes far inland from landfall.{{cite:shelter-ready-hurricanes}} The decision to go is largely made for you by the evacuation zone you live in. The page says: know your evacuation zone, learn your evacuation routes, practice with your household and pets, and identify where you will stay. Follow local emergency managers, who issue the latest recommendations.{{cite:shelter-ready-hurricanes}}

If you stay, the page's instructions for wind are to take refuge in a designated storm shelter or an interior room. If you are trapped by flooding, go to the highest level of the building and do not climb into a closed attic, where you may be trapped by rising water. It repeats "Turn Around. Don't Drown!": just six inches of fast-moving water can knock you down, and one foot of moving water can sweep a vehicle away.{{cite:shelter-ready-hurricanes}}

Editor's note Notice the structure. Evacuation is chosen before the storm, based on a zone and an order. Staying put is chosen by default only if you are not in the zone and have not been told to go, and it brings the loss-of-services problems described in {{link:outage-heat-cold-generators|heat, cold and generator safety in an outage}}.

Floods: the answer depends on the kind of flood

Ready.gov calls floods the most common disaster in the United States and says flash floods can come without warning. Its instruction under a flood warning is to find safe shelter right away, and then "depending on the type of flooding": evacuate if told to, move to higher ground or a higher floor, or stay where you are.{{cite:shelter-ready-floods}} It adds: never drive around barricades; local responders use them to direct traffic out of flooded areas. If your car is trapped in rapidly moving water, stay inside, and get on the roof only if water is rising inside the car. If you are trapped in a building, go to the highest level, signal for help, and do not climb into a closed attic.{{cite:shelter-ready-floods}}

The page also says homeowner's insurance policies do not cover flooding and that flood policies typically take up to 30 days to take effect, so the time to buy is well before a disaster.{{cite:shelter-ready-floods}}

Wildfires: leave early, and prepare for smoke if you stay

Ready.gov's wildfire page says to evacuate immediately if authorities tell you to, and to check local authorities or the FEMA app for open shelters. It suggests considering making plans with friends or family to shelter with them, where you may be safer and more comfortable. If you are trapped, it says to call 911 and give your location, but to be aware that emergency response could be delayed or impossible, and to turn on lights to help rescuers find you.{{cite:shelter-ready-wildfires}}

The "stay" version of wildfire is about smoke. Ready.gov's advice is to choose a room that can be closed off from outside air, run a portable air cleaner or filter in it, and use an N95 mask where available. If your central air system has a fresh-air intake, set it to recirculate and close the outdoor intake damper. If you are not ordered to evacuate but conditions are smoky, stay inside in a safe location or go to a community building where smoke levels are lower.{{cite:shelter-ready-wildfires}} Do not return until authorities say it is safe.{{cite:shelter-ready-wildfires}}

Chemical and hazardous-materials incidents: the case where the answer really changes

The page lists signs of a chemical release, including difficulty breathing, eye irritation, loss of coordination, nausea, or burning in the nose, throat and lungs, and notes that some toxic chemicals are odorless.{{cite:shelter-ready-hazmat}} What it says to do depends on the instruction you are given:

  • If told to evacuate: do so immediately. Work out which areas are affected or where the chemical is coming from and get away. If the chemical is inside your building, leave without passing through the contaminated area if possible. If you cannot, move as far from it as you can and shelter in place.
  • If told to stay indoors: bring pets inside; go to an internal room; close and lock exterior doors and windows; close vents, fireplace dampers and as many interior doors as possible; turn off air conditioners and ventilation, or set ventilation to 100 percent recirculation; seal the room with duct tape and plastic sheeting; seal gaps with wet towels or similar materials. If gas or vapors may have entered, take shallow breaths through a cloth or towel. Avoid eating or drinking anything that may be contaminated.
  • If outside: move away immediately, upwind of the source. In a car, keep windows and vents closed and shut off the heater and air conditioner. Find the closest building to shelter in.

All of these are from Ready.gov's hazmat page.{{cite:shelter-ready-hazmat}} The page also says not to leave the shelter, even to help others, until authorities say it is safe, and that someone affected by a chemical agent should, if medical help is not immediately available, decontaminate (remove clothing and items that touched the body, bag them, wash with soap and water, flush the eyes), then go to a medical facility.{{cite:shelter-ready-hazmat}}

Sealing a room is a temporary measure

Ready.gov's shelter page describes "sealing a room" as a temporary protective measure that creates a barrier between you and potentially contaminated air outside, and says it requires pre-planning: plastic sheeting and duct tape kept in your emergency supply kit, with sheeting cut several inches wider than each opening and labeled in advance.{{cite:shelter-ready-shelter}} Uncertain Neither the shelter page nor the hazmat page says how long a sealed room remains safe to stay in, and we have not found an official figure. Editor's note That means do not plan around a duration, and do not run any fuel-burning appliance, grill or generator inside a sealed or unsealed room. See {{link:outage-heat-cold-generators|the outage guide}} for why.

Ready.gov's shelter page also gives its general conditions for choosing to shelter in place: use common sense and available information to judge whether there is immediate danger; large amounts of debris in the air, or local authorities saying the air is badly contaminated, are the examples it gives. It notes that local authorities may not immediately have information about what is happening and what you should do, and says to pay attention to local media for official instructions. For household steps it says: bring family and pets inside; lock doors; close windows, air vents and fireplace dampers; turn off fans, air conditioning and forced-air heating; take your emergency kit unless it may be contaminated; and go to an interior room with few windows.{{cite:shelter-ready-shelter}}

How you will know: warnings you can rely on

Every decision above depends on getting the order. Ready.gov's hurricane and wildfire pages recommend several ways to receive alerts: the FEMA app, which gives real-time National Weather Service alerts for up to five locations; community alert systems you sign up for; and the Emergency Alert System (EAS) and Wireless Emergency Alerts (WEA), which require no sign-up.{{cite:shelter-ready-hurricanes}}{{cite:shelter-ready-wildfires}} The tornado page also says to learn your community's siren tone if you have sirens.{{cite:shelter-ready-tornadoes}}

The National Weather Service describes NOAA Weather Radio All Hazards as a nationwide network broadcasting official warnings, watches and forecasts 24 hours a day, working with the Emergency Alert System, and also carrying non-weather hazard information, including chemical releases. It needs a special receiver, and some individual transmitters are out of service at any given time, so check the county coverage listing.{{cite:shelter-nws-nwr}}

If you evacuate: the checklist from Ready.gov

Ready.gov's evacuation page is the most complete single list. Its main points:{{cite:shelter-ready-evacuation}}

  • Before: learn the likely disasters and the local evacuation and shelter plans for each. Identify several destinations in different directions, such as a friend's home in another town or a motel. Know alternate routes and other means of transport. Remember that "your evacuation route may be on foot depending on the type of disaster."
  • If you have a car: keep a full tank if an evacuation seems likely, and keep at least a half tank at all times, since gas stations may be closed or unable to pump during outages. Plan to take one car per family to reduce congestion. Keep an emergency kit in the car.
  • If you do not have a car: plan how you will leave with family, friends or your local emergency management office.
  • Go-bag: prepare one you can carry, and make sure everyone knows where it is and who is responsible for it.
  • As you leave: take your supply kit and pets; leave early enough to avoid being trapped by severe weather; if time allows, call or email your out-of-state contact and tell them where you are going; lock doors and windows; unplug small electrical equipment but leave freezers and refrigerators plugged in unless there is a risk of flooding; if your home is damaged and you are instructed to, shut off water, gas and electricity; leave a note about when you left and where you are going.
  • On the road: follow recommended routes and avoid shortcuts, which may be blocked. Watch for washed-out roads and bridges and downed power lines. Never drive into flooded areas. Check whether neighbors need a ride.
  • Coming back: check with local officials, both where you are and at home, before traveling. Returning before debris is cleared is dangerous. Let friends and family know when you leave and when you arrive.

Go-bag contents are their own topic, and we do not repeat them here; Ready.gov points to its kit page.{{cite:shelter-ready-evacuation}}

If you go to a mass care shelter

Ready.gov says mass care shelters often provide water, food, medicine and basic sanitary facilities, but you should still bring your emergency supply kit. Living with many people in a confined space can be difficult and unpleasant, so it suggests bringing cleaning items such as soap, hand sanitizer and disinfecting wipes. All shelters accept service animals, but many public shelters and hotels do not allow pets, so know a safe place for pets before an emergency. You can search for open shelters by texting SHELTER and your ZIP code to 43362 (standard rates apply).{{cite:shelter-ready-shelter}} The FEMA app also lists open shelters during an active disaster.{{cite:shelter-ready-evacuation}}

People who need to plan earlier

  • People with disabilities. Ready.gov's disability page recommends a support network and telling them where your supplies are, planning accessible transportation for evacuation by checking with local transit and emergency management, asking your local emergency management office about voluntary registries, knowing more than one dialysis or treatment facility if you rely on one, and planning how to evacuate with assistive devices or replace them. Some shelters allow only service or support animals.{{cite:shelter-ready-disabilities}}
  • Households without a car. Decide with family, friends or local emergency management what resources are available.{{cite:shelter-ready-evacuation}}
  • Older adults, children, people with medical needs. The Ready.gov planning page asks you to account for different ages in the household, medical needs including prescriptions and equipment, disabilities or access and functional needs, languages, cultural and religious considerations, pets or service animals and school-aged children.{{cite:shelter-ready-plan}}
  • Places away from home. Ready.gov says plan for the places you go regularly, including school, daycare and work, and ask what alerts, evacuation routes and shelter-in-place plans each has.{{cite:shelter-ready-plan-locations}} See {{link:family-communication-plan|the family communication plan}} for how a household stays in touch when members are in different places.
  • Households that cannot easily afford to leave. The sources we read do not address cost directly, other than listing free options such as friends, relatives and shelters. Editor's note A tank of gas, a few days of lost work and a motel room are real barriers. Plan the free options first, such as a relative in another town and a pre-identified shelter route, and find out in advance whether your county provides evacuation transportation. We have not verified any particular program.

A way to decide in advance

Editor's note This is our own checklist, built from the sources above, not an official procedure. For each hazard you might face, write down five answers while it is calm:

  1. Which hazards are realistic where I live and where I regularly go?
  2. For each one, what does the official guidance say: shelter, evacuate or "depends"?
  3. If shelter: where exactly in this home, and what will I need there for hours or days?
  4. If evacuate: where to, which route, which backup route, how, and with whom?
  5. Who will tell me, and how: which alerts do I have, and which works without power?

Then compare your answers with what the county or city emergency management office publishes. Ready.gov's planning page asks households to discuss almost exactly these questions: how will I receive alerts, what is my shelter plan, what is my evacuation route, what is my communication plan, and do I need to update my kit.{{cite:shelter-ready-plan}}

What this guide has and hasn't done

Every instruction here comes from a Ready.gov page or the National Weather Service page cited beside it, read in full on the date shown. Those pages are dated from 2023 (hazardous materials) to October 2026 (floods); the hazmat page is the oldest and the one most worth re-checking. Where a page says "depends," we say it depends. We have not verified your state's or county's evacuation zones, orders, shelter locations or transportation programs; those are the information that matters most, and they are local. We did not cover earthquakes, tsunamis, radiological or nuclear emergencies, or civil unrest, and we did not read the Red Cross pages Ready.gov links for several hazards. We have not tested any plan, and we have no figure for how long a sealed room stays safe.
