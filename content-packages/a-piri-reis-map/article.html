The Piri Reis map is a torn piece of parchment, not a complete world map, and it is one of the most over-claimed objects in popular history. An Ottoman sailor-cartographer drew it at Gallipoli in 1513. It was rediscovered in the Topkapı Palace in 1929. Since then it has been called a lost map of Columbus, evidence of an ice-free Antarctica, and proof of a vanished civilization.

The real object is more interesting than the viral versions, because the map explains itself. Its author wrote notes in the margins saying where he got his information. Most of the mystery is answered by reading them.

What the object is

The map is drawn in colour on parchment. What survives is part of a much larger world map. Its marginal notes are torn along the eastern edge, which shows that the sheet once continued beyond the Atlantic. The inscription that names the maker gives his name as Piri ibn Haji Mehmed, “known as the nephew of Kemal Reis,” and says he drew it in Gallipoli in the month of Muharram in the year 919 of the Islamic calendar. The 1935 Turkish Historical Society edition converts that to 9 March–7 April 1513.

The same edition quotes Piri Reis’s later sea-book, the Kitab-ı Bahriye, saying he presented the map to Sultan Selim I in Cairo, where Selim had just conquered Egypt in 1517. After that, the map disappears from the record. Nobody knows what Selim made of it.

How it was found in 1929

In 1929, the Topkapı Palace was being turned into a museum. According to the 1935 edition, the museum director Halil Edhem Eldem found the map and examined it with the German orientalist Paul Kahle, who was visiting Istanbul. Kahle announced the discovery at the Eighteenth Congress of Orientalists at Leiden in September 1931. Newspapers soon called it “the map of Christopher Columbus,” and the Turkish society that published the 1935 edition describes the views that went with that label as “erroneous and misleading.”

The map lists its own sources

One long inscription says how the chart was made. In the 1935 English translation, Piri Reis says he drew on about twenty charts and mappae mundi, eight charts he says the Arabs call Jaferiye, one Arabic map of India, four recently drawn Portuguese maps, and a map by Columbus. He says he reduced them all to a single scale.

Another inscription tells the story of Columbus in the Ottoman voice. It calls him “a Genoese infidel,” says he persuaded the Spanish king to give him ships, and adds that Kemal Reis once held a Spanish prisoner who claimed to have sailed to the new lands three times with Columbus. Piri Reis relays his account of the route.

That is the actual Columbus connection. It is a compilation note, written by a working chart-maker, about a map he says he copied from. The Columbus map itself has not survived, so the claim can only be tested against the fragment. Gregory McIntosh’s book-length study, according to its publisher’s description, concludes that the Columbus material comes from the Second Voyage (1493–1496) and not, as some earlier scholars thought, the Third.

The Antarctica claim

The popular claim is that the southern coast on the map is Antarctica, drawn without ice. It rests on Charles Hapgood’s book Maps of the Ancient Sea Kings. David Jolly, reviewing Hapgood’s method in Skeptical Inquirer, describes how Hapgood concluded that a section of the South American coast on the map was really a misplaced piece of Antarctica. Jolly argues that Hapgood worked by stacking assumptions: when an old map matched a modern feature he credited ancient knowledge, and when it did not he blamed the mapmaker’s error. That makes the idea impossible to test, because every contrary result is explained away.

There is also a timing problem. The claim needs an ice-free coastline within human history. A 2023 study in Nature Communications states that the East Antarctic Ice Sheet has its origins about 34 million years ago. No human mapmaker appears anywhere in that timeline.

The map’s own text points to something ordinary. Geoscientist Steven Dutch, writing against the claim, argues that the marginal notes show a sixteenth-century origin and that, beyond southern Brazil, the coast matches neither South America nor Antarctica well. Jolly adds that sixteenth-century maps varied widely in how they drew a southern continent, which fits a long-standing belief in one (Terra Australis) rather than a surveyed coast. McIntosh’s study, again by the publisher’s account, rejects the idea that the map shows Antarctica as evidence of ancient civilizations or extraterrestrial visitors.

What is still uncertain

  • How much comes from Columbus. Piri Reis says the Caribbean coasts come from a Columbus map. Scholars have argued over which voyage that map reflects, and the map itself is lost, so the question can only be argued from the fragment.
  • The other source maps. Piri Reis names his sources by type, not by title, so identifying the Arabic map, the four Portuguese maps and the “twenty charts” is a matter of scholarly inference.
  • The southern coastline. The map’s text does not say what its southern edge is meant to show. Every reading, from a bent South America to a hypothetical southern continent, is an inference.
  • The missing two-thirds. The eastern part of the world map is lost, along with whatever it said.

Why it still matters

An Ottoman naval man, writing in Turkish and drawing on Portuguese charts, a Spanish prisoner’s story and a map he credits to Columbus, put the Atlantic world on one sheet and listed his sources on it. If his note is right, information from a Columbus map survives only because he copied it. The map does not need an ice-free Antarctica to be remarkable.

Common questions

Does the Piri Reis map show Antarctica?

No source I could open supports that. The claim comes from Charles Hapgood’s book Maps of the Ancient Sea Kings, which critics say relied on assumptions that cannot be tested. The map’s own notes describe Portuguese maps and Atlantic coasts, and the East Antarctic Ice Sheet dates to about 34 million years ago.

Did Piri Reis use a map by Columbus?

He says so. An inscription on the map lists “a map drawn by Colombo” among his sources, and another recounts what a Spanish prisoner told Kemal Reis about voyages with Columbus. The Columbus map itself is lost.

When was the Piri Reis map found?

In 1929, in the Topkapı Palace in Istanbul, while the palace was being converted into a museum. Paul Kahle announced it in 1931.

Who was Piri Reis?

An Ottoman sailor and cartographer, described on his own map as the nephew of the naval commander Kemal Reis. He also wrote the Kitab-ı Bahriye (Book of the Sea).
